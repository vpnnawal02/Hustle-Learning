Climate-smart agriculture (CSA) guides actions to transform agri-food systems towards green and climate-resilient practices, supporting the Sustainable Development Goals, the Paris Agreement and the FAO Strategic Framework 2022–2031 built on the Four Betters: better production, better nutrition, a better environment and a better life for all.

Q: A CSA expert suggested increased production and consumption of millets in India. Justify their stance.

A: Millets have high nutritional value. They are rainfed, hardly need irrigation facilities and hence can be grown in arid and semi-arid regions. Millets do not require a lot of investment to flourish, which can make them great commercial grain substitutes in poorer nations.

Q: What is the necessity of thinking about climate-smart agriculture in India?

A: It is important to start planning for climate-smart agriculture in India because of the changing climate due to global warming.

Q: Suggest two methods through which India can shift towards climate-smart agriculture.

A: India can shift towards climate-smart agriculture through the use of genetically modified seeds resistant to insect damage for cropping, and through a shift towards organic and natural farming methods.

Q: Why is agriculture the mainstay of India? OR Write the features of Indian agriculture.

A: Two-thirds of the population is engaged in agricultural activities, and agriculture is an age-old economic activity and a primary activity producing most of the food we consume. Besides food grains, it produces raw materials for various industries, and products like tea, coffee and spices are also exported.

Q: Explain slash-and-burn agriculture. How is it known in different parts of India?

A: Farmers clear a patch of land and produce cereals and other food crops to sustain their families. When soil fertility decreases, the farmers shift and clear a fresh patch of land for cultivation, and this shifting allows nature to replenish soil fertility through natural processes. Land productivity in this type of agriculture is low, as the farmer does not use fertilisers or other modern inputs. It is known as Jhumming in the north-eastern states like Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Nagaland, Pamlou in Manipur, and Dipa in the Bastar district of Chhattisgarh and in the Andaman and Nicobar islands.

Q: By which other names is slash-and-burn farming or shifting agriculture known in different countries?

A: Slash-and-burn farming is known as Milpa in Mexico and Central America, Conuco in Venezuela, Roca in Brazil, Ray in Vietnam and Masole in Central Africa.

Q: State the characteristics of primitive subsistence farming.

A: Farmers grow crops for self-consumption on small patches of land using primitive tools like hoe, dao and digging sticks, depending completely upon monsoon, natural soil fertility and the suitability of environmental conditions. Only family labour is used, land productivity is low, and it is also known as slash-and-burn agriculture.

Q: Establish the difference between commercial farming and subsistence farming with a suitable example.

A: Subsistence farming is practised on small patches of land with primitive techniques and tools for family consumption, dependent on monsoons with family labour and no costly fertilisers. Commercial farming is practised on big land holdings with modern techniques and tools for sale in the market, dependent on irrigation facilities with hired labourers and chemical fertilisers.

Q: What is plantation farming? Describe its characteristics.

A: Plantation farming is a type of commercial farming in which a single crop is grown on a large area using capital-intensive inputs with the help of migrant labourers, as in tea, coffee, rubber and sugarcane. Its characteristics are very large plantation areas, capital-intensive inputs, migrant labour, production mainly for the market with the sole aim of earning profit, an interface between agriculture and industry, and produce used as raw material in respective industries.

Q: Which are the main cropping seasons in India? Mention their growing and harvesting periods.

A: India has three cropping seasons: Rabi, Kharif and Zaid. Rabi crops are sown in winter from October to December and harvested in summer from April to June, with wheat, barley, peas and gram as the main crops. Kharif crops are sown with the onset of monsoon in June–July and harvested in September–October, with rice, maize, jowar, bajra, tur, moong, cotton, jute and groundnut as the main crops. Between Rabi and Kharif lies the short Zaid season of the summer months, with watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables and fodder crops.

Q: What are the growing conditions required for the main staple food crop of India? Mention the main growing regions.

A: Rice is the staple food crop of the majority of people in India. It needs high temperature above 25°C, high humidity with annual rainfall above 100 cm, and is grown as a Kharif crop. The major producing areas are West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh.

Q: What are the geographical conditions required for the cultivation of wheat? Explain its distribution also.

A: Wheat is the second staple food crop of the majority of people in India. It requires a cool growing season with bright sunshine at the time of ripening, 50 to 75 cm of annual rainfall evenly distributed over the growing season, and is grown as a Rabi crop. The major producing areas are Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and Rajasthan.

Q: Name any two important pulse-producing states. State the importance of pulses. Why are pulses mostly grown in rotation with other crops?

A: **States:** Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and Karnataka. **Importance:** pulses are a major source of protein in a vegetarian diet, and being leguminous crops they help restore soil fertility, except arhar, by fixing nitrogen from the air; they need less moisture and survive even in dry conditions. **Rotation:** pulses are grown in rotation with other crops precisely because, as leguminous crops, they restore soil fertility by fixing nitrogen from the air.

Q: Mohan owns a farm in Uttar Pradesh and wishes to cultivate either jute or sugarcane. Which crop should he cultivate keeping in mind the conditions required for their growth? Explain.

A: He should cultivate sugarcane, as the geographical conditions it requires are available in Uttar Pradesh. Sugarcane grows well in a hot and humid climate, requires a temperature of 21°C to 27°C, needs annual rainfall between 75 cm and 100 cm with irrigation in low-rainfall regions, and can be grown on a variety of soils with manual labour from sowing to harvesting — all these conditions are available in Uttar Pradesh.

Q: Name any four oilseeds produced in India. Explain the importance of oilseeds in day-to-day life. State the importance of groundnut. Which state is the largest producer of groundnut?

A: Major oilseeds produced in India are groundnut, mustard, coconut, sesamum (til), soyabean and castor seeds. Most oilseeds are edible and used as cooking mediums, and they are used as raw materials in the production of soap, cosmetics and ointments. Groundnut shares this importance as an edible oil and industrial raw material, and Gujarat is the largest producer of groundnut.

Q: Name the beverage crop introduced by the British in India. Write the geographical conditions needed for its cultivation. Mention its two major producing states.

A: Tea was introduced by the British in India. It grows well in tropical and sub-tropical climates endowed with deep, fertile, well-drained soil rich in humus and organic matter; tea bushes require a warm, moist, frost-free climate all through the year; and frequent showers evenly distributed over the year ensure continuous growth of tender leaves. Two major tea-producing states are Assam and West Bengal, in the hills of the Darjeeling and Jalpaiguri districts.

Q: Mr. Palani from Tamil Nadu wishes to cultivate either tea or wheat. Which crop can he cultivate in his state? Substantiate with any two reasons.

A: Mr. Palani must cultivate tea in Tamil Nadu, as the soil and climatic conditions there are suitable for tea. The tea plant grows well in tropical and sub-tropical climates with deep, fertile, well-drained laterite soil rich in humus and organic matter, requiring a warm, moist, frost-free climate all through the year. Frequent showers evenly distributed over the year ensure continuous growth of tender leaves.

Q: Explain rubber cultivation in India under the heads of importance, geographical conditions and any two rubber-producing states.

A: **Importance:** rubber is an important raw material for industries. **Geographical conditions:** it is an equatorial crop requiring a moist and humid climate with rainfall of more than 200 cm and temperature above 25°C. **Producing states:** Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka are rubber-producing states.

Q: Name the four major fibre crops grown in India. Which fibre is not obtained directly from crops, and what is its production method? Discuss the geographical conditions for golden fibre and cotton. Mention any four uses of golden fibre.

A: The four major fibre crops are cotton, jute, hemp and natural silk. Silk is not obtained directly from crops; it is obtained through sericulture, the rearing of silkworms for silk fibre production. **Jute (golden fibre):** grows well on well-drained fertile soils in flood plains where soils are renewed every year, with high temperature during growth. **Cotton:** grows well in the drier parts of the black cotton soil of the Deccan plateau, requiring high temperature, light rainfall or irrigation, 210 frost-free days and bright sunshine; it is a Kharif crop taking 6 to 8 months to mature. **Uses of jute:** gunny bags, mats, ropes, yarn, carpets and similar products.

Q: Why do we need technical and institutional reforms in agriculture?

A: Sustained use of land without compatible techno-institutional changes has hindered the pace of agricultural development. In spite of developed irrigation sources, most farmers still depend upon monsoons and natural fertility. Agriculture, which provides a livelihood for more than 60 per cent of the population, needs serious technical and institutional reforms.

Q: Explain the technical and institutional reforms brought by the government to improve Indian agriculture in the 1980s and 1990s. OR Describe any five steps taken by the government to increase agricultural productivity in India.

A: **Institutional reforms:** collectivisation, consolidation of holdings, cooperation and abolition of zamindari were prioritised after Independence; land reform laws were enacted; crop insurance was provided against drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease; Grameen banks, cooperative societies and banks gave loans to farmers at lower interest rates; and the Kisan Credit Card (KCC) and Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS) were introduced. **Technological reforms:** the Green Revolution and White Revolution (Operation Flood); HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers and pesticides; modernised irrigation; latest agricultural equipment; weather bulletins and agricultural programmes on radio and television; and minimum support price (MSP) with remunerative and procurement prices for important crops.

Q: Describe the major challenges faced by farmers in India.

A: Indian farmers face international competition and reduced public investment in agriculture, decreased fertiliser subsidies raising production costs, reduced import duties proving detrimental to domestic agriculture, withdrawal of investment from agriculture causing falling farm employment, and the uncertainty of the monsoon.

Q: Why are Bhoodan and Gramdan called bloodless revolutions?

A: They are called a bloodless revolution because some zamindars, owners of many villages, offered to distribute some villages among the landless, and many landowners chose to give part of their land to poor farmers due to the fear of the Land Ceiling Act.

Q: The process of increasing the area under cultivation does not have a bright future. What steps should be taken to increase the food supply?

A: Increase productivity with modern inputs like HYV seeds, irrigation, fertilisers and pesticides; initiate agricultural research to evolve better production techniques; increase the use of organic manure; use better irrigation methods like sprinklers and drip irrigation to cover larger areas with less water; and implement land reforms like ceilings on holdings and consolidation of holdings.
