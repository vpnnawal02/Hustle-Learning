## Money as a Medium of Exchange

- Money is used in most daily transactions: buying goods, paying for services, and making promises to pay later (credit).
- Reason for using money: it can be exchanged for any good or service, so everyone prefers to receive payments in money.
- Money separates trade into two easy steps: sell what you have for money, then use money to buy what you want.
- Without money (barter), exchange needs **double coincidence of wants** (both sides must want exactly what the other offers), which is hard.
- Example: A shoe maker sells shoes for money, then buys wheat with that money; far easier than finding a wheat farmer who also wants shoes.
- Barter problems simplified: no common value measure, hard to match wants, difficult to store value; money solves these by being widely acceptable.
- Simple terms: **barter** (direct goods-for-goods exchange), **double coincidence** (both parties' wants match at the same time).
- Money removes the need for **double coincidence of wants;** you don't need the buyer of your product to also sell what you want.
- Trade becomes two steps: sell goods for **money,** then use that money to buy whatever is needed.
- Example: the shoe maker only needs a buyer for shoes; after getting money, wheat can be bought from any seller.
- Because money is the **medium of exchange** (an accepted intermediate), it streamlines transactions and expands market possibilities.
- Simple terms: **medium of exchange** = widely accepted payment; **double coincidence of wants** = both sides want exactly what the other offers at the same time.

## Modern Forms of Money

### Currency

- Modern money = **currency** (paper notes and coins); unlike earlier money (gold/silver/copper, grain/cattle), it has **no intrinsic use.**
- It is accepted because it is **authorised by the government;** trust comes from legal backing, not from metal content.
- In India, the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** issues currency notes on behalf of the central government.
- By law, **no other individual or organisation** can issue currency in India.
- The law makes the **rupee** legal tender, so payment in rupees **cannot be refused** for settling transactions.
- Therefore, the **rupee is widely accepted** as a medium of exchange despite having no value of its own outside the legal guarantee.

### Deposits with Banks

- People also hold money as **bank deposits;** only some cash is needed daily, the rest is kept safely in accounts and may earn **interest.**
- Banks accept deposits and allow withdrawals **on demand;** such balances are called **demand deposits.**
- Demand deposits function like money because they can settle payments directly.
- **Cheques** enable non-cash payments: the account holder instructs the bank to pay a specified amount to a named person.
- Simple meanings: **demand deposit** (money you can withdraw anytime), **cheque** (paper instruction to bank to transfer money from your account), **interest** (extra money paid by bank on deposits).
- **Demand deposits** share the key features of money because they are widely accepted for payments and can be used to settle transactions directly via **cheques.**
- The cheque facility lets people transfer funds from their bank accounts without using cash, enabling cashless payments.
- Together with **currency,** demand deposits form money in the modern economy since both are accepted as a **medium of exchange.**
- Banks are essential: without banks, there would be no demand deposits and no cheque-based payments against them.
- Modern money = **currency + bank deposits;** both work because of and through the **modern banking system.**

## Loan Activities of Banks

- Banks keep only a small part of deposits as **cash** to meet daily withdrawals; the rest is used to give **loans.**
- Example figure: around **5%** of deposits may be held as cash to handle withdrawals; most days only some depositors withdraw, so this is sufficient.
- The bulk of deposits are lent out to meet high **loan demand** for business, housing, education, and other activities.
- Banks **mediate** between savers with surplus funds (**depositors**) and borrowers who need funds (**loans**).
- Banks pay a lower **interest** on deposits and charge a higher **interest** on loans; the **spread** (difference) is their main income.
- Simple terms: **depositors** (people who keep money in bank), **borrowers** (people who take loans), **spread** (loan rate minus deposit rate), **liquidity** (cash available to meet withdrawals).

## Two Different Credit Situations

- Credit is an agreement where a lender provides money, goods, or services now in return for a promise of payment in the future, usually with interest.
- It appears everywhere in daily life: shopkeeper tabs, credit card purchases, BNPL, bank loans, supplier trade credit, and utility bill cycles.
- Core elements: a lender, a borrower, a principal amount, repayment schedule, and a cost (interest/fees); often supported by collateral or a credit score.
- Why it matters: lets households and firms smooth spending, invest, and grow before their income arrives; misused, it can cause debt stress.
- Two common pathways it works through:
  - When credit is productive (e.g., a crop loan or business inventory loan), income generated later repays the loan and can improve livelihoods.
  - When terms are harsh (very high interest or penalties) or income falls short, borrowers may struggle to repay, leading to a debt trap.
- Credit used for working capital lets a producer pay for inputs and running costs on time, finish production without delays, and sell to realize revenue.
- In Salim's case, the short-term loan covers ongoing expenses (raw materials, wages, utilities), enabling timely completion and higher sales; after selling output, he repays the loan with interest and retains a larger profit.
- Why this is positive: it smooths cash flow, prevents production stoppages, helps capture seasonal demand, and can improve supplier relationships through timely payments.
- Key caution: benefits depend on reasonable interest, aligned repayment schedules, and productive use of funds; when these align, working-capital credit boosts earnings and business growth.
- Rural credit is crucial for crop production because farmers must pay for inputs months before harvest; repayment hinges on farm income, so outcomes vary with risk.
- When crops succeed, credit is **productive:** it finances seeds, fertilisers, water, electricity, and repairs, and is repaid from sales, improving livelihoods.
- When crops fail (like Swapna's case), credit can trigger a **debt trap:** inability to repay forces asset sales (land), leaving the borrower worse off than before.
- Key risks: weather shocks, pests, price crashes, input cost spikes, and health emergencies that erode ability to repay.
- Whether credit helps depends on risk management and support systems: crop insurance, fair pricing, timely institutional loans at reasonable interest, and grace periods can prevent distress.
- Safer design: align repayment with harvest cycles, cap effective interest, bundle loans with insurance, provide emergency restructuring after shocks, and expand access to formal credit to avoid high-cost moneylenders.

## Terms of Credit

- Every loan specifies two key parts: the principal (amount borrowed) and an interest rate (the cost of borrowing) that must be paid back with the principal.
- Lenders often require collateral (security): an asset the borrower owns that is pledged until the loan is fully repaid.
- If the borrower defaults, the lender has the legal right to sell the collateral to recover dues.
- Common collateral examples: land and building titles, vehicles, livestock, fixed deposits or other bank deposits, and sometimes inventory or equipment for business loans.
- Simple meanings: principal (original amount borrowed), interest (extra cost paid to the lender), collateral (pledged asset), default (failure to repay as agreed).
- The "terms of credit" are the agreed conditions of a loan, typically covering the interest rate, collateral, documentation, and mode/tenure of repayment.
- Core components:
  - Interest rate: the cost of borrowing, fixed or floating, plus any processing fees or penalties.
  - Collateral: assets pledged as security (e.g., land, buildings, vehicles, livestock, bank deposits); can be seized if the borrower defaults.
  - Documentation: proofs and contracts that record eligibility, identity, income, asset titles, and the loan agreement.
  - Mode and schedule of repayment: when and how the loan is repaid (EMIs, bullet/seasonal payments, monthly/quarterly/harvest-linked), including grace periods.
- Why terms vary:
  - Lender type: banks and cooperatives generally offer lower rates with stricter documentation; informal lenders are faster but charge higher rates and may set harsh conditions.
  - Borrower risk: credit history, income stability, collateral quality, and purpose (crop loan vs. business vs. housing) change pricing, security, and repayment structure.
- Practical takeaway:
  - Always compare the APR (including fees), check collateral clauses (what triggers enforcement), verify documentation requirements, and choose a repayment schedule aligned with cash flows (e.g., harvest-linked for farm credit).

## Formal Sector Credit in India

- Rural households borrow from both **formal** (banks, cooperatives, NBFCs, MFIs) and **informal** sources (moneylenders, traders, employers, friends/relatives).
- In the commonly cited Class 10 "Graph 1" for 2012, the largest single source was **moneylenders (~33%),** while **cooperatives/banks and commercial banks** together contributed about half, indicating a substantial informal share then.
- Recent surveys show improvement: by early **2025,** roughly **51–53%** of rural borrowing households relied only on **formal** sources, with around **18%** using only **informal** sources and the rest mixing both.
- Direct answer: Historically, more credit came from the **informal sector** in rural India, but the balance has shifted in recent years toward the **formal sector** as access expanded; the exact answer depends on the year referenced by the graph.
- The RBI supervises formal lenders to ensure prudential norms, including maintaining required cash reserves and overall liquidity, through on-site inspections and off-site surveillance under powers in the Banking Regulation Act and its supervisory departments and Board for Financial Supervision.
- Banks periodically report to the RBI on lending, exposures, and compliance; RBI uses inspections, returns, and supervisory letters to monitor solvency, asset quality, earnings, liquidity, and systems/controls (CAMELS), and to direct corrective action.
- RBI's remit includes promoting credit flow to priority sectors such as small cultivators, small industries, and other small borrowers via regulatory guidance and supervision of banks and allied financial institutions.
- By contrast, there is no unified public authority supervising informal lenders, so interest rates and recovery practices in the informal sector are not systematically regulated or monitored in the way bank lending is.
- Informal lenders typically charge much higher interest than formal lenders, so borrowers spend a bigger share of their income on repayments and have less left over for consumption or investment.
- Very high rates can make total repayments exceed a borrower's income, causing rising arrears and a debt trap; fear of such costs also discourages new enterprises from borrowing to start or expand.
- Expanding low-cost credit from banks, cooperatives, MFIs, and other regulated channels enables people to borrow affordably for crops, small businesses, and services, raising productivity and incomes.
- Key policy levers: widen access to formal credit in rural/urban informal areas, simplify documentation, align repayments with cash flows, cap or supervise abusive pricing, and bundle credit with insurance to reduce default risk aftershocks.
- Bottom line: cheap and accessible formal credit reduces dependency on costly informal loans, prevents debt traps, and is crucial for inclusive growth and development.

## Formal and Informal Credit: Who Gets What?

- Urban credit comes from both formal sources (banks, cooperatives, MFIs, NBFCs) and informal sources (moneylenders, traders, employers, friends/relatives). Poor urban households rely heavily on informal credit because of documentation gaps, thin credit histories, rigid products, and limited branch reach. Over half of urban poor borrowers continue to use informal credit, reflecting these access barriers.
- The pattern by income group is stark: about 54% of loans for poor urban households come from informal sources, while rich urban households obtain only about 17% of their loans informally and 83% from formal channels, highlighting unequal access and pricing.
- A similar gradient exists in rural areas: wealthier households obtain more formal, cheaper credit; poorer households pay more via informal lenders, which depresses their net incomes and investment capacity.
- Implications: the formal sector still does not meet all credit needs, especially for the poor, so expanding bank and cooperative lending targeted to low-income urban and rural borrowers is essential. Better distribution of formal credit — simpler documentation, alternative-data underwriting, small-ticket and flexible repayment products — can reduce dependence on high-cost informal loans.

## Self-Help Groups for the Poor

- Poor households remain dependent on informal credit because bank branches are sparse in many rural areas and formal loans require documents and collateral that the poor often lack, making access difficult even where banks exist.
- Informal lenders know borrowers personally, lend quickly without collateral, and offer flexible repayment, so borrowers can return even without clearing older dues; but these loans come with very high interest, no formal records, and frequent harassment in recovery.
- Eligibility barriers at banks include strict KYC, income proof, collateral, and credit history; many low-income workers have irregular earnings and no property titles, which blocks formal credit access.
- Net effect: convenience and flexibility draw the poor to informal lenders, but high costs trap them in debt; expanding accessible, collateral-light formal credit is essential to reduce exploitation.
- Self Help Groups (SHGs) organise poor, especially women, into small neighbourhood groups (about 15–20 members) that meet regularly, pool savings, and lend to one another at interest rates lower than moneylenders.
- Typical savings per member range from about Rs 25 to Rs 100 or more, based on ability; these pooled funds finance quick, collateral-free microloans for needs like working capital, small repairs, or emergencies.
- With a steady savings and lending record (usually 1–2 years), SHGs become eligible for bank linkage; the bank then sanctions a loan in the name of the group, often without traditional collateral, expanding affordable credit access.
- Loans commonly support self-employment and asset building: releasing mortgaged land, buying seeds and fertilisers, raw materials (bamboo, cloth), housing materials, or assets like sewing machines, handlooms, and cattle.
- Why this works: peer monitoring improves repayment, lowers default risk, and builds credit histories; group-based lending reduces paperwork, speeds disbursal, and replaces exploitative informal credit with fairer, predictable terms.
- Broader benefits: increased women's financial autonomy, smoother cash flows for livelihoods, resilience against shocks, and a bridge to formal finance that can scale microenterprises into sustainable income sources.
- SHGs make loan decisions collectively: members decide purpose, amount, interest, and repayment schedule in regular meetings, and they enforce timely repayment through peer monitoring. This shared responsibility lowers default risk and builds trust with banks, even when members lack traditional collateral.
- Because the group guarantees repayment, banks are more willing to lend to poor women organised in SHGs, enabling timely, reasonably priced loans for diverse needs such as working capital, releasing mortgaged land, housing materials, or productive assets like sewing machines, handlooms, and cattle.
- SHGs overcome collateral barriers through social collateral: if one member struggles, others follow up seriously and support repayment, which sustains strong credit discipline and access to larger bank linkages over time.
- Beyond finance, SHGs are platforms for empowerment and community action: regular meetings help women become financially self-reliant and discuss issues like health, nutrition, and domestic violence, often catalysing collective action and social change.
- Key takeaways: democratic decision-making, peer accountability, and social collateral reduce risk and borrowing costs; bank linkages scale affordable credit; and the SHG forum strengthens both livelihoods and voice on critical social concerns.

## Conclusion

- Modern money consists of currency and bank deposits, both tied to the banking system: depositors place savings in banks, and banks lend to borrowers for economic activities.
- Credit can be beneficial (financing production and growth) or harmful (creating debt traps when shocks hit and income falls short).
- Credit sources are formal (banks, cooperatives, regulated lenders) and informal (moneylenders, traders, employers, friends/relatives), with very different terms.
- Terms of credit — interest rate, collateral, documentation, and repayment mode — vary widely; formal loans tend to be cheaper but require documents and collateral, while informal loans are easier to access but costly.
- Currently, richer households access more formal credit, while poorer households rely more on informal lenders, paying higher rates and risking debt cycles.
- Development priorities: expand total formal credit so dependence on expensive informal loans declines, and ensure equitable access so poorer households receive a larger share of affordable loans.
- Practical steps that help: simplify documentation and KYC, use alternative data for underwriting, align repayments with cash flows (e.g., harvest-linked), strengthen SHG and cooperative linkages, and bundle loans with insurance to cushion shocks.
