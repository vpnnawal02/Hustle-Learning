Detailed notes on Geography Chapter 1: what resources are and how they are classified — sustainable development, resource planning in India, land utilisation, soil types and conservation.

## Introduction

- Resource: Anything from the environment that meets needs and is **technologically accessible, economically feasible,** and **culturally acceptable.**
- A thing becomes a resource only when we can use it with available technology, at reasonable cost, and society accepts it.
- Nature provides materials; technology turns them usable; institutions (laws, policies, organizations) manage their use.
- Humans use technology to convert natural things into useful resources.
- Institutions (government, markets, communities) set rules for fair and efficient resource use.
- Improved technology and strong institutions increase efficient use and support **economic development.**
- Resources are not free gifts; they are created by human use and decision.
- Human beings are essential parts of resources; their skills and actions turn natural materials into useful resources.
- Resources exist because people identify, develop, and use them with technology and organization.
- Classification by origin: **Biotic** (living, e.g., forests, animals) and **Abiotic** (non-living, e.g., minerals, water).
- Classification by exhaustibility: **Renewable** (replenish naturally, e.g., solar, wind) and **Non-renewable** (form very slowly, e.g., coal, petroleum).
- Classification by ownership: **Individual** (private), **Community** (used by a group), **National** (owned by a nation), **International** (beyond national control, e.g., high seas).
- Classification by status of development: **Potential** (known but not used), **Developed** (surveyed and in use), **Stock** (available but cannot be used due to lack of technology), **Reserves** (part of stock currently usable under present conditions).

## Development of Resources

- Resources are essential for survival and a good quality of life.
- Seeing resources as "free gifts" led to careless overuse and waste.
- Depletion: Excess use by a few dries up **forests, minerals, water,** and **fossil fuels.**
- Inequality: Resources pile up with the rich, creating **haves** and **have-nots.**
- Ecological damage: Overuse causes **global warming, ozone layer depletion, pollution,** and **land degradation.**
- Reason: When use is profit-driven and unplanned, nature can't replenish, and harms spread to society.
- Need: **Sustainable use** (use at a rate nature can renew), fair sharing, and strict control on over-extraction.
- Equitable sharing of resources is essential to keep a good quality of life for all and maintain **global peace** by reducing inequality and conflict.
- If a few people/countries keep overusing resources, shortages and tensions will rise, putting the planet's future at risk.
- Resource planning: matching needs with availability, improving efficiency, reducing waste, and protecting ecosystems for long-term use.
- Goal: **Sustainable existence** — using resources so that nature can renew them and future generations also benefit.
- Note: **Sustainable existence** is a part of **sustainable development** (meeting today's needs without harming future needs).
- Sustainable economic development means 'development should take place without damaging the environment, and development in the present should not compromise with the needs of the future generations.'

## Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit, 1992

- Date and place: **June 1992,** Rio de Janeiro, Brazil — first International Earth Summit.
- Purpose: Tackle urgent global issues of environmental protection and socio-economic development.
- Key declarations: Signed statements on **Global Climatic Change** and **Biological Diversity.**
- Forest Principles: Global guidelines approved for sustainable management and conservation of forests.
- Agenda 21: Comprehensive action plan adopted to guide **Sustainable Development** in the 21st century.
- Significance: Set a common global roadmap linking environment with development and cooperation among nations.

## Agenda 21

- Name: **Agenda 21** — a declaration adopted at **UNCED 1992** in **Rio de Janeiro, Brazil.**
- Aim: Achieve **global sustainable development** by linking environment protection with socio-economic progress.
- Focus: Tackle environmental damage, **poverty,** and **disease** through international cooperation based on common interests, shared needs, and shared responsibilities.
- Levels of action: Global, national, and local implementation with coordinated policies and programs.
- Local mandate: Every local government should prepare its own **Local Agenda 21** with community participation and time-bound actions.
- Core idea: Think globally and act locally to ensure long-term human well-being and environmental health.

## Resource Planning

- Planning ensures wise and fair use of resources across diverse regions in **India.**
- Resource availability varies widely between states and regions.
- Some areas are rich in some resources but poor in others, so planning avoids waste and meets local needs.
- Jharkhand, **Chhattisgarh,** and **Madhya Pradesh** are rich in minerals and **coal.**
- **Arunachal Pradesh** has abundant water resources but weak infrastructure development.
- **Rajasthan** has strong **solar** and **wind** energy potential but lacks water resources.
- **Ladakh** is culturally rich but has shortages of water, infrastructure, and some key minerals.
- This diversity requires balanced resource planning at national, state, regional, and local levels.

## Resource Planning in India

Resource planning steps:

- Identification and inventory of resources in all regions through surveying, mapping, and qualitative and quantitative measurement.
- Building a planning framework with suitable technology, skilled people, and strong institutions to execute development plans.
- Aligning resource development plans with overall national development goals for coherence and impact.
- India's efforts: From the **First Five Year Plan** after **Independence,** continuous attempts have been made to implement resource planning.
- Key point: Having resources is necessary but not sufficient for development.
- Without proper technology and supportive institutions, resources may remain unused and regions stay backward.
- Some resource-rich regions are economically backward due to poor technology or weak institutions.
- Some resource-poor regions are economically developed because of advanced technology, better infrastructure, and efficient institutions.
- Colonisers were drawn to colonies mainly for their **rich resources** and used them to build power.
- Superior **technology** in colonising nations enabled large-scale extraction and control over colonies.
- Resources support development only when matched with proper technology and supportive institutions.
- India's colonial experience shows how resource-rich areas can be exploited without local development.
- Development in India needs more than resources; it requires technology, skilled **human resources,** and learning from historical experiences.
- Key idea: Availability of resources alone is not enough; technology and institutions decide who benefits and how much.

## Conservation of Resources

- Resources are essential for development but careless and excessive use creates social, economic, and environmental problems.
- Solution is **resource conservation** at national, regional, local, and individual levels.
- Past leaders stressed conservation as a duty for society.
- **Mahatma Gandhi** warned, "There is enough for everybody's need and not for anybody's greed," highlighting fairness and restraint.
- Root causes of depletion are greed, selfish behavior, and **exploitative technology** focused only on profit.
- Gandhi opposed mass production and favored "production by the masses," promoting local, inclusive, and sustainable livelihoods.

## Land Resources

- Land is a **natural resource** of highest importance supporting vegetation, wildlife, human life, economic activities, transport and communication.
- Land is finite, so careful and planned use is necessary to meet multiple needs without degradation.
- India has diverse relief: **mountains, plateaus, plains,** and **islands.**
- Plains cover about **43%** of area and are suitable for agriculture and industry due to flat terrain and better soils.
- Mountains cover about **30%** and provide perennial rivers, tourism opportunities, and vital ecological functions.
- Plateaus cover about **27%** and contain rich reserves of **minerals, fossil fuels,** and **forests.**
- Balanced land-use planning is needed to allocate land among farming, industry, settlements, forests, and infrastructure sustainably.

## Land Utilisation

Land use categories:

- **Forests.**
- **Land not available for cultivation:** barren and waste land; land under non-agricultural uses such as buildings, roads, and factories.
- **Other uncultivated land (excluding fallow):** permanent pastures and grazing land; land under miscellaneous tree crops and groves not counted in net sown area; culturable waste land left uncultivated for more than **5 agricultural years.**
- **Fallow lands:** current fallow left without cultivation for **one or less than one agricultural year;** other than current fallow left uncultivated for **1 to 5 agricultural years.**
- **Net sown area:** total land where crops are actually sown and harvested.
- **Gross cropped area:** net sown area plus the area sown more than once in the same agricultural year.

## Land Use Pattern in India

- Land use depends on physical factors like **topography, climate,** and **soil,** and human factors like **population density, technology,** and **culture.**
- India's total geographical area is **3.28 million sq km.**
- Land use data is available for about **93%** of this area.
- Gaps exist because many **North-East states** (except **Assam**) lack full reporting.
- Parts of **Jammu and Kashmir** under **Pakistan** and **China** control have not been fully surveyed.
- Permanent pasture area has declined, yet large cattle are fed using crop residues, stall-feeding with fodder crops, and grazing on common lands; this strains commons and reduces fodder quality.
- Other-than-current fallow is often poor quality or costly to farm, so it is cultivated only once or twice in 2–3 years.
- Including these lands, **Net Sown Area (NSA)** is about **54%** of the reporting area.
- NSA is over **80%** in **Punjab** and **Haryana,** but under **10%** in **Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur,** and **Andaman & Nicobar Islands.**
- Forest cover is below the desired **33%** target set in **National Forest Policy (1952),** which is needed for ecological balance and local livelihoods.
- Waste land includes **rocky, arid,** and **desert** areas; other non-agricultural uses include **settlements, roads, railways,** and **industry.**
- Continuous, unmanaged use causes **land degradation,** leading to soil fertility loss, reduced productivity, and environmental harm.

## Land Degradation and Conservation

- We must share land fairly across generations; about **95%** of basic needs for food, shelter, and clothing come from land.
- Human actions speed up land damage beyond natural wear.
- Major human causes of land degradation: **deforestation, overgrazing, mining and quarrying, over-irrigation,** dusty **mineral processing,** and untreated **industrial effluents.**
- Mining often leaves abandoned pits and over-burden, scarring the land.
- Severe deforestation-led degradation: **Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha.**
- Overgrazing-led degradation: **Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra.**
- Over-irrigation causes waterlogging, rising **salinity** and **alkalinity** in soils: **Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh.**
- Dust from grinding **limestone, calcite, soapstone** settles on soil, blocks water infiltration, and lowers soil health.
- Untreated industrial waste pollutes land and water, harming ecosystems and people.
- Waste land: rocky, arid, desert areas; non-agricultural uses: settlements, roads, railways, industry.
- Impacts: loss of soil fertility, reduced farm output, biodiversity decline, and social–environmental stress.
- Solutions: **afforestation,** managed grazing, shelter-belts, stabilising sand dunes with thorny bushes, restoring waste lands, regulating mining, and proper treatment and disposal of industrial wastes.

## Soil as a Resource

- Soil is the most important renewable resource and the medium for plant growth, supporting many forms of life.
- Soil is a living system and takes **millions of years** to form just a few centimeters.
- Main factors of soil formation: **relief (topography), parent rock/bedrock, climate, vegetation and organisms,** and **time.**
- Natural agents shaping soil: temperature changes, running water, wind, glaciers, and decomposers.
- Important internal changes: chemical reactions and organic processes that build **humus** and alter minerals.
- Soil is a mix of **organic (humus)** and **inorganic** materials.
- Indian soils are classified by formation factors and properties such as **colour, thickness, texture, age,** and **chemical/physical** traits.

## Classification of Soil

### Alluvial Soil

- Alluvial soil is the most widespread and important soil in India; the entire **Northern Plains** are made of it.
- Main source of deposits: **Indus, Ganga,** and **Brahmaputra** river systems; it also extends into **Rajasthan** and **Gujarat** through a narrow belt.
- Found in eastern coastal plains, especially the deltas of **Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna,** and **Kaveri.**
- Composition: mix of **sand, silt,** and **clay;** grain size gets coarser near upper river valleys and break of slope.
- Coarse, piedmont alluvium occurs in **Duars, Chos,** and **Terai** regions.
- Age-based types: **Bangar (old alluvium)** and **Khadar (new alluvium).**
- Bangar has more **kankar** nodules and relatively coarser texture.
- Khadar is finer, renewed by floods, and generally **more fertile** than Bangar.
- Fertility: usually adequate **potash, phosphoric acid,** and **lime;** supports **sugarcane, paddy, wheat,** cereals, and pulses.
- High fertility leads to intensive farming and dense population in alluvial regions.
- In drier tracts, alluvial soils are more **alkaline** but can be made productive with proper treatment and irrigation.

### Black Soil

- Black soils are **black** in colour and also called **regur** or **black cotton soil.**
- Best for cotton; hence the name black cotton soil.
- Formation is linked to climate and **parent rock,** especially **basalt** of the Deccan Trap.
- Distribution: Deccan plateau parts of **Maharashtra, Saurashtra (Gujarat), Malwa, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh,** extending along **Godavari** and **Krishna** valleys.
- Texture is very fine and **clayey;** soils are sticky when wet.
- High **moisture-holding capacity** supports crops during dry spells.
- Nutrient status: rich in **calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash,** and **lime;** generally poor in **phosphorus.**
- In hot weather, deep **cracks** develop, improving aeration of deeper layers.
- Farm practice tip: difficult to work when fully wet; plough immediately after the first shower or in the pre-monsoon period.

### Red and Yellow Soil

- Red soil forms over **crystalline igneous rocks** in low rainfall areas of the eastern and southern Deccan plateau.
- Yellow and red soils also occur in parts of **Odisha, Chhattisgarh,** southern middle **Ganga** plain, and along the **Western Ghats** piedmont.
- The **red** colour comes from widespread iron oxide in crystalline and metamorphic rocks.
- The soil appears **yellow** when iron is in a hydrated form.

### Laterite Soil

- Meaning and origin: **Laterite** comes from Latin 'later' meaning brick; it hardens on drying and is often used as building material.
- Formation: Develops in tropical and subtropical climates with alternate wet and dry seasons; heavy rainfall causes intense **leaching,** removing nutrients and leaving iron and aluminium oxides.
- Properties: Usually deep, **acidic (pH < 6.0),** low in nutrients like nitrogen, potash, lime; fertility improves where forest litter adds humus.
- Distribution in India: Common in southern states and the **Western Ghats** belt of Maharashtra; found in **Odisha,** parts of **West Bengal,** and several **North-East** areas.
- Humus variation: Under deciduous and evergreen forests it can be humus-rich; under sparse vegetation or semi-arid conditions it is humus-poor.
- Erosion risk: Located on uplands and hill slopes, so it is prone to **erosion** and **degradation** without conservation.
- Soil conservation and crops: With contour bunding, terracing, mulching, and manuring — especially in hilly areas of **Karnataka, Kerala,** and **Tamil Nadu** — it supports plantation crops like **tea** and **coffee.**
- Red laterite soils: In **Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh,** and **Kerala,** red laterite areas are suitable for **cashew nut** cultivation.

### Forest Soils

- These are **forest and mountain soils,** found in hilly and mountainous regions with sufficient rain and forest cover.
- Texture changes with altitude and slope conditions.
- On valley sides the soil is **loamy and silty,** supporting better moisture and nutrients.
- On upper slopes the soil is **coarse-grained,** reflecting stronger erosion and thin profiles.
- In snow-covered Himalayan areas the soils face **denudation,** are **acidic,** and have **low humus.**
- In lower valley parts, especially on **river terraces** and **alluvial fans,** the soils are **more fertile.**

## Soil Erosion and Conservation

- Soil erosion: removal of soil cover and washing down by natural forces; balance between soil formation and erosion gets disturbed by human actions.
- Human causes: **deforestation, over-grazing, construction, mining** increase erosion beyond natural rates.
- Natural agents: **running water, wind, glaciers** move soil and reshape land.
- Water erosion forms gullies in **clayey soils,** creating deep channels; such unusable land is called **bad land** and in the **Chambal** basin these are called **ravines.**
- When water flows as a uniform sheet down slopes, the **topsoil** is removed; this is **sheet erosion.**
- **Wind erosion** lifts and removes loose soil from flat or sloping land, especially in dry areas.
- Faulty farming raises risk: ploughing up-and-down the slope makes channels for fast runoff and accelerates erosion.
- Control 1: **Contour ploughing** — plough along contour lines to slow water flow on slopes.
- Control 2: **Terrace cultivation** — cut steps on slopes to reduce speed of water; widely practiced in **Western and Central Himalayas.**
- Control 3: **Strip cropping** — alternate crop strips with **grass strips** to break wind force and trap soil.
- Control 4: **Shelter belts** — rows of trees reduce wind speed; they help stabilise **sand dunes** and check desert spread in western India.
