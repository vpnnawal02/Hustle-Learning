Detailed notes on History Chapter 1: how the idea of the nation took shape in Europe — from the French Revolution and the Napoleonic Code to the unification of Germany and Italy.

## Introduction

1. In 1848, Frédéric Sorrieu, a French artist, prepared a series of four prints visualising his dream of a world made up of 'democratic and social Republics', as he called them.
2. The first print (Image on the left) of the series, shows the peoples of Europe and America – men and women of all ages and social classes – marching in a long train, and offering homage to the statue of Liberty as they pass by it.
3. Here you can recognise the torch of Enlightenment she bears in one hand and the Charter of the Rights of Man in the other. On the earth in the foreground of the image lie the shattered remains of the symbols of the government that had no restraints on the powers exercised.
4. In Sorrieu's utopian vision, the peoples of the world are grouped as distinct nations, identified through their flags and national costume. Leading the procession, way past the statue of Liberty, are the United States and Switzerland, which by this time were already nation-states.

## The French Revolution and the Idea of the Nation

1. France was a full fledged territorial state in 1789 under the rule of an absolute monarch.
2. The political and constitutional changes that came in the wake of the French Revolution led to the transfer of sovereignty from the monarchy to a body of French citizens. **The revolution proclaimed that it was the people who would henceforth constitute the nation and shape its destiny.**
3. The ideas of la patrie (the fatherland) and le citoyen (the citizen) emphasised the notion of a united community enjoying equal rights under a constitution and created a sense of collective identity amongst the French people.
4. The revolutionaries declared that it was the mission and the destiny of the French nation to liberate the peoples of Europe from despotism.
5. When the news of the events in France reached the different cities of Europe, students and other members of educated middle classes began setting up Jacobin clubs. With the outbreak of the revolutionary wars, the French armies began to carry the idea of nationalism abroad.
6. With the outbreak of the revolutionary wars, the French armies began to carry the idea of nationalism abroad.
7. **The Civil Code of 1804** – usually known as **the Napoleonic Code** – did away with all privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and secured the right to property. This Code was exported to the regions under French control.
8. In the Dutch Republic, in Switzerland, in Italy and Germany, Napoleon simplified administrative divisions, abolished the feudal system and freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues. In the towns too, guild restrictions were removed. Transport and communication systems were improved. Peasants, artisans, workers and new businessmen enjoyed a new-found freedom.
9. However, in the areas conquered, the reactions of the local populations to French rule were mixed. Increased taxation, censorship, forced conscription into the French armies required to conquer the rest of Europe, all seemed to outweigh the advantages of the administrative changes.

## The Making of Nationalism in Europe

1. What we know today as Germany, Italy and Switzerland were divided into kingdoms, duchies and cantons whose rulers had their autonomous territories.
2. They did not see themselves as sharing a collective identity or a common culture. Often, they even spoke different languages and belonged to different ethnic groups.

## The Aristocracy and the New Middle Class

1. **Aristocracy** refers to a system of government or a social class where a small, privileged minority holds power and high social status, typically based on noble birth, wealth, or other inherited qualifications.
2. People with land were the aristocrats on the continent.
3. They owned estates in the countryside and also town-houses. They spoke French for purposes of diplomacy and in high society. Their families were often connected by ties of marriage.
4. This powerful aristocracy was, however, numerically a small group. The majority of the population was made up of the **peasantry**.
5. Industrialisation began in England in the second half of the eighteenth century, but in France and parts of the German states it occurred only during the nineteenth century.
6. In its wake, new social groups came into being: a working-class population, and middle classes made up of industrialists, businessmen, professionals.
7. Due to these classes ideas of national unity following the abolition of aristocratic privileges gained popularity.

## What did Liberal Nationalism Stand for?

1. The term liberalism comes from the Latin **liber** (free). For the middle classes of Europe in the early 19th century, it symbolized individual freedom and equality before the law. It was a guiding ideology for both social and political reform.
2. Liberalism emphasized government by consent, end of autocratic rule and clerical privileges, adoption of constitutions, and establishment of representative parliaments. It drew heavily from the ideas of the French Revolution, which stood for liberty and equality.
3. Despite its stress on equality before law, liberalism did not promote universal suffrage.
4. In France, political rights like voting and elections were only granted to property-owning men. Women and non-propertied men were excluded, which contradicted the ideal of true democracy.
5. Only under the Jacobins did all adult males enjoy voting rights for a short time. Later, the Napoleonic Code restricted suffrage again and lowered women to a minor status, bound to the authority of fathers and husbands.
6. This exclusion pushed women and poorer men to organize movements demanding equal political rights throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries.
7. Liberalism also meant economic freedom — free markets, free movement of goods, and no restrictions by the state.
8. The new middle classes strongly opposed barriers like multiple customs duties and inconsistent systems of weights and measures, which they saw as major hurdles to trade and economic growth.
9. In the German-speaking regions, Napoleon's restructuring had left 39 states, each with its own currencies and measures, creating serious problems for merchants.
10. In 1834, Prussia initiated the Zollverein (customs union), joined by most German states. It removed tariff barriers and reduced currencies from 30+ to 2.
11. The growth of railways further improved trade and mobility, linking economic interests with nationalist ideas. This economic nationalism reinforced the broader movement for national unification.

## A New Conservatism after 1815

1. **Conservatism** is a political philosophy that stressed the importance of tradition, established institutions and customs, and preferred gradual development to quick change
2. After Napoleon's defeat in 1815, Europe entered an age of conservatism, where governments sought to preserve traditional institutions like monarchy, the Church, social hierarchies, property, and the family.
3. Conservatives, however, did not want to fully return to the pre-revolutionary world. They realized from Napoleon's rule that **modern reforms could help strengthen monarchies** instead of destroying them.
4. Elements of modernization that conservatives supported included the creation of a modern army, the development of an efficient bureaucracy, a stronger economy, and the abolition of outdated practices like feudalism and serfdom.
5. To decide Europe's future, the **Congress of Vienna** was held in 1815, attended by Britain, Russia, Prussia, and Austria—the main powers that defeated Napoleon.
6. The Congress was hosted by **Austrian Chancellor Duke Metternich**, who played a leading role in shaping the post-war settlement.
7. The outcome was the **Treaty of Vienna (1815)**, which aimed to undo Napoleonic changes and restore monarchies across Europe.
8. The **Bourbon dynasty** was restored in France, and France was forced to give up all territories it had annexed during Napoleon's rule.
9. To contain France and prevent future expansion, a number of **buffer states** were created: the Kingdom of Netherlands (including Belgium) in the north, and Genoa was added to Piedmont in the south.
10. Territories were redistributed among the victorious powers: Prussia gained new lands on its western frontier and a part of Saxony; Austria got control over northern Italy; Russia obtained part of Poland; while the German Confederation of 39 states was left untouched.
11. The central aim of the Vienna settlement was to **restore old monarchies** and maintain a balance of power in Europe so that no single state could dominate as France once had.
12. The conservative regimes established after 1815 were **autocratic**. They crushed criticism and dissent, imposed censorship on newspapers, books, plays, and songs, and suppressed liberal-nationalist ideas. However, the memory of the French Revolution survived and continued to inspire liberals, who criticized the conservative order and strongly demanded **freedom of the press**.

## The Revolutionaries

1. After 1815, the **fear of repression** forced many liberal-nationalists to go underground as conservative regimes tried to crush revolutionary ideas.
2. **Secret societies** began to emerge in several European states with the aim of training revolutionaries and spreading ideas of liberty and nationalism.
3. To be a **revolutionary** at this time meant opposing the monarchical governments established after the Vienna Congress and actively fighting for liberty and freedom.

Many revolutionaries also believed that the creation of **independent nation-states** was essential for true liberty and progress.

4. Among the most significant revolutionary leaders was **Giuseppe Mazzini**, born in Genoa in 1805.
5. As a young man, Mazzini joined the **Carbonari**, a secret society in Italy, and quickly became involved in revolutionary activity.
6. At the age of 24, in 1831, Mazzini was **exiled** for attempting a revolution in Liguria, which marked the beginning of his long career as a political activist in exile.
7. In exile, Mazzini went on to found two influential underground organizations: **Young Italy** in Marseilles, and later **Young Europe** in Berne.
8. Young Europe united like-minded young revolutionaries from different countries such as Poland, France, Italy, and the German states, creating a network of international solidarity.
9. Mazzini strongly believed that **God had created nations as the natural units of mankind**, and therefore Italy should not remain divided into multiple states and kingdoms.
10. His vision was the creation of a **unified Italian republic**, which would be part of a wider alliance of democratic republics across Europe. This, he believed, was the true foundation of liberty.
11. Inspired by his ideas, **similar secret societies** were later set up in Germany, France, Switzerland, and Poland, spreading the cause of nationalism.
12. Mazzini's uncompromising opposition to monarchy and his call for democratic republics deeply alarmed conservatives. Metternich, the Austrian Chancellor, even described him as "**the most dangerous enemy of our social order.**"

## The Age of Revolutions: 1830-1848

1. Conservative regimes in Europe tried to consolidate their power, and liberalism with nationalism became linked with revolutions in regions like Italian and German states, Ottoman provinces, Ireland, and Poland.
2. Revolutions were led by liberal-nationalists from the educated middle-class elite such as professors, teachers, clerks, and commercial middle classes.
3. In July 1830, the first upheaval occurred in France where Bourbon kings were overthrown and a constitutional monarchy was established with Louis Philippe as the head.
4. Metternich remarked, "When France sneezes, the rest of Europe catches cold," as the July Revolution sparked uprisings like in Brussels where Belgium broke away from the United Kingdom of the Netherlands.
5. The Greek War of Independence began in 1821 as Greece, under Ottoman rule since the fifteenth century, started its struggle for freedom.
6. The struggle gained support from Greeks in exile and West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture, with poets and artists portraying Greece as the cradle of European civilisation.
7. English poet Lord Byron raised funds, joined the war, and died of fever in 1824 while fighting for Greece.
8. The Treaty of Constantinople in 1832 recognised Greece as an independent nation.

## The Romantic Imagination and National Feeling

1. Nationalism developed not only through wars and territorial expansion but also through culture, where art, poetry, stories, and music helped express and shape nationalist feelings.
2. Romanticism was a cultural movement that promoted nationalist sentiment by criticising the glorification of reason and science and instead focusing on emotions, intuition, and mystical feelings.
3. Romantic artists and poets aimed to create a sense of shared collective heritage and a common cultural past as the foundation of a nation.
4. Johann Gottfried Herder, a German philosopher, argued that true German culture existed among the common people (das volk) and could be expressed through folk songs, folk poetry, and folk dances, which together represented the spirit of the nation (volksgeist).
5. Collecting and recording folk culture and promoting vernacular languages were essential for nation-building, as they helped spread nationalist messages to largely illiterate populations.
6. In Poland, which had been partitioned by Russia, Prussia, and Austria in the eighteenth century, national feelings were preserved through music and language even without political independence.
7. Karol Kurpinski used operas and music to celebrate the national struggle, turning traditional folk dances like the polonaise and mazurka into nationalist symbols.
8. After Russian occupation, the Polish language was banned from schools and replaced with Russian, leading to resistance.
9. An armed rebellion against Russian rule in 1831 was crushed, but Polish clergy used the native language in church gatherings and religious instruction as an act of defiance.
10. Many priests and bishops were jailed or exiled to Siberia for refusing to preach in Russian, and Polish language itself became a symbol of resistance against Russian dominance.

## Hunger, Hardship and Popular Revolt

1. The 1830s in Europe were marked by severe economic hardship, with rapid population growth leading to more job seekers than available employment.
2. Many people from rural areas migrated to cities, where they lived in overcrowded slums.
3. Small producers in towns faced tough competition from cheap machine-made goods imported from England, which was more industrially advanced.
4. Textile production in much of Europe was still carried out in homes or small workshops and was only partly mechanised, making it vulnerable to English competition.
5. In regions where the aristocracy still held power, peasants continued to suffer under heavy feudal dues and obligations.
6. Rising food prices or a year of poor harvest caused widespread poverty in both towns and rural areas.
7. The year 1848 saw food shortages and massive unemployment in Paris, leading to uprisings where barricades were set up and Louis Philippe was forced to flee.
8. A National Assembly declared France a Republic, introduced suffrage for all adult males above 21, and guaranteed the right to work, while setting up national workshops to provide employment.
9. In 1845, weavers in Silesia revolted against contractors who supplied raw materials and orders for textiles but had sharply reduced their payments.

## 1848: The Revolution of the Liberals

1. In 1848, alongside revolts of peasants and workers, a revolution led by the educated middle classes also spread across Europe.
2. The February 1848 Revolution in France forced the monarch to abdicate, and a Republic based on **universal male suffrage** was proclaimed.
3. In regions without independent nation-states, such as **Germany, Italy, Poland, and the Austro-Hungarian Empire**, the liberal middle classes demanded constitutionalism along with national unification.
4. Their demands included a **constitution, freedom of the press,** and **freedom of association**.
5. In the German regions, middle-class professionals, businessmen, and artisans formed political associations that gathered in **Frankfurt** and voted for an **all-German National Assembly**.
6. On **18 May 1848, 831 elected representatives** assembled in the **Church of St Paul (Frankfurt Parliament)** and drafted a constitution for a German nation headed by a monarchy subject to a parliament.
7. The crown was offered to **Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia**, but he rejected it and, along with other monarchs, opposed the elected assembly.
8. The Frankfurt Parliament, dominated by the middle class, resisted the demands of workers and artisans, losing their support, while aristocracy and military opposition grew stronger.
9. Eventually, troops were called in, and the assembly was forcibly disbanded.
10. Women actively participated in the liberal movements by forming associations, publishing newspapers, and joining meetings and demonstrations.
11. Despite this, women were denied **suffrage rights**, and in the Frankfurt Parliament they were only admitted as **observers** in the visitors' gallery.
12. Although conservative forces suppressed liberal movements in 1848, the old order could not be fully restored, and monarchs realised they had to grant concessions.
13. After 1848, autocratic monarchies of Central and Eastern Europe introduced reforms such as the abolition of **serfdom** and **bonded labour** in the **Habsburg dominions** and **Russia**.
14. In **1867**, the Habsburg rulers granted greater autonomy to the **Hungarians**.

## The Making of Germany and Italy

### Germany: Can the Army be the Architect of a Nation?

1. After 1848, nationalism in Europe moved away from its link with democracy and revolution, and was instead used by conservatives to promote state power and political domination.
2. In **Germany**, nationalist feelings were widespread among the middle class, who in **1848** tried to unite the German confederation into a nation-state governed by an elected parliament, but this attempt was suppressed by the monarchy, military, and large landowners (Junkers) of Prussia.
3. From then on, **Prussia** took leadership of the unification movement under its chief minister **Otto von Bismarck**, who used the Prussian army and bureaucracy to achieve success.
4. Three wars fought over seven years – with **Austria, Denmark, and France** – ended in Prussian victory and led to the unification of Germany.
5. In **January 1871**, the Prussian king **William I** was proclaimed **German Emperor (Kaiser)** at a ceremony in **Versailles**.
6. On **18 January 1871**, in the cold Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles, princes of the German states, army representatives, Prussian ministers, and **Bismarck** proclaimed the new German Empire.
7. The unification of Germany highlighted the dominance of **Prussian state power**, as the new state placed strong emphasis on modernising the **currency, banking, legal, and judicial systems**, with Prussian practices serving as a model for the rest of Germany.

### Italy Unified

1. **Italy** had a long history of political fragmentation, with Italians scattered across several dynastic states and the multi-national **Habsburg Empire**.
2. In the nineteenth century, Italy was divided into **seven states,** of which only **Sardinia-Piedmont** was ruled by an Italian princely house.
3. The **north** was under **Austrian Habsburgs**, the **centre** was controlled by the **Pope**, and the **south** was dominated by the **Bourbon kings of Spain**.
4. The **Italian language** lacked a common form and existed in many regional and local variations.
5. **Giuseppe Mazzini** in the **1830s** tried to promote the idea of a unitary **Italian Republic** and founded the secret society **Young Italy**.
6. The failure of uprisings in **1831** and **1848** shifted the responsibility of unification to **Sardinia-Piedmont** under **King Victor Emmanuel II**.
7. For the elites of Sardinia-Piedmont, a unified Italy meant economic development and political dominance.
8. **Chief Minister Cavour**, though not a revolutionary or democrat, led the unification through diplomacy and alliances.
9. Through an alliance with **France, Cavour** defeated the **Austrian forces** in **1859**.
10. **Giuseppe Garibaldi** led armed volunteers who marched into **South Italy** and the **Kingdom of the Two Sicilies** in **1860**, gaining peasant support to drive out the **Spanish rulers**.
11. In **1861, Victor Emmanuel II** was proclaimed **King of United Italy**.
12. Much of the Italian population remained unaware of nationalist ideology due to illiteracy, and southern peasants misunderstood **Italia** as **'La Talia'**, thinking it was the king's wife.

## The Strange Case of Britain

1. The **nation-state in Britain** was formed through a **long process,** not by a sudden revolution.
2. Before the **eighteenth century,** there was no British nation, only ethnic identities like **English, Welsh, Scot, and Irish**.
3. As the **English nation** grew in **wealth, power, and importance**, it extended influence over the other groups.
4. The **English Parliament,** which gained power after **1688,** became the instrument for forging a nation-state with **England at the centre**.
5. The **Act of Union (1707)** between **England** and **Scotland** created the **United Kingdom of Great Britain**, but it meant English dominance over Scotland.
6. **Scottish culture and institutions** were suppressed; the **Catholic clans of the Highlands** faced repression, were forbidden to speak **Gaelic** or wear national dress, and many were forcibly displaced.
7. **Ireland** was divided between **Catholics and Protestants**; the English supported Protestant dominance over the Catholic majority.
8. After a failed revolt by **Wolfe Tone and the United Irishmen (1798),** Ireland was incorporated into **the UK in 1801**.
9. A new **British identity** was created through **English culture**, the **Union Jack,** the national anthem **'God Save Our Noble King'**, and the promotion of the **English language**.
10. The older nations survived only as **subordinate partners** within the union.

## Visualising the Nation

1. In the **eighteenth and nineteenth centuries,** artists personified nations by representing them as **female figures,** giving an abstract idea a concrete form.
2. During the **French Revolution,** female allegories were used to portray ideals such as **Liberty, Justice, and the Republic**.
3. **Liberty** was shown with a **red cap** or **broken chain,** while **Justice** was depicted as a **blindfolded woman with scales**.
4. In **France,** the female allegory was named **Marianne,** symbolising a people's nation.
5. **Marianne's attributes** were drawn from Liberty and the Republic – the **red cap, the tricolour, and the cockade**.
6. **Statues of Marianne** were placed in public squares, and her images were used on **coins and stamps** to symbolise unity.
7. In **Germany,** the allegory was **Germania,** shown wearing a **crown of oak leaves,** as the **oak symbolised heroism**.

## Nationalism and Imperialism

1. By the **last quarter of the nineteenth century,** nationalism lost its liberal-democratic ideals and turned into a **narrow creed,** intolerant and war-driven.
2. The most serious source of nationalist tension after **1871** was the **Balkans,** comprising **Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia, and Montenegro,** inhabited mainly by the **Slavs**.
3. A large part of the Balkans was under the **Ottoman Empire,** which tried reforms and modernisation but failed, leading to its decline.
4. Various Balkan nationalities broke away, declaring independence and using **history** to justify claims of lost independence.
5. The **Balkan states** were jealous of one another, each seeking more territory, which led to intense conflict.
6. The Balkans also became a centre of **big power rivalry** among **Russia, Germany, England, and Austro-Hungary,** each trying to extend control.
7. This rivalry caused a series of wars in the Balkans, eventually leading to the **First World War (1914)**.
8. Nationalism aligned with **imperialism,** pushing Europe into disaster.
9. Meanwhile, **anti-imperial nationalist movements** grew in colonised regions worldwide, aiming to form **independent nation-states**.
10. These movements forged **collective unity against imperialism**, creating their own versions of nationalism while accepting the idea of nation-states as natural and universal.
