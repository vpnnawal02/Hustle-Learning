Detailed notes on History Chapter 4: how print travelled from East Asia to Europe and India — woodblocks, Gutenberg's press, the reading public, religious debate, and colonial censorship.

## Introduction

- Print is part of daily life: books, journals, newspapers, posters, theatre programmes, official circulars, calendars, diaries, ads, and cinema posters.
- We read printed literature, see printed images, follow news in newspapers, and watch public debates in print.
- We take this world of print for granted and forget there was a time before print.
- Print has a history that has shaped the modern world.
- Key questions raised: What is the history of print? When did printed literature start to circulate? How has print helped create the modern world?
- This chapter studies the development of print from its beginnings in East Asia to its spread in Europe and in India.
- It explains how the spread of printing technology changed social life and culture.

## The First Printed Books

- Earliest print technology arose in **China, Japan, and Korea** as hand printing using **woodblocks.**
- From **AD 594,** in **China,** paper was rubbed against inked woodblocks to print books.
- Thin, porous paper meant both sides could not be printed; pages were folded into the Chinese **"accordion book"** and stitched at the side.
- Skilled craftsmen reproduced the beauty of **calligraphy** with great accuracy using woodblock printing.
- For a long time, the **Chinese imperial state** was the main producer of printed material.
- China had a large **bureaucracy** recruited through **civil service examinations,** so the state sponsored massive printing of **exam textbooks.**
- From the **sixteenth century,** the number of **examination candidates** increased, raising the volume of printed material.
- By the **seventeenth century,** with growing **urban culture,** printing had more uses beyond officials and scholars.
- **Merchants** used printed materials to collect and track **trade information** in daily business.
- Reading became a **leisure activity** with growing popularity in cities.
- New readers preferred **fictional narratives, poetry, autobiographies, anthologies,** and **romantic plays.**
- **Rich women** began to read; many **women authors** started publishing **poetry** and **plays.**
- **Wives of scholar-officials** published their writings; **courtesans** wrote about their lives.
- In the **late nineteenth century,** Western powers set up outposts in China, bringing **Western printing techniques** and **mechanical presses.**
- **Shanghai** became the hub of the new **print culture,** serving **Western-style schools.**
- Printing gradually shifted from traditional **hand printing** to **mechanical printing.**

## Print in Japan

- **Buddhist missionaries** from **China** introduced hand-printing to **Japan** around AD 768–770.
- The oldest Japanese printed book is the **Buddhist Diamond Sutra,** dated **AD 868,** with six sheets of text and **woodcut illustrations.**
- Pictures were printed on **textiles, playing cards,** and **paper money,** showing wide use of print beyond books.
- In medieval **Japan,** poets and prose writers were regularly published; **books were cheap and abundant.**
- Printing of **visual material** encouraged new publishing practices and popular illustrated works.
- In the **late eighteenth century,** in urban **Edo** (later **Tokyo**), illustrated collections portrayed elegant city life with **artists, courtesans,** and **teahouse gatherings.**
- **Libraries** and **bookstores** were full of hand-printed materials: books on **women, musical instruments, calculations, tea ceremony, flower arrangements, proper etiquette, cooking,** and **famous places.**

## Print Comes to Europe

- For centuries, **silk** and **spices** from **China** reached **Europe** via the **Silk Route;** in the **eleventh century, Chinese paper** also arrived the same way, enabling manuscript production by **scribes.**
- In **1295, Marco Polo** returned to **Italy** after years in **China** and shared knowledge of **woodblock printing,** which led Italians to produce woodblock-printed books; the method soon spread across **Europe.**
- Luxury editions continued to be handwritten on costly **vellum,** serving **aristocrats** and **monastic libraries,** who scorned printed books as cheap; meanwhile, **merchants** and **students** bought cheaper printed copies.
- Rising demand led **booksellers** to export books widely and hold **book fairs;** manuscript production adapted, with **scribes** now hired by booksellers, sometimes **50+ scribes** working for one seller to meet demand.
- Handwritten manuscript copying was **expensive, slow,** and **labour-intensive;** manuscripts were **fragile, awkward,** and hard to carry or read, so their circulation stayed **limited.**
- As demand grew, **woodblock printing** became increasingly popular; by the **early fifteenth century,** woodblocks were widely used in Europe to print **textiles, playing cards,** and **religious pictures** with short texts.
- There was a clear need for **faster** and **cheaper** text reproduction, pushing innovation toward a new technology.
- The breakthrough came at **Strasbourg, Germany,** where **Johann Gutenberg** developed the first-known **printing press** in the **1430s.**

## Gutenberg and the Printing Press

- **Johann Gutenberg** was the son of a **merchant** and grew up on a large **agricultural estate;** from childhood he saw **wine and olive presses,** which later inspired his design.
- He learned **stone polishing,** became a master **goldsmith,** and mastered making **lead moulds** for trinkets; he combined these skills to adapt existing technologies for printing.
- The **olive press** was the model for the **printing press,** and metal **moulds** were used to cast movable **metal types** for alphabet letters.
- By **1448,** Gutenberg had perfected his system; the first printed book was the Bible.
- About **180 copies** of the Gutenberg Bible were produced, taking roughly **three years** — considered very fast for that period.
- Early printed books closely **resembled manuscripts** in look and layout; metal letters imitated **ornamental handwriting.**
- Pages had **hand-illuminated borders** with foliage and patterns; **illustrations were painted** by hand even after printing.
- For luxury buyers, printers left **blank spaces** for custom decoration; purchasers chose the **designs** and the **painting school** for illustrations.
- Between **1450 and 1550, printing presses** were established in most European countries; **German printers** traveled widely to set up and staff new presses.
- As presses multiplied, **book production boomed:** in the late fifteenth century, about **20 million** printed books entered European markets.
- In the **sixteenth century,** output rose to roughly **200 million** copies, marking the shift from **hand printing** to **mechanical printing.**
- This rapid expansion is known as the **print revolution,** transforming the **scale, speed,** and **accessibility** of books across Europe.

## The Print Revolution and Its Impact

### A New Reading Public

- With the **printing press,** a new **reading public** emerged as books became cheaper and faster to produce in multiple copies.
- Book **costs fell,** and less **time and labour** were needed per book, so markets were flooded with printed material for growing audiences.
- Access to books created a new **culture of reading;** earlier, reading was limited to **elites,** while common people relied on **oral culture.**
- In the oral world, people heard **sacred texts, ballads,** and **folk tales** recited; knowledge was shared through collective listening and performances.
- Before print, books were **expensive** and too **few** to reach many; after print, books could reach **wider sections** of society, shifting from a "hearing public" to a **reading public.**
- The shift was gradual because **literacy rates** in most European countries stayed **low** until the twentieth century.
- Publishers adapted by targeting non-literate audiences: they issued **popular ballads** and **folk tales,** often **profusely illustrated.**
- Such printed pieces were **sung and recited** at village gatherings and in **taverns,** so even non-readers could enjoy them.
- **Oral culture** entered **print,** and printed material was spread **orally,** blurring the line between **hearing** and **reading** cultures.
- Over time, the **hearing public** and **reading public intermingled,** making print central to both spoken and silent consumption of stories and ideas.

## Religious Debates and the Fear of Print

- **Print** enabled the wide spread of ideas and opened a new world of **debate** and **discussion.**
- People who disagreed with authorities could now **print and circulate** their views, persuading others and moving them to **action.**
- Many welcomed print, but there were also **fears:** easier access to books might spread **rebellious** or **irreligious** ideas and undermine the authority of "valuable" literature.
- This anxiety was voiced by **religious authorities, monarchs,** and many **writers and artists,** leading to strong criticism of the new printed literature.
- Example in **religion:** In **1517, Martin Luther** wrote the **Ninety Five Theses** criticizing practices of the **Roman Catholic Church.**
- A printed copy was posted on a church door in **Wittenberg,** inviting debate and challenging Church authority.
- Luther's writings were **rapidly reproduced** and widely read, causing a split in the Church and starting the **Protestant Reformation.**
- Luther's **New Testament translation** sold about **5,000 copies** within weeks; a **second edition** appeared within three months.
- Luther praised print: **"Printing is the ultimate gift of God and the greatest one."**
- Many scholars believe print created a **new intellectual atmosphere,** helping spread the ideas that led to the **Reformation.**

## Print and Dissent

- **Print** and popular religious books encouraged personal interpretations of faith, even among little-educated working people.
- In the **sixteenth century, Menocchio,** an Italian miller, read locally available books and reinterpreted the **Bible,** forming views on **God** and **Creation** that angered the **Roman Catholic Church.**
- During the Church's **Inquisition** against heresy, Menocchio was tried twice and ultimately **executed** for his beliefs.
- Alarmed by such outcomes of popular reading and questioning, the **Roman Church** imposed strict controls on **publishers** and **booksellers.**
- From **1558,** the Church maintained an **Index of Prohibited Books** to curb the spread of ideas it deemed heretical.

## The Reading Mania

- Through the **seventeenth** and **eighteenth centuries, literacy rates** rose across Europe as churches of various denominations set up **village schools,** bringing reading skills to **peasants** and **artisans.**
- By the late **eighteenth century,** some European regions reached **60–80% literacy,** creating a strong demand for reading material and a virtual **reading mania.**
- As literacy spread, **printers** issued books in **ever-increasing numbers,** and new forms of **popular literature** targeted newly literate audiences.
- **Booksellers** sent **pedlars** to villages with small, affordable books; common offerings included **almanacs (ritual calendars), ballads,** and **folktales.**
- In **England,** cheap **penny chapbooks** were sold by **chapmen,** making reading accessible even to the **poor.**
- In **France,** the **Bibliothèque Bleue** featured **low-priced,** small books printed on **poor-quality paper** with **cheap blue covers.**
- Other popular formats included short **romances** (4–6 pages) and more substantial **"histories"** (narratives about the past), showing wide variety in **size, purpose,** and **audience.**
- From the early **eighteenth century,** the **periodical press** grew, mixing **current affairs** with **entertainment** in **newspapers** and **journals.**
- Periodicals reported on **wars, trade,** and **developments** in distant places, widening public awareness and curiosity.
- **Scientific** and **philosophical** ideas became more accessible: ancient and medieval **scientific texts** were compiled and printed; **maps** and **scientific diagrams** circulated widely.
- When scientists like **Isaac Newton** published their discoveries, they reached a broader circle of **scientifically minded readers,** expanding the impact of new knowledge.
- Writings of thinkers such as **Thomas Paine, Voltaire,** and **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** were widely printed and read, spreading ideas of **science, reason,** and **rationality** into **popular literature.**

## 'Tremble, therefore, tyrants of the world!'

- By the **mid-eighteenth century,** many people believed that **books** spread **progress** and **enlightenment,** and could help free society from **despotism** and **tyranny.**
- It was widely felt that **reason** and **intellect** would guide society if ideas could circulate through print, making reading a transformative act.
- **Louise-Sébastien Mercier** (eighteenth-century France) declared: "The **printing press** is the most powerful engine of **progress** and **public opinion** is the force that will sweep **despotism** away."
- In Mercier's novels, heroes are changed by **reading:** they consume books, immerse themselves in new worlds, and become **enlightened.**
- Convinced of print's power to defeat tyranny, Mercier proclaimed: **"Tremble, tyrants of the world! Tremble before the virtual writer!"**

## Print Culture and the French Revolution

- By the **mid-eighteenth century,** many believed that **print culture** helped create conditions for the **French Revolution.**
- Argument 1: Print spread the ideas of **Enlightenment thinkers** who criticised **tradition, superstition,** and **despotism,** and promoted the **rule of reason** and **rationality** over custom.
- Their writings challenged the **sacred authority of the Church** and the **despotic power of the state,** weakening the legitimacy of a social order based on tradition.
- Works of **Voltaire** and **Rousseau** were widely read, encouraging readers to see the world with **questioning, critical,** and **rational** eyes.
- Argument 2: Print fostered a **culture of dialogue and debate** where values, norms, and institutions were re-examined by a public newly aware of the **power of reason.**
- In this public culture, **new ideas of social revolution** took shape as people discussed and challenged existing beliefs.
- Argument 3: By the **1780s,** there was a surge of literature **mocking royalty** and **criticising their morality,** which questioned the existing social order.
- **Cartoons and caricatures** portrayed a monarchy lost in **sensual pleasures** while the **common people** suffered, spreading **hostile sentiments** against the crown.
- This critical literature often **circulated underground,** amplifying opposition to monarchy despite censorship.
- How to assess these claims: **Print clearly spread ideas,** but readers encountered **diverse literatures** — Enlightenment critiques alongside **monarchical** and **Church propaganda.**
- People were **not directly shaped** by everything they read; they **accepted some ideas, rejected others,** and **interpreted** them in their own ways.
- Conclusion: Print did not mechanically determine beliefs, but it **opened the possibility of thinking differently,** enabling the **questioning** that underpinned revolutionary change.

## Children, Women and Workers

- From the late nineteenth century, with **primary education compulsory, children** became a major group of readers, making **school textbooks** vital for publishers.
- A dedicated **children's press** was founded in **France in 1857,** printing new works and **old fairy tales** and **folk tales.**
- In **Germany,** the **Grimm Brothers** compiled **folk tales** from peasants; these were **edited** before publication in **1812** to remove parts seen as **unsuitable** or **vulgar,** so rural tales were both **recorded** and **changed** in print.
- **Women** became important as **readers and writers; penny magazines** and **housekeeping/etiquette manuals** targeted women specifically.
- With the rise of **novels** in the nineteenth century, women were seen as key readers; notable women novelists included **Jane Austen,** the **Brontë sisters,** and **George Eliot,** whose works helped define a new image of womanhood — **will, strength, determination,** and the **power to think.**
- **Lending libraries** existed since the **seventeenth century;** in the **nineteenth century** in **England,** they educated **white-collar workers, artisans,** and the **lower-middle class.**
- As the working day shortened from the **mid-nineteenth century,** workers gained time for **self-improvement** and **self-expression,** producing large numbers of **political tracts** and **autobiographies.**

## Further Innovations

- By the **late eighteenth century,** presses were made of **metal,** replacing earlier materials and enabling sturdier, more precise machines.
- Across the **nineteenth century,** printing saw many **technological innovations,** steadily increasing speed, reliability, and print quality.
- By the **mid-nineteenth century, Richard M. Hoe** of **New York** perfected the **power-driven cylindrical press,** capable of printing about **8,000 sheets per hour,** ideal for **newspapers** due to high-speed output.
- In the **late nineteenth century,** the **offset press** emerged, able to print up to **six colours at a time,** greatly improving multi-colour images and commercial graphics.
- From the **early twentieth century, electrically operated presses** further **accelerated** operations and improved consistency in long print runs.
- Additional advances improved the whole workflow: better **paper-feeding methods,** higher-quality **plates, automatic paper reels,** and **photoelectric controls** for accurate **colour register.**
- These many mechanical improvements together **transformed the appearance** of printed texts, yielding cleaner type, better alignment, and richer illustration quality.
- Printers and publishers also innovated in **marketing: nineteenth-century periodicals** often **serialised novels,** which shaped how authors structured stories in instalments.
- In **1920s England,** popular books were issued in cheap **series** like the **Shilling Series,** widening access for price-sensitive readers.
- The **dust cover (book jacket)** is a **twentieth-century** innovation, used for protection and as a marketing surface for titles and artwork.
- During the **Great Depression (1930s),** fearing a slump in sales, publishers promoted **cheap paperback editions** to keep book buying **affordable** and demand steady.

## Manuscripts Before the Age of Print

- India had a rich, old tradition of handwritten **manuscripts** in **Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian,** and many **vernacular** languages.
- Manuscripts were copied on **palm leaves** or **handmade paper,** with pages often **beautifully illustrated.**
- For preservation, pages were **pressed between wooden covers** or **sewn together.**
- Manuscripts continued to be produced even after print arrived, up to the **late nineteenth century.**
- Manuscripts were **expensive** and **fragile;** they had to be handled carefully.
- Reading them was difficult because **scripts** were written in **different styles,** so they were not used widely in daily life.
- In pre-colonial **Bengal,** there was an extensive network of **village primary schools.**
- Students often did not **read texts;** they mainly **learned to write.**
- Teachers **dictated** portions of texts from memory, and students **copied** them.
- Many became **literate** without actually **reading** any texts.

## Print Comes to India

- The printing press first came to **Goa** with **Portuguese missionaries** in the **mid-sixteenth century.**
- **Jesuit priests** learned **Konkani** and printed several **tracts;** by **1674,** about **50 books** had been printed in **Konkani** and **Kanara** languages.
- **Catholic priests** printed the first **Tamil** book in **1579** at **Cochin;** the first **Malayalam** book was printed in **1713** by them.
- By **1710, Dutch Protestant missionaries** had printed **32 Tamil texts,** many being **translations of older works.**
- The **English-language press** in India grew late, even though the **English East India Company** began importing presses from the **late seventeenth century.**
- From **1780, James Augustus Hickey** edited the **Bengal Gazette,** a weekly calling itself "a commercial paper open to all, but influenced by none," marking private English enterprise in Indian printing.
- Hickey ran many **advertisements** (including for the import and sale of slaves) and printed **gossip** about Company officials, angering **Governor-General Warren Hastings.**
- **Hastings persecuted** Hickey and promoted **officially sanctioned newspapers** to counter information that harmed the colonial government's image.
- By the **late eighteenth century,** several **newspapers and journals** were in print in India.
- Indians also began publishing newspapers; the first was the weekly **Bengal Gazette** by **Gangadhar Bhattacharya,** who was close to **Rammohun Roy.**

## Religious Reform and Public Debates

- From the early nineteenth century, intense public debates on **religious issues** unfolded in **print,** as different groups in colonial society offered new interpretations, criticised practices, or countered reformers, bringing a wider public into discussion.
- Printed **tracts** and **newspapers** not only spread ideas but also shaped the form of debate, enabling new ideas to emerge through clashes of opinion in accessible, everyday language.
- Major controversies pitted **social and religious reformers** against **Hindu orthodoxy** over issues like **widow immolation (sati), monotheism, Brahmanical priesthood,** and **idolatry,** with a proliferation of tracts and newspapers in Bengal.
- **Rammohun Roy** published the **Sambad Kaumudi (1821);** in response, Hindu orthodoxy backed **Samachar Chandrika** to oppose his views, reflecting the contested public sphere.
- From **1822,** two **Persian** newspapers appeared: **Jam-i-Jahan Nama** and **Shamsul Akhbar;** the **Gujarati** paper **Bombay Samachar** also began the same year, widening regional participation.
- In north India, anxious about the decline of **Muslim dynasties** and potential colonial changes to **Muslim personal law,** the **ulama** used cheap **lithographic presses** to publish **Persian and Urdu translations** of scriptures and to issue **religious newspapers** and **tracts.**
- The **Deoband Seminary (1867)** published thousands of **fatwas,** guiding everyday conduct and explaining **Islamic doctrines,** while multiple **Muslim sects and seminaries** used **Urdu print** to debate and expand followings.
- Among **Hindus,** print boosted reading of **vernacular religious texts:** the first printed **Ramcharitmanas (Tulsidas)** appeared in **1810** from **Calcutta;** by the mid-nineteenth century, **cheap lithographic** editions flooded north Indian markets.
- From the **1880s, Naval Kishore Press (Lucknow)** and **Shri Venkateshwar Press (Bombay)** issued numerous **vernacular religious texts,** making them portable and easy to read or recite to **illiterate** audiences.
- Printed religious texts reached **very wide circles,** encouraging **discussions, debates,** and **controversies** within and among religions, while also connecting communities across regions.
- Newspapers carried **news across India,** helping create **pan-Indian identities** by linking distant places and communities through shared information and debate.

## New Forms of Publication

- Printing increased the demand for new kinds of writing, as more readers wanted to see their own lives, feelings, and relationships reflected in texts.
- The **novel** (a form developed in Europe) met this need and soon took on **distinctively Indian** styles and themes.
- Novels opened new worlds of experience for readers and showed the **diversity of human lives** in vivid detail.
- New literary forms — **lyrics, short stories,** and **essays** on social and political issues — also grew, stressing human emotions and everyday life.
- These forms highlighted how **social and political rules** shape personal feelings and relationships.
- By the **late nineteenth century,** a new **visual culture** emerged as more presses enabled easy reproduction of images.
- **Raja Ravi Varma** and others created images for **mass circulation,** spreading art to wider audiences.
- **Wood engravers** set up near letterpresses; print shops hired them to supply **woodblocks** for images.
- **Cheap prints and calendars** sold in bazaars let even the **poor** decorate homes and workplaces.
- These popular images shaped ideas about **modernity and tradition, religion and politics,** and **society and culture.**
- By the **1870s, caricatures and cartoons** appeared in journals and newspapers, commenting on **social** and **political** issues.
- Some cartoons mocked educated Indians' **Western tastes and clothes;** others reflected **fears of social change.**
- There were **imperial caricatures** attacking nationalists, and **nationalist cartoons** criticising **imperial rule.**

## Women and Print

- Lives and feelings of **women** began to be written in vivid, intense ways; women's **reading** rose sharply in **middle-class homes.**
- **Liberal husbands and fathers** educated women at home and later sent them to **women's schools** set up in cities and towns after the **mid-nineteenth century.**
- Many **journals** published women's writings and argued for **women's education;** they also provided **syllabi** and suitable reading material for **home-schooling.**
- Not all families were liberal: some **conservative Hindus** believed a **literate girl** would be **widowed,** while some **Muslims** feared educated women would be corrupted by **Urdu romances.**
- Some women defied bans: a girl in a conservative **Muslim family** secretly learned to read and write **Urdu** when told to read only the **Arabic Quran** she didn't understand.
- In **East Bengal,** early nineteenth century, **Rashsundari Debi** learned to read in the secrecy of her **kitchen;** she later wrote her autobiography **Amar Jiban (1876),** the first full-length **Bengali** autobiography.
- From the **1860s, Kailashbashini Debi** and a few **Bengali women** wrote about women's experiences: confinement at home, enforced ignorance, hard domestic labour, and **injustice** by those they served.
- In the **1880s** (present-day **Maharashtra**), **Tarabai Shinde** and **Pandita Ramabai** wrote with passionate anger about the **miserable lives** of **upper-caste Hindu women,** especially **widows.**
- A woman in a **Tamil novel** described reading as freedom within confinement: "my world is small … more than half my life's **happiness** has come from **books.**"
- While **Urdu, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi** print cultures developed early, **Hindi printing** grew seriously only from the **1870s,** soon focusing largely on **women's education.**
- In the **early twentieth century, women-oriented journals** (often written or edited by women) became popular, discussing **education, widowhood, widow remarriage,** and the **national movement,** plus **household, fashion,** short stories, and **serialised novels.**
- In **Punjab,** early twentieth century, **folk literature** for women was widely printed: **Ram Chaddha's Istri Dharm Vichar** taught women to be **obedient wives;** the **Khalsa Tract Society** issued cheap booklets with similar messages, often as **dialogues** about the virtues of a "good woman."
- In **Bengal, Battala** (central **Calcutta**) became a hub for **popular books:** cheap **religious tracts,** scriptures, and also literature deemed **obscene** or **scandalous.**
- By the **late nineteenth century,** many Battala books were richly **illustrated** with **woodcuts** and **coloured lithographs; pedlars** carried them to homes, allowing **women** to read in their **leisure time.**

## Print and the Poor People

- In nineteenth-century **Madras** towns, very **cheap small books** were sold at **crossroads,** so even **poor travelers** to markets could buy them.
- From the **early twentieth century, public libraries** were set up, mostly in **cities and towns** (and some prosperous villages), expanding access to books; for **rich local patrons,** founding a library brought **prestige.**
- From the **late nineteenth century, caste discrimination** became a major theme in **printed tracts and essays.**
- **Jyotiba Phule** (Maratha reformer) attacked caste injustices in **Gulamgiri (1871).**
- In the **twentieth century, B. R. Ambedkar** (Maharashtra) and **E. V. Ramaswamy Naicker (Periyar)** (Madras) wrote powerfully on **caste,** and their writings gained readers **across India.**
- **Local protest movements** and **sects** produced popular **journals** and **tracts** that **criticised ancient scriptures** and envisioned a **new, just future.**
- Factory **workers** were often **overworked** and had limited **education,** so they wrote little about their lives; exceptions became influential.
- **Kashibaba,** a **Kanpur millworker,** wrote and published **Chhote Aur Bade Ka Sawal (1938),** linking **caste** and **class exploitation.**
- Another **Kanpur millworker,** writing as **Sudarshan Chakr,** composed poems (1935–1955) later collected as **Sacchi Kavitayan.**
- By the **1930s, Bangalore cotton millworkers** established **libraries** to educate themselves, inspired by **Bombay** workers; **social reformers** sponsored these to curb **excessive drinking,** promote **literacy,** and sometimes **nationalism.**

## Print and Censorship

- Before **1798,** the East India Company showed little concern for **censorship,** and its early controls targeted critical **Englishmen in India** whose attacks on Company misrule could fuel opposition in **England** against its trade monopoly.
- By the **1820s,** the **Calcutta Supreme Court** issued regulations curbing **press freedom,** while the Company promoted newspapers that **celebrated British rule.**
- In **1835,** after petitions from editors of **English and vernacular** papers, Governor-General **Bentinck** agreed to revise press laws; **Thomas Macaulay** framed new rules that **restored earlier freedoms.**
- After the **1857 Revolt,** imperial attitudes hardened; angry Englishmen demanded a crackdown on the **'native' press,** and officials debated **stringent controls** as vernacular papers turned **nationalist.**
- The **Vernacular Press Act (1878),** modeled on **Irish Press Laws,** granted sweeping powers to **censor** vernacular reports and editorials, with surveillance across provinces.
- Under the Act, papers judged **seditious** received **warnings;** ignoring them could lead to **seizure of the press** and **confiscation** of printing machinery.
- Despite repression, **nationalist newspapers** multiplied nationwide, exposed **colonial misrule,** and encouraged **nationalist activities,** triggering militant protests and cycles of **persecution and resistance.**
- In **1907,** the deportation of **Punjab revolutionaries** spurred sympathy pieces by **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** in **Kesari;** he was **imprisoned in 1908,** prompting **widespread protests** across India.
