## Introduction

- We share Earth with millions of life forms from micro-organisms to **blue whales,** showing vast **biodiversity** (variety of life) across habitats.
- Humans are only one part of a complex **ecological system** and are dependent on it for survival (interlinked living and non-living).
- Plants, animals, and micro-organisms maintain the quality of **air, water,** and **soil** through processes like nutrient cycling and food webs.
- **Biodiversity** supports ecosystem stability, productivity, and resilience to stress (e.g., droughts), which sustains human life.
- **Forests** are primary producers (make food via photosynthesis) forming the base of **food chains/webs;** all higher organisms depend on them directly or indirectly.
- Loss of biodiversity weakens these natural services (clean air, safe water, fertile soil), risking human health and food security.
- Key idea for exams: Humans are interdependent with other organisms; conserving **forests** and biodiversity is essential for sustaining life-support systems.
- **Biodiversity (Biological Diversity) =** variety of life forms in nature, including wild species and **cultivated** (human-grown) species, across all habitats.
- It is rich in both form (shapes, sizes, structures) and function (roles like pollination, decomposition, nutrient cycling).
- All organisms are linked by a network of **interdependencies** (mutual dependence), forming a stable, balanced system.
- Interdependence examples: plants provide **oxygen** and food; animals aid **pollination** and seed dispersal; microbes drive **decomposition** and soil fertility.
- Key exam idea: Biodiversity is not just the number of species, but their **variety** and the **functional links** that keep ecosystems working.

## Flora and Fauna of India

- Many areas have unique (local) **endemic** plants and animals that occur only there.
- **India** is among the richest countries in biological diversity, with a very large variety of species across different climates and habitats.
- Actual diversity is higher than known; many species are still **undiscovered** (possibly 2–3 times more than recorded).
- Forest and wildlife resources support daily life: food, medicine, oxygen, clean water, fertile soil, and climate balance.
- These flora and fauna are deeply integrated into human routines, so their benefits are often **taken for granted.**
- Recently, biodiversity is under **stress** due to human **insensitivity** (careless use), leading to habitat loss, pollution, overuse, and species decline.
- Key exam idea: Protect local **endemic** species and habitats; sustainable use and awareness reduce stress on ecosystems.

## Conservation of Forest and Wildlife in India

- Rapid decline in wildlife and forests makes **conservation** essential to prevent ecosystem collapse.
- Need to conserve: protects **ecological diversity** and life-support systems — **water, air, soil** that sustain human life.
- Preserves **genetic diversity** (variety within species) for stronger breeds, disease resistance, and better **breeding** programs.
- Agriculture relies on **traditional crop varieties** for traits like drought/pest resistance; conserving wild relatives secures future food.
- **Fisheries** depend on healthy **aquatic biodiversity;** diverse species keep food webs stable and yields sustainable.
- 1960s–1970s: rising concern led to demand for a national wildlife protection programme.
- **Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972:** landmark law with provisions to protect **habitats,** control hunting/poaching, and regulate trade.
- Key exam idea: Conservation safeguards services (pollination, soil fertility, water purification) and future options (crop improvement, medicines).
- Practical outcomes: creation of **protected areas** (national parks, sanctuaries), species recovery plans, and stricter enforcement.
- Conclusion line: Conserving forests and wildlife secures **biodiversity,** stabilizes climate and resources, and supports sustainable livelihoods.
- An all-India list of **protected species** was issued to standardize legal protection across the country.
- Core measures: ban on **hunting/poaching,** legal protection of **habitats,** and restrictions on **wildlife trade** to save endangered populations.
- Governments (central and states) set up **national parks** and **wildlife sanctuaries** to secure critical habitats.
- Species-focused projects launched for gravely threatened animals: **tiger, one-horned rhinoceros, Kashmir stag (hangul), crocodiles** (freshwater, saltwater, gharial), **Asiatic lion,** etc.
- More recent full/partial legal protection given to: **Indian elephant, black buck (chinkara), great Indian bustard (godawan), snow leopard,** among others.
- Conservation focus has shifted from single-species protection to **biodiversity** as a whole, aiming to safeguard entire ecosystems and their interactions.
- Efforts now explore multiple **conservation measures** (habitat protection, corridors, community participation, legal safeguards) instead of limited approaches.
- Even **insects** are included in planning due to their roles in pollination, decomposition, and food webs, making ecosystems more resilient.
- Wildlife Act notifications in **1980** and **1986** expanded protection to hundreds of **butterflies, moths, beetles,** and one **dragonfly,** recognizing their ecological importance.
- In **1991,** plants were added to legal protection lists for the first time, beginning with **six species,** reflecting a broader, ecosystem-based conservation outlook.

## Project Tiger

- **Tiger** is a key predator in the **faunal web,** maintaining balance by controlling herbivore numbers and supporting healthy ecosystems.
- By **1973,** tiger numbers in India had fallen to **1,827** (from ~**55,000** around 1900), showing severe decline.
- Major threats: **poaching** for skins and bones (traditional medicines), **habitat loss/fragmentation, prey depletion,** and rising **human pressure.**
- India and **Nepal** host about **two-thirds** of the world's remaining tigers, making them prime targets for illegal hunting and trade.
- **Project Tiger (1973):** flagship conservation program to protect tigers and their **habitats;** aimed at curbing poaching, securing core areas, and restoring prey.
- Conservation goal: save the species and also preserve large **biotypes** (entire habitat types with full food webs and processes).
- Important tiger reserves: **Corbett (Uttarakhand), Sunderbans (West Bengal), Bandhavgarh (Madhya Pradesh), Sariska (Rajasthan), Manas (Assam), Periyar (Kerala).**

## Types and Distribution of Forest and Wildlife Resources

- Reserved Forests: More than half of total forest land; highest legal protection for conserving forest and wildlife resources; activities like grazing, hunting, and tree felling are generally prohibited unless specifically permitted.
- Protected Forests: About one-third of total forest area; declared by the Forest Department; forests are safeguarded from further **depletion** (reduction), with regulated use allowed under rules.
- Unclassed Forests: Other forests and **wastelands** under government, private, or community ownership; generally have the least formal protection and variable management.
- Reserved and protected forests together are called **permanent forest estates;** they are maintained to produce **timber** and other forest produce and for protective functions like soil and water conservation.
- **Madhya Pradesh** has the largest area under permanent forests, about **75%** of its total forest area.
- States with large shares of their forest area as **reserved forests: Jammu & Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Maharashtra.**
- States with a bulk of their forest area as **protected forests: Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, Rajasthan.**
- **Unclassed forests** are high in all **North-Eastern states** and parts of **Gujarat,** where many are managed by **local communities.**

## Community and Conservation

- Conservation is traditional in India; many **forest-dwelling communities** depend on forests for livelihood and protect them for long-term security.
- Community–government partnerships are crucial; in some places, locals lead protection and even restrict outside intervention to safeguard habitats.
- Example: In **Sariska Tiger Reserve (Rajasthan),** villagers opposed **mining** using the **Wildlife (Protection) Act** to defend ecosystems and rights.
- Example: Five villages in **Alwar (Rajasthan)** created the **Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'** over about **1,200 hectares,** with local rules banning hunting and blocking encroachment.
- **Chipko Movement (Himalayas):** villagers (notably women) protected trees by hugging them; it stopped deforestation and proved **community afforestation** with **indigenous species** works.
- Traditional conservation and **ecological farming** are being revived; they enhance soil, water, and biodiversity without heavy external inputs.
- **Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri)** and **Navdanya:** promoted **seed diversity,** organic methods, and showed diversified crops can be raised without **synthetic chemicals,** with viable incomes.
- **Joint Forest Management (JFM):** began formally in **1988** (first by **Odisha**); village institutions protect degraded forests with the forest department.
- JFM model: communities protect forests; in return they get **intermediate benefits** like **non-timber forest produce (NTFP)** and a share of **timber** from successful protection.
- Key lesson: involve **local communities** in natural resource management for both preventing destruction and enabling restoration.
- Policy direction: prioritize **people-centric, environment-friendly,** and **economically rewarding** development activities; put communities at the **centre** of decisions.

## Sacred Groves – A Wealth of Diverse and Rare Species

- **Nature worship:** ancient tribal belief that all creations of nature must be protected; led to strong community-based conservation.
- **Sacred groves:** untouched forest patches dedicated to deities; interference is **banned,** preserving **pristine** (original, undisturbed) ecosystems.
- Many communities protect specific **sacred trees** across generations, tying rituals to conservation.
- Examples (tribal traditions):
  - **Mundas** and **Santhals** (Chota Nagpur): worship **mahua (Bassia latifolia)** and **kadamba (Anthocaphalus cadamba).**
  - Tribals of **Odisha** and **Bihar:** worship **tamarind (Tamarindus indica)** and **mango (Mangifera indica)** during **weddings.**
  - Common sacred trees: **peepal** and **banyan.**
- Sacred places: springs, mountain peaks, plants, and animals given **sacred** status and strictly protected.
- Temple fauna: troops of **macaques** and **langurs** around temples are fed and treated as part of devotees, ensuring their protection.
- **Bishnoi** community (Rajasthan): protects **blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai,** and **peacocks;** animals freely roam villages without harm.
- Key idea: Cultural and religious practices act as effective, community-led **biodiversity conservation** systems.
