Detailed notes on Civics Chapter 4: why parties exist and what they do — functions, one-party to multiparty systems, India's major parties, and how parties can be reformed.

## Why Do We Need Political Parties?

- **Political parties** are among the most visible institutions in a **democracy.** For many ordinary citizens, democracy itself is understood as political parties.
- Even in remote areas, less-educated people may not know about the **Constitution** or government structure, but they are usually aware of political parties.
- Despite this visibility, political parties are often **criticised** and blamed for issues in democracy and public life.
- Parties are also linked to **social and political divisions,** which raises the question: *Do we really need political parties?*
- Around **100 years ago,** only a few countries had political parties, while today, almost all democracies have them.
- The reason for their spread lies in their role, so it is necessary to ask: *What are political parties and what do they do?*

Meaning of Political Parties:

- A **political party** is a group of people who come together to **contest elections** and **hold power** in government.
- They agree on certain **policies and programmes** for society, aiming to promote the **collective good.**
- Since there can be many views on what benefits society, parties try to **persuade people** that their ideas are better than those of others.
- By **winning elections** and gaining popular support, they seek to carry out these policies.
- Political parties represent **fundamental divisions** in society and are inherently **partisan,** meaning they stand for particular policies, interests, and sections of society.

Components of a Political Party:

- **Leaders** – guide the party and contest elections.
- **Active members** – participate regularly and help organise activities.
- **Followers** – ordinary supporters who align with the party's ideology and vote for it.

The main role of **political parties** is to **fill political offices** and exercise **political power.** They perform this role through several important **functions:**

## Functions of Political Parties

1. **Contesting Elections**
  - In most democracies, elections are mainly fought among candidates nominated by political parties.
  - Selection of candidates differs across countries:
  - In the **USA,** party members and supporters directly choose candidates.
  - In countries like **India,** top party leaders usually decide the candidates.
2. **Providing Policies and Programmes**
  - Parties present different **policies and programmes** before voters, allowing citizens to choose among alternatives.
  - Since people have many diverse opinions, parties **group similar views together,** reducing them into a few clear policy positions.
  - The **ruling party** is expected to frame government policies in line with the stance it presented to voters.
3. **Making Laws**
  - While laws are debated in the legislature, most members follow the **direction of party leadership,** not just their personal views.
  - In this way, parties have a decisive role in shaping laws.
4. **Forming and Running Governments**
  - Major policy decisions are taken by the **political executive,** which comes from political parties.
  - Parties **recruit leaders, train them, and appoint them as ministers** to run governments as per party priorities.
5. **Role of Opposition**
  - Parties that lose elections act as the **opposition,** voicing dissent, criticising wrong policies, and highlighting failures.
  - They also work to **mobilise resistance** against the ruling government when necessary.
6. **Shaping Public Opinion**
  - Parties raise and highlight important issues before society.
  - With **lakhs of members and activists** across the country, they spread awareness and influence people's thinking.
  - Many **pressure groups** are linked to political parties and act as their extensions.
  - Parties sometimes launch **movements** to solve people's problems, and public opinion often aligns with party positions.
7. **Connecting People to Government**
  - Parties provide ordinary citizens with **access to government machinery and welfare schemes.**
  - For many people, approaching a **local party leader** is easier than contacting a government officer.
  - This closeness creates a sense of connection, even if people do not fully **trust parties.**
  - To stay relevant, parties must respond to people's needs, or risk being **rejected in the next elections.**

## Necessity

- The functions of parties explain **why we need them,** but it also raises the question: **Why can't modern democracies exist without parties?**
- In a situation without parties:
  - Every election candidate would be **independent.**
  - No one could promise **major policy changes** or a clear programme to the people.
  - Even if a government was formed, its **stability and utility** would remain uncertain.
  - Representatives would only be accountable locally, not for the overall running of the country.
- Experiences from **panchayat elections** in some states show that even when parties do not formally contest, villages split into **factions,** each presenting a panel of candidates — essentially functioning like political parties.
- That is why **political parties exist everywhere** in the world — big or small countries, old or new, developed or developing.
- The rise of political parties is directly linked to the growth of **representative democracy.**
  - Large and complex societies need parties to **coordinate views** from different groups.
  - Parties bring representatives together to form a **responsible government.**
  - They act as mechanisms to **support or oppose governments,** shape policies, justify them, or criticise them.
- Thus, **political parties are a necessary condition for democracy.**

## How Many Parties Should We Have?

- In a democracy, any group of citizens can **form a political party.**
- India has more than **750 registered parties** with the **Election Commission,** though only a few are serious contenders in elections.
- This raises the question: *How many major parties are good for democracy?*

### One-Party System

- In some countries, only one party runs the government, called a **one-party system.**
- Example: **China,** where only the **Communist Party** is allowed to rule.
- Although people are legally free to form parties, **competition is not allowed,** making it **undemocratic.**

### Two-Party System

- In some countries, power alternates between **two main parties.**
- Smaller parties exist and can win seats, but only the main two can realistically form the government.
- Examples: **United States of America** and **United Kingdom.**

### Multiparty System

- When **several parties compete for power** and more than two have a reasonable chance of winning or forming a coalition, it is a **multiparty system.**
- India follows this system, where governments are usually formed through **alliances or fronts.**
- Example: In **2004 Parliamentary Elections,** three major alliances contested – **National Democratic Alliance (NDA), United Progressive Alliance (UPA),** and the **Left Front.**
- The system can seem **messy** and cause **political instability,** but it allows diverse **interests and opinions** to be represented.

### Choosing a Party System

- No country **chooses its party system.** It **evolves** over time, depending on its:
  - **Society** and divisions
  - **Regional diversity**
  - **History of politics**
  - **Electoral system**
- For example, India has a **multiparty system** because its vast **social and geographical diversity** cannot be represented adequately by just two or three parties.
- Therefore, there is no single "perfect" party system — each country develops one based on its **circumstances.**

## National Parties

- In **federal democracies,** political parties are usually of two types:
  - Parties active only in **one federal unit** (state).
  - Parties active in **several or all units** of the federation.
- In **India,** such parties exist as well:
  - **National parties** – country-wide presence, with units across states, following common policies, programmes, and strategies decided at the national level.
  - **State parties** – active mainly in one state or region.
- Every political party must **register with the Election Commission of India (ECI).**
- While all registered parties are treated equally, **large and established parties** receive:
  - A **unique election symbol** (can only be used by official candidates of the party).
  - Other special facilities.
- These parties are formally known as **recognised political parties.**

Criteria for Recognition by the Election Commission:

- **State Party:**
  - Must secure **at least 6% of total votes** in a State Legislative Assembly election.
  - Must win **at least 2 seats** in that Assembly.
- **National Party:**
  - Must secure **at least 6% of total votes** in elections to the **Lok Sabha** or Assembly elections in **4 States.**
  - Must win **at least 4 seats** in the **Lok Sabha.**
- As per **2023 ECI notification,** there are **six recognised national parties** in India.

## Major Parties

- **Aam Aadmi Party (AAP)**
  - Formed on **26 November 2012** after the 2011 anti-corruption movement.
  - Founded on **accountability, transparency, clean administration, and good governance.**
  - In **2013 Delhi elections,** became 2nd largest party and formed government with **Congress support.**
  - In **2022 Gujarat elections,** became a **third political front** in the state.
  - Currently governs **Delhi and Punjab.**
  - In **2019 Lok Sabha elections,** won **1 seat.**
- **Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP)**
  - Founded in **1984** by **Kanshi Ram.**
  - Represents **Bahujan Samaj** – Dalits, Adivasis, OBCs, and minorities.
  - Inspired by **Sahu Maharaj, Mahatma Phule, Periyar Ramaswami Naicker, and Ambedkar.**
  - Focus: securing **welfare and rights of oppressed groups.**
  - Strong base in **Uttar Pradesh;** presence in **Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand, Delhi, Punjab.**
  - Has formed **UP governments** several times with support of different parties.
  - In **2019 Lok Sabha elections,** got **3.63% votes** and **10 seats.**
- **Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP)**
  - Founded in **1980,** revival of **Bharatiya Jana Sangh (1951)** by **Syama Prasad Mukherjee.**
  - Ideology: **Integral Humanism** (Deendayal Upadhyaya), **Antyodaya,** and **Hindutva (cultural nationalism).**
  - Goals: **integration of J&K with India, uniform civil code, ban on religious conversions.**
  - Expanded support from **north, west, urban areas** in 1980s–90s to **south, east, northeast, rural areas** later.
  - Came to power in **1998** leading **NDA coalition.**
  - In **2019 Lok Sabha elections,** emerged largest with **303 seats.**
  - Currently leads NDA government at the Centre.
- **Communist Party of India (Marxist) – CPI(M)**
  - Founded in **1964.**
  - Believes in **Marxism-Leninism,** supports **socialism, secularism, democracy,** and opposes **imperialism and communalism.**
  - Accepts **democratic elections** as means for socio-economic justice.
  - Strong base: **West Bengal, Kerala, Tripura** among poor, workers, farmers, and intellectuals.
  - Critical of **economic liberalisation** allowing free foreign capital/goods.
  - Ruled **West Bengal** for **34 years** continuously.
  - In **2019 Lok Sabha elections,** got **1.75% votes** and **3 seats.**
- **Indian National Congress (INC)**
  - Founded in **1885,** among the **world's oldest parties.**
  - Played leading role in **freedom struggle** and post-independence politics.
  - Under **Nehru's leadership,** aimed to build a **modern, secular, democratic India.**
  - Ruling party at Centre till **1977,** then again **1980–1989.**
  - After 1989, support declined but maintained nationwide presence across social groups.
  - Ideology: **centrist** (neither leftist nor rightist), promotes **secularism, minority welfare,** and **social justice.**
  - Supports **economic reforms with a human face.**
  - Led the **UPA coalition government (2004–2019).**
  - In **2019 Lok Sabha elections,** won **19.5% votes** and **52 seats.**

## State Parties

- Apart from national parties, most major parties in India are recognized by the **Election Commission** as **State Parties,** commonly called **regional parties.**
- These parties are not always "regional" in outlook; some have **national organisations** but succeed mainly in certain states.
- Examples: **Samajwadi Party (SP)** and **Rashtriya Janata Dal (RJD)** have units in several states.
- Some parties openly focus on **state identity,** like **Biju Janata Dal (BJD), Sikkim Democratic Front (SDF), Mizo National Front (MNF),** and **Telangana Rashtra Samithi (TRS).**
- In the last **three decades,** the number and influence of state parties has grown significantly.
- This has made the **Indian Parliament** more politically diverse.
- Until **2014,** no single national party could secure a **majority in Lok Sabha** on its own.
- As a result, national parties are often required to form **alliances with state parties.**
- Since **1996,** almost every major state party has participated in one or more **coalition governments** at the national level.
- This trend has strengthened both **federalism** and **democracy** in India.

## Challenges to Political Parties

- **Political parties** are central to democracy, but they are often **criticised worldwide** for failing to perform effectively.
- In India too, people express dissatisfaction with parties, focusing on **four major challenges:**

1. **Lack of Internal Democracy**
  - Power is concentrated in the hands of **one or few top leaders.**
  - Parties do not maintain **membership registers,** hold **regular meetings,** or conduct **internal elections.**
  - Ordinary members lack proper access to information and influence over decisions.
  - **Loyalty to leaders** often becomes more important than loyalty to party principles.
  - Members who disagree with leadership find it hard to continue in the party.
2. **Dynastic Succession**
  - Linked to the lack of internal democracy.
  - Few opportunities for ordinary workers to **rise to leadership.**
  - Leadership positions often passed to **family members** of top leaders.
  - This creates **unfair advantages,** promotes inexperience, and undermines democratic values.
  - Present not only in India but also in **older democracies worldwide.**
3. **Role of Money and Muscle Power**
  - Elections see growing influence of **wealth and force.**
  - Parties nominate candidates who can **raise large amounts of money.**
  - **Rich people and big companies** funding parties gain influence over decisions.
  - Some parties even support **criminal candidates** capable of winning elections.
  - Democracies across the world are concerned about the rising control of **rich groups** in politics.
4. **Lack of Meaningful Choice**
  - For healthy democracy, parties should offer **distinct alternatives,** but differences have reduced.
  - In many countries, ideological gaps between parties (e.g., **Labour and Conservative in Britain**) are minimal.
  - In India too, differences among parties on **economic policies** have narrowed.
  - Voters seeking **different policies** often have no option.
  - Same set of leaders sometimes **shift between parties,** reducing meaningful choice of leadership.

## How Can Parties Be Reformed?

- **Political parties** need reforms to face the challenges of democracy, but the big question is whether they are really **willing to reform.**
- In democracies, final decisions are made by **party leaders,** and people can only replace them with **other party leaders.** If none want change, reforms are difficult to force.

Recent Efforts and Steps in India:

- **Anti-defection Law:** The Constitution was amended to stop **MLAs/MPs from changing parties** for power or money. If they switch parties, they lose their seat. This reduced defections but also made **dissent harder,** as members must follow party leaders strictly.
- **Supreme Court Orders:** Made it **mandatory for candidates** to file affidavits giving details of property and criminal cases. This increased public information, but no checks are in place to ensure the truth of the details. So, the actual **decline in money and criminal influence is uncertain.**
- **Election Commission Orders:** Political parties must now hold **organisational elections** and file **income tax returns.** While parties follow this, it is often treated as a **formality,** with little effect on internal democracy.

Suggested Reforms:

- Pass a **law to regulate internal party affairs,** requiring:
  - Proper membership registers.
  - Following the party's constitution.
  - **Independent authority** to resolve disputes.
  - **Open elections** for top leadership posts.
- **Reserve one-third tickets for women** candidates and ensure **women quotas in decision-making bodies.**
- Introduce **state funding of elections,** either in kind (petrol, paper, facilities) or in cash based on the **votes secured** in the last election.

Challenges to Reform:

- These reforms are not yet accepted by political parties.
- Too much **legal control** may backfire, as parties might **find loopholes** to break the rules.
- Parties rarely pass laws **they dislike,** which makes legal reform difficult.

Other Ways to Reform:

1. **Public pressure** – through petitions, protests, media, and people's movements. If parties fear losing public support, they may accept reforms.
2. **Citizen participation** – those who demand reforms must also **join parties** and influence them from within. Simply criticising from outside is not enough.
- Ultimately, the **quality of democracy** depends on **public participation.**
- The solution to bad politics is not withdrawal, but **more and better politics.**
