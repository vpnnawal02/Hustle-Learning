Detailed notes on Civics Chapter 2: what federalism means — Belgium's shift, India's three lists, language policy, centre–state relations and panchayati raj.

## What is Federalism

- In **1993,** Belgium transferred key powers from the Central Government to regional governments by giving them constitutional authority that the centre cannot revoke, turning Belgium from a unitary to a **federal** state.
- Earlier regional bodies existed, but their powers were delegated and could be withdrawn; after 1993, regions gained independent, guaranteed powers, making decentralisation durable and legally protected.
- In a federal system, power is divided between a national authority and constituent units (provinces/states), each level operating independently within its own domain to handle national matters and day-to-day regional administration.
- Belgium's shift ensured clearer division of subjects, direct accountability of regional governments, and stronger accommodation of linguistic and cultural diversity through autonomy.
- By contrast, **Sri Lanka** largely remains a unitary system where the national government holds most powers; Tamil leaders have sought federalism to secure regional autonomy and protect linguistic-cultural rights.
- Typically, federations have two levels — national and state/provincial — with further devolution possible to municipalities and panchayats; each level's powers are constitutionally defined, preventing unilateral takeover by another level.
- In a **unitary** system, there is a single central authority; any provincial or local bodies are subordinate and can be directed or overruled by the centre, including having their powers granted or withdrawn by it.
- In a **federal** system, the central and state governments each have constitutionally defined powers; the centre cannot order a state to act in its domain, and each level governs independently within its sphere.
- Accountability differs: in a unitary setup, sub-units answer upward to the central government; in a federal setup, both the central and state governments are separately answerable to the people through their own elected institutions.
- Practical effect: unitary systems enable uniform policy and centralized command, while federal systems protect regional autonomy and diversity by preventing any one level from exercising unlimited authority over the other.
- There are **two or more levels** (tiers) of government. Different tiers govern the same citizens, but each tier has its own **jurisdiction** over specific subjects of legislation, taxation, and administration.
- The **constitution specifies** the jurisdictions of each tier, so the existence and authority of every level is constitutionally guaranteed.
- **Fundamental provisions** of the constitution cannot be changed unilaterally by one level; changes need consent of both levels.
- **Courts** interpret the constitution and the powers of different levels; the highest court acts as an **umpire** in disputes between levels.
- **Sources of revenue** for each level are clearly specified to secure financial autonomy.
- Dual objectives: promote **unity** while accommodating **regional diversity;** this needs agreed rules of power-sharing and **mutual trust** to live together.
- Federations distribute power differently based on how and why they were formed; historical context shapes whether the centre or the states hold greater authority.
- Route 1 — **coming together:** Independent states voluntarily unite to pool sovereignty, retain their identity, and enhance security; examples include the **USA, Switzerland,** and **Australia,** where states are usually strong and enjoy equal powers vis-à-vis the federal government.
- Route 2 — **holding together:** A large country internally divides power between the national government and constituent states to manage diversity; examples include **India, Spain,** and **Belgium,** where the central government is generally more powerful than the states.
- Power balance variation: In "coming together" models, constituent units typically have equal status and high autonomy; in "holding together," units may have different levels of autonomy and the centre often retains key levers.
- Unequal powers: Constituent units can have **unequal powers,** with some granted **special powers** due to history, security, culture, or geographic needs.
- Core idea: The exact central–state balance is not fixed across federations; it reflects each federation's origins, compromises, and the need to blend unity with diversity.
- Net effect: Both models aim to increase stability and representation — either by strengthening states within a union or by carefully devolving authority in a large, diverse nation.

## What Makes India a Federal Country

- India is a "Union of States" that functions on federal principles, even though the Constitution doesn't use the word federation; this structure was shaped by Independence, Partition, and the integration of princely states.
- The Constitution began with two tiers — the **Union (Central) Government** and State Governments — and later added a third tier through Panchayats and Municipalities to strengthen local self-government.
- Each tier has its own jurisdiction like a typical federation; powers are constitutionally defined and protected so that autonomy at every level is real and enforceable.
- **Union List:** Subjects of national importance like defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications, and currency; kept here to ensure a uniform, countrywide policy; only the Union Government can make laws on these subjects.
- **State List:** Subjects of state and local importance like police, trade and commerce within the state, agriculture, and irrigation; only State Governments can make laws on these subjects.
- **Concurrent List:** Subjects of common interest like education, forests, trade unions, marriage, adoption, and succession; both Union and State Governments can legislate, but if there is a conflict, the Union law will prevail.
- Residuary subjects: Matters not listed in the three lists, including new areas like computer software, fall under the Union's law-making power; Parliament can legislate on these to ensure uniform regulation for emerging issues.
- Unequal powers across states: In "holding together" federations, not all states have identical authority; some receive a **special status** due to unique social and historical contexts.
- Special provisions: States like **Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh, and Mizoram** enjoy special protections (e.g., land rights of indigenous peoples, culture, and preferential public employment); non-permanent residents generally cannot buy land or houses in such states.
- Union Territories: Small or unique regions such as **Chandigarh, Lakshadweep,** and **Delhi** are Union Territories with limited powers compared to states; the Central Government exercises special administrative authority over them.
- Basic structure protection: The Union–State power-sharing is fundamental to the Constitution; Parliament cannot alter it alone. Any change requires passage by both Houses with a **two-thirds majority** and ratification by **at least half of the state legislatures.**
- Judicial umpire: High Courts and the **Supreme Court** interpret the Constitution and settle disputes over the division of powers, ensuring that federal boundaries are respected in practice.
- Financial powers: Both Union and State governments can raise resources through **taxation,** funding their respective responsibilities and ensuring functional autonomy at each level.

## How is Federalism Practised

- Constitutional provisions are necessary but not enough for federalism to succeed.
- India's federal success is linked to **democratic politics,** not just to written rules.
- Democratic practice helped make the spirit of federalism, **respect for diversity,** and the desire to **live together** shared ideals.
- The text sets up a look at the major ways this happened in practice.

## Linguistic States

- The creation of linguistic States was the first big test of democratic politics; comparing India's map from **1947 to 2019** shows many changes in state areas, boundaries, and names.
- After **1947,** several old state boundaries were altered so that people speaking the same language could live within the same State, aligning administration with linguistic identities.
- Not all new States were based on language; some were formed to recognise cultural, ethnic, or geographic differences, such as **Nagaland, Uttarakhand,** and **Jharkhand.**
- When language-based reorganisation was demanded, some national leaders feared it might break the country; the Central Government initially resisted creating linguistic States.
- Experience showed the opposite: linguistic States made the country more **united** and made **administration** easier by aligning governance with people's everyday language and identity.

## Language Policy

- The Constitution did not assign any one language as a national language; **Hindi** was identified as the official language, but only about **40%** speak it as a mother tongue, so safeguards were created for other languages.
- Besides Hindi, there are **22 Scheduled Languages;** candidates for Central Government exams can choose any of these languages, and States conduct most work in their own official language.
- Leaders adopted a cautious approach to spreading Hindi; English was supposed to stop in **1965,** but non-Hindi-speaking States demanded continuation, and in **Tamil Nadu** the movement turned violent.
- The Centre agreed to continue English along with Hindi for official purposes; some critics argue this benefited the English-speaking elite.
- Promotion of Hindi remains official policy, but it does not allow imposing Hindi on States with different languages; this flexibility helped avoid a situation like **Sri Lanka.**

## Centre-State Relations

- Restructuring Centre–State relations strengthened federalism in practice by ensuring that constitutional power-sharing was respected in day-to-day politics, not just on paper.
- For a long period, the same party ruled at the Centre and in most States, so States often did not assert their rights as autonomous federal units, which diluted genuine federal functioning.
- When a State government belonged to a rival party, the Centre at times tried to undermine State autonomy, including misusing constitutional provisions to dismiss elected State governments, which harmed the federal spirit.
- After **1990,** regional political parties rose across many States, changing power dynamics and making it harder for the Centre to dominate States unilaterally.
- The era of coalition governments began at the Centre, since no single party had a clear majority in the Lok Sabha; national parties formed alliances with regional parties to govern, which encouraged negotiation and accommodation.
- This coalition culture fostered a new habit of power sharing and greater respect for the autonomy of State Governments in practice.
- A major Supreme Court judgment made it difficult for the Centre to arbitrarily dismiss State governments, reinforcing constitutional boundaries and protecting elected State mandates.
- Together, these shifts made federal power sharing more effective in practice than in the early years after the Constitution came into force, aligning political behavior with federal principles.

## Decentralisation in India

- India's size and diversity make two tiers insufficient; many States are as large as countries and are internally diverse, so power must also be shared within States to address local needs efficiently.
- A third tier called **local government** emerged from the idea of **decentralisation,** where power is shifted from Central and State governments to local bodies for decisions best taken close to the people.
- Decentralisation means local communities, who know their area's problems best, can decide how to spend money and manage services more effectively, improving responsiveness and accountability.
- Local government enables direct public participation in decisions, building the habit of democratic involvement and fulfilling the principle of **local self-government.**
- The Constitution recognised the need for decentralisation; Panchayats in villages and Municipalities in towns were set up in all States to bring government closer to citizens.
- Initially, these local bodies were controlled by State governments; elections were irregular, and they lacked their own powers and resources, so actual decentralisation remained very limited.
- The rationale is clear: bringing authority to the grassroots improves service delivery, reflects local priorities, and strengthens democracy, but without regular elections, real powers, and finances, local bodies cannot function effectively.
- In **1992,** the Constitution was amended to strengthen the third tier of democracy and make decentralisation real and effective.
- Regular elections: It became constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections for all local government bodies (Panchayats and Municipalities).
- Social justice reservations: Seats in local bodies and posts of executive heads are reserved for **Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes,** and **Other Backward Classes** to ensure fair representation.
- Women's representation: At least **one-third** of all seats and leadership positions are reserved for **women,** expanding participation and leadership at the grassroots.
- Independent conduct of polls: Every State now has a **State Election Commission** to independently conduct Panchayat and Municipal elections.
- Devolution of powers and funds: State governments must share certain **powers** and **revenue** with local bodies; the exact extent and subjects of devolution can differ from State to State.
- Rural local government is called **panchayati raj;** each village or group of villages has a gram panchayat made up of ward members (panch) and a president (sarpanch), all directly elected by adult residents of the ward or village.
- The gram panchayat is the main decision-making body for the village and functions under the supervision of the gram sabha, which includes all voters of the village and meets at least two or three times a year to approve the annual budget and review work.
- Above the village level, several gram panchayats are grouped into a panchayat samiti (also called block or mandal); its members are elected by all panchayat members in that area, creating a representative body at the block level.
- At the district level, all panchayat samitis or mandals together form the zilla (district) parishad; most members are elected, and MPs (Lok Sabha), MLAs, and certain district officials are also members.
- The zilla parishad chairperson is the political head of the zilla parishad and leads district-level rural local governance and coordination.
- Urban local bodies exist for towns and cities; smaller urban areas have municipalities, while big cities are governed by municipal corporations.
- Both municipalities and municipal corporations are run by elected bodies made up of people's representatives to ensure local self-governance.
- The municipal chairperson is the political head of a municipality and oversees its functioning and decisions.
- In a municipal corporation, the political head is called the mayor, who leads the corporation's elected body and represents the city's civic administration.
- This local government system is the largest democratic experiment in the world, with about **36 lakh** elected representatives in panchayats and municipalities across India.
- The scale of representation is massive — this number is larger than the population of many countries, showing how deep participation goes at the grassroots.
- Giving local bodies constitutional status has strengthened democracy by bringing decision-making closer to people and making institutions more accountable.
- Women's participation has risen significantly due to reservations, increasing their **representation** and **voice** in local governance.
- Challenges remain: while elections happen regularly and enthusiastically, **gram sabhas** are often not held as required, weakening direct participation and oversight.
- Most State governments have not devolved enough **powers** to local bodies, limiting their authority to make and implement decisions effectively.
- Financial constraints persist because adequate **resources** are not shared with local governments, restricting service delivery and development work.
- Due to these gaps, the country is still far from fully achieving the ideal of **self-government** at the local level.
