## Conquest, Disease and Trade

- In the **sixteenth century,** the world seemed to "shrink" when European sailors found a **sea route to Asia** and crossed the Atlantic to **America,** creating faster, wider connections than before.
- Long before this, the **Indian Ocean** was a busy trade zone where goods, people, ideas, and customs mixed, with the **Indian subcontinent** serving as a central hub in these networks.
- European entry **expanded or redirected** many of these trade flows toward **Europe,** increasing its influence over global exchange.
- Before its 'discovery', **America** had little regular contact with the Old World; from the sixteenth century, its **vast lands, crops, and minerals** began transforming global trade and everyday life.
- Precious metals, especially **silver** from **Peru** and **Mexico,** greatly increased **Europe's wealth** and helped pay for its **trade with Asia;** myths like **El Dorado** inspired many expeditions.
- **Spanish and Portuguese** conquest in America advanced rapidly by the **mid-sixteenth century,** not only due to weapons but also because of **diseases like smallpox** that devastated Indigenous peoples who lacked immunity.
- **Smallpox** spread ahead of Europeans, wiping out communities and easing conquest; **guns** could be resisted, but **disease** could not, especially as Europeans were mostly immune.
- Up to the **nineteenth century, Europe** faced poverty, hunger, crowding, and disease; religious conflicts pushed many to **migrate to America,** where **enslaved Africans** worked on **cotton and sugar plantations** for European markets.
- Until late in the **eighteenth century, China** and **India** remained among the **richest** and led **Asian trade,** but **China's turn toward isolation** and the rise of the **Americas** gradually shifted the world trade centre **westwards to Europe.**

## The Nineteenth Century (1815-1914)

- The nineteenth century saw deep changes as **economic, political, social, cultural, and technological** forces worked together to transform societies and their relations with the wider world.
- Economists describe three major international **flows** that defined this era:
  - The flow of **trade** (mainly goods like cloth and wheat).
  - The flow of **labour** (people migrating to find jobs).
  - The flow of **capital** (money moving across borders for short- or long-term investment).
- These three flows were **closely connected** and influenced people's lives more deeply than before, shaping work, markets, and living conditions across continents.
- Sometimes the links broke unevenly — for instance, **labour migration** could be more **restricted** than the movement of goods or money due to laws and controls.
- Studying these three flows **together** gives a clearer picture of how the nineteenth-century **world economy** worked and why changes in one flow affected the others.

## A World Economy Takes Shape

- In industrial Europe, changing **food production and consumption** reshaped life; in nineteenth-century **Britain,** aiming for food self-sufficiency meant high prices, lower living standards, and social conflict.
- Rapid **population growth** from the late eighteenth century and expanding cities and industries raised demand for grain, pushing **food prices up.**
- Under pressure from landowners, the government restricted **corn (grain) imports** through the **Corn Laws,** keeping prices high; industrialists and urban people opposed this and pushed for repeal.
- After the **Corn Laws were abolished,** food could be **imported more cheaply** than producing it at home, making British farming uncompetitive; large areas lay **uncultivated,** and many agricultural workers lost jobs.
- Displaced rural workers **moved to cities or emigrated overseas;** as **food prices fell,** people in Britain consumed more, and rising **industrial incomes** from the mid-nineteenth century further boosted **food imports.**
- To meet British demand, regions in **Eastern Europe, Russia, America, and Australia** cleared land and expanded farming; this required **railways, new harbours, settlements,** and large amounts of **capital and labour.**
- **Capital** flowed from financial centres like **London,** while labour shortages in **America** and **Australia** led to mass **migration;** about **50 million** Europeans moved to America and Australia, and roughly **150 million** people worldwide migrated in search of better lives.
- By **1890,** a **global agricultural economy** existed: food came from thousands of miles away, grown by **hired farm workers** on large, newly created farms, moved by **railways** and **steamships** staffed by low-paid crews from southern Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Caribbean.
- A similar though smaller shift happened in **west Punjab,** where the British Indian government built **irrigation canals** to turn semi-desert into fertile **Canal Colonies** for **wheat and cotton** exports, settled by peasants from elsewhere in Punjab.
- The same pattern extended beyond food: **cotton** cultivation expanded globally to supply British mills, and **rubber** production grew; between **1820 and 1914, world trade multiplied 25–40 times,** with about **60%** being **primary products** like wheat, cotton, and coal.

## Role of Technology

- Technology like **railways, steamships,** and the **telegraph** was crucial to the nineteenth-century transformation, enabling faster movement of goods, people, and information across long distances.
- These advances were driven by wider **social, political, and economic** forces; for example, **colonisation** pushed heavy investment into transport — faster rail lines, lighter wagons, and bigger ships — to move food cheaply from distant farms to markets.
- The meat trade shows this link well: before the **1870s,** animals were shipped live from **America to Europe,** which wasted ship space and led to deaths, illness, and weight loss, making meat a costly luxury.
- The breakthrough came with **refrigerated ships,** which allowed slaughter at source (in **America, Australia, New Zealand**) and shipment of **frozen meat** to Europe, cutting costs and prices.
- Cheaper meat widened diets for Europe's poor — beyond just **bread and potatoes,** many could now add **meat, butter, and eggs** — improving living conditions.
- Better diets and living standards supported **social peace** at home and helped sustain public backing for **imperialism** abroad.

## Late nineteenth-century Colonialism

- The late nineteenth century saw booming **trade** and expanding **markets,** but this growth also had a darker side for many regions tied into the world economy.
- Integration often meant a **loss of freedoms and livelihoods** in colonised areas, as economic, social, and ecological disruptions accompanied tighter global links.
- European **conquests** in this period imposed harsh changes that forcibly drew colonised societies into global trade on unequal terms.
- In **Africa,** many borders were drawn as straight lines — reflecting the arbitrary division by European powers rather than local histories or communities.
- In **1885,** major European powers met in **Berlin** to carve up Africa, formalising spheres of control without African consent.
- **Britain** and **France** greatly expanded their empires; **Belgium** and **Germany** emerged as new colonial rulers; the **US** became a colonial power in the late **1890s** by taking former **Spanish** colonies.
- This expansion often brought **coercive labour, resource extraction,** and **environmental damage,** reshaping colonised economies to serve imperial interests.

## Rinderpest, or the Cattle Plague

- In the **1890s,** the cattle plague **Rinderpest** swept across Africa, devastating herds and crippling local economies and livelihoods.
- This crisis highlights how **European imperialism** reshaped colonised societies, as even a cattle disease could alter people's lives and their ties to the wider world.
- Historically, Africa had **abundant land** and **low population;** land and livestock sustained livelihoods, so most people did not seek **wage work.**
- With few desirable **consumer goods,** there was little incentive to earn wages; those owning land and herds saw no need to work on plantations or in mines.
- Europeans were drawn by Africa's **land and minerals,** aiming to set up **plantations and mines** to supply export markets in Europe.
- A key challenge was a **shortage of wage labour;** to force recruitment, rulers imposed **heavy taxes** payable only through wage work.
- **Inheritance laws** were changed so only one heir kept land, pushing others into the **labour market** and breaking traditional access to land.
- **Mineworkers** were confined in **compounds,** restricted from free movement, tightening employer control over labour.
- **Rinderpest** entered via infected cattle from **British Asia** for Italian troops in **Eritrea** in the late **1880s,** then spread west "like a forest fire."
- By **1892,** it reached the **Atlantic coast,** and five years later the **Cape,** killing about **90%** of cattle and collapsing pastoral economies.
- The loss of herds destroyed livelihoods; **planters, mine owners, and colonial governments** seized control of the remaining cattle to **monopolise power.**
- This control over scarce cattle resources allowed European colonisers to **force Africans into wage labour** and further **subdue** the continent.
- Similar patterns of coercion, dispossession, and economic restructuring under **Western conquest** appeared elsewhere in the **nineteenth century.**

## Indentured Labour Migration from India

- The story of **indentured labour migration** from India shows the two-sided nineteenth century: rapid **economic growth** and **technology** on one side, but **misery** and new forms of **coercion** on the other.
- Hundreds of thousands of Indian and Chinese workers were sent to **plantations, mines, roads, and railways** worldwide under **contracts** promising return passage after **five years** of work.
- Most Indian indentured workers came from **eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, central India,** and the dry districts of **Tamil Nadu,** where **cottage industries declined, rents rose,** and land was cleared for **mines and plantations,** pushing the poor into **debt** and migration.
- Main destinations included the **Caribbean** (Trinidad, Guyana, Surinam), **Mauritius, Fiji;** nearer destinations were **Ceylon (Sri Lanka), Malaya (Malaysia),** and **Assam** for tea plantations.
- Recruitment was handled by **agents** paid commissions; many migrants joined to escape **poverty** and **oppression,** but agents often **misled** them about journeys, destinations, and work — sometimes even **abducting** unwilling people.
- Conditions on plantations were harsh, with **few legal rights,** leading many to call indenture a **"new system of slavery";** some escaped and faced punishment, while others coped by creating **new cultural expressions.**
- In **Trinidad,** the **Muharram** procession became a vibrant carnival called **Hosay,** open to people of all backgrounds; **Rastafarianism** reflects Caribbean cultural links with Indian migrants; **Chutney music** in Trinidad and Guyana blends Indian and local styles.
- Many workers **stayed on** after contracts, or returned briefly to India and then settled again, creating large **diasporic Indian communities;** figures like **V.S. Naipaul** and cricketers **Shivnarine Chanderpaul** and **Ramnaresh Sarwan** trace roots to this migration.
- From the **1900s,** Indian nationalists condemned indenture as **abusive** and **cruel;** the system was **abolished in 1921,** yet descendants often remained an **uneasy minority** in Caribbean societies, a theme echoed in Naipaul's early works.

## Indian Entrepreneurs Abroad

- Growing for the world market needed **capital;** big plantations borrowed from banks, but small peasants relied on regional financier networks.
- Indian banker-groups like the **Shikaripuri Shroffs** and **Nattukottai Chettiars** funded **export agriculture** across **Central** and **Southeast Asia,** using their own funds and also money sourced from European banks.
- They built sophisticated **long-distance money transfer** systems and even developed indigenous forms of **corporate organisation,** enabling reliable credit and settlement across regions.
- Indian **traders and moneylenders** accompanied European expansion into **Africa,** extending credit and commerce alongside imperial supply chains.
- **Hyderabadi Sindhi** merchants went further, setting up global **emporia** from the **1860s** in major port cities, selling local and imported **curios** to a growing tourist market.
- Their success was boosted by safer, comfortable **passenger vessels,** which increased travel and created steady demand for port-based retail and exchange.

## Indian Trade, Colonialism and the Global System

- Indian **fine cottons** once dominated exports to Europe, but with British **industrialisation,** local mill owners lobbied for protection; Britain imposed **tariffs** on cloth imports, sharply cutting the inflow of Indian textiles.
- Shut out of Britain, Indian textiles then faced **tough competition** in other markets; India's cotton textile share in exports fell from about **30% (c. 1800)** to **15% (1815)** and to **below 3% by the 1870s.**
- As manufactured exports declined, **raw material** exports surged: **raw cotton** rose from about **5% (1812)** to **35% (1871); indigo** for dyeing remained key for decades, and **opium** to China became India's single largest export for a time after the **1820s.**
- Britain cultivated **opium in India,** sold it in **China,** and used the earnings to pay for **tea** and other Chinese imports, creating a triangular flow that tied Indian production to global trade finance.
- Over the nineteenth century, British **manufactures flooded** Indian markets, while India exported **food grains** and **raw materials;** however, British exports to India exceeded imports from India, creating a British **trade surplus** with India.
- Britain used this surplus to settle **deficits** with other countries through a **multilateral settlement system,** effectively using India's surplus to balance its wider global accounts.
- The surplus from India also funded **'home charges':** remittances by British officials and traders, **interest** on India's external debt, and **pensions** for British officials, underscoring India's pivotal role in the late nineteenth-century world economy.

## The Inter-war Economy

- The **First World War (1914–1918)** was fought mainly in **Europe,** but its consequences spread across the globe, affecting economies, politics, and societies far beyond the battlefields.
- The war triggered a prolonged **global crisis** that stretched through the first half of the twentieth century, taking more than **three decades** to overcome fully.
- In its aftermath, the world faced deep **economic instability** (debt burdens, inflation, unemployment, disrupted trade) and **political turmoil** (regime changes, social unrest, rising extremism).
- These unresolved strains contributed to the conditions that led to a second, even more **catastrophic war** later in the century.

## Wartime Transformations

- The First World War was fought between two blocs: the **Allies** (Britain, France, Russia, later the US) and the **Central Powers** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Turkey), beginning in **August 1914** and lasting over four years despite early expectations of a quick end.
- It was the first **modern industrial war,** with leading industrial nations using **machine guns, tanks, aircraft, and chemical weapons** at scale, made possible by large-scale industry.
- The war required moving **millions of soldiers** from around the world to the frontlines using **ships and trains,** reflecting unprecedented logistical mobilisation.
- The human cost was immense: about **9 million dead** and **20 million injured,** losses unimaginable before the industrial age; most were **working-age men,** shrinking Europe's labour force.
- With fewer earners, many **household incomes fell** after the war, intensifying social and economic strain.
- During the conflict, industries were **restructured** to produce armaments and supplies, and societies were reorganised for total war: as men fought, **women entered jobs** previously done only by men.
- The war broke key economic links among major powers that were now enemies, forcing them to seek new ways to finance massive costs.
- To fund its war effort, **Britain borrowed heavily** from private banks and the public in the **United States,** shifting global financial balances.
- As a result, the **United States moved from debtor to creditor status,** meaning by war's end Americans owned more assets abroad than foreigners owned in the US.

## Post-war Recovery

- After the war, recovery was hard; **Britain** — the pre-war leading economy — entered a prolonged crisis and could not easily regain dominance.
- While Britain fought, **industries grew in India and Japan,** so after 1918 Britain struggled both in the **Indian market** and against **Japanese competition** globally.
- To fund the war, Britain **borrowed heavily from the US,** leaving it with large **external debts** at the war's end.
- The war created a temporary **boom** (high demand, production, employment); when it ended, **production fell** and **unemployment rose,** as the state cut inflated wartime spending.
- By **1921,** about **one in five British workers** was unemployed, making job anxiety a persistent feature of the post-war years.
- Global agriculture also faced crisis: with **Eastern Europe's** wheat back after the war, the earlier expansion in **Canada, America, and Australia** led to a **glut,** pushing **grain prices down.**
- Falling prices meant **lower rural incomes** and **rising farm debt,** trapping many agricultural producers in prolonged distress.

## Rise of Mass Production and Consumption

- The US recovered faster after the war, returning to strong growth in the early 1920s, with the decade defined by the rise of mass production and consumer markets.
- A key driver was Henry Ford's adaptation of the slaughterhouse conveyor into the car factory assembly line in Detroit, making production faster and cheaper by having workers repeat single tasks at a belt-driven pace.
- The T-Model Ford became the first mass-produced car, rolling off the line roughly every three minutes, far outpacing earlier manufacturing methods.
- Assembly-line pressure caused high worker turnover, so Ford doubled the daily wage to $5 in January 1914 and barred trade unions, later calling the wage hike his best cost-cutting decision because output rose as he sped up the line.
- "Fordist" practices spread across the US and Europe in the 1920s, cutting costs and prices so more workers could afford durable goods like cars, refrigerators, washing machines, radios, and gramophones.
- US car output climbed from about 2 million in 1919 to over 5 million in 1929; purchases surged via "hire purchase" (consumer credit with weekly or monthly instalments).
- A housing boom, financed by loans, amplified demand for household appliances, creating a cycle: higher investment → more jobs and incomes → more consumption → more investment.
- By 1923, the US was exporting capital again and became the leading overseas lender, helping European recovery and lifting world trade and incomes for several years.
- The prosperity was fragile, and in 1929 the world entered a severe depression that ended the 1920s boom.

## The Great Depression

- The **Great Depression (c. 1929–mid-1930s)** caused steep global declines in production, jobs, incomes, and trade, with timing and intensity differing by country; overall, **agricultural regions suffered most** because farm prices fell more, and for longer, than industrial prices.
- Causes were multiple and interconnected: the post-war world economy was fragile; persistent **agricultural overproduction** met falling prices, so farmers produced even more to keep incomes up, worsening the **glut** and pushing prices down further as produce rotted unsold.
- In the mid-1920s many countries relied on **US loans;** when US lenders pulled back rapidly (overseas loans dropping from over $1 billion in early 1928 to about a quarter a year later), borrower countries faced acute crises.
- The loan withdrawal triggered bank failures and currency collapses in **Europe** (including pressure on the British pound) and deepened commodity price slumps in **Latin America** and other raw-material exporters; US **tariff hikes** during the downturn further damaged world trade.
- The **US** itself was hit hardest among industrial nations: banks cut domestic lending and called in loans, farms couldn't sell harvests, households defaulted and lost homes, cars, and appliances; unemployment surged and businesses failed.
- Systemic breakdown followed: by **1933,** over **4,000 US banks** had closed, and between **1929 and 1932** about **110,000 companies** collapsed, wiping out much of the consumer prosperity of the 1920s.
- By **1935,** recovery had begun in most industrial countries, but the Depression's deeper effects on **society, politics, international relations,** and public psychology lasted far longer.

## India and the Great Depression

- The Depression showed how **tightly linked** the early twentieth-century global economy had become, as shocks in one region quickly spread and disrupted lives and markets elsewhere.
- Colonial India, then a major **exporter of farm goods** and **importer of manufactures,** was hit immediately: between **1928 and 1934,** India's exports and imports nearly **halved.**
- As world prices crashed, Indian prices fell too; **wheat prices** in India dropped by about **50% (1928–1934),** severely hurting rural producers.
- Peasants suffered more than urban residents; despite collapsing crop prices, the colonial state did **not reduce revenue demands,** intensifying rural distress.
- **Jute** growers in Bengal were among the worst hit: when **gunny bag** exports collapsed, the price of **raw jute** fell by over **60%,** trapping indebted peasants even deeper as incomes evaporated.
- Across rural India, indebtedness rose; families **used savings, mortgaged land,** and **sold jewellery and precious metals** to survive, turning India into an **exporter of gold** during the crisis years.
- Economist **J. M. Keynes** argued India's gold exports aided global recovery and sped **Britain's** rebound, but they **did not relieve** Indian peasants' suffering.
- Rural anger was high when **Mahatma Gandhi** launched the **Civil Disobedience Movement** in **1931,** right amid the Depression's peak effects.
- Urban India fared comparatively better: with **falling prices,** people on **fixed incomes** (rent-receiving landowners, salaried middle class) saw their purchasing power rise.
- Industrial investment picked up as the government, under **nationalist pressure,** extended **tariff protection** to domestic industries, cushioning some urban sectors.

## Rebuilding a World Economy: The Post-war Era

- The **Second World War** began just two decades after the First, pitting the **Axis** (Nazi Germany, Japan, Italy) against the **Allies** (Britain, France, the Soviet Union, the US) in a six-year conflict fought on land, sea, and in the air across multiple regions.
- The human cost was vast: at least about **60 million deaths** (roughly **3%** of the 1939 world population), with many more injured and a majority of deaths occurring **outside battlefields,** as civilians suffered from bombings, sieges, famine, and displacement.
- Large parts of **Europe and Asia** were devastated; numerous cities were destroyed by **aerial bombardment** and relentless **artillery,** causing deep **economic damage** and **social disruption** that made post-war **reconstruction** long and difficult.
- Two powers shaped the post-war order: the **United States,** emerging as the dominant economic, political, and military force in the Western world; and the **Soviet Union,** which made massive sacrifices to defeat Nazi Germany and transformed from a largely agricultural society into a **world power** during the years when capitalist economies struggled in the **Great Depression.**

## Post-war Settlement and the Bretton Woods Institutions

- Two core lessons: a mass-production economy needs broad-based, stable **mass consumption** supported by high, steady incomes and thus near-full employment; markets alone cannot ensure this, so governments must stabilise prices, output, and jobs through active intervention.
- Full employment also depends on managing external economic ties: governments need authority over international flows of **goods, capital, and labour** so external shocks don't undermine domestic stability.
- To secure post-war stability and employment, nations agreed in July 1944 at **Bretton Woods (New Hampshire, USA)** on a coordinated monetary-financial framework.
- That agreement created the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** to help members handle external surpluses/deficits and the **International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank)** to finance reconstruction; together they're called the Bretton Woods institutions/twins.
- The system is often called the **Bretton Woods system;** both institutions began operations in **1947,** with decision power concentrated in Western industrial countries and the **US** holding an effective veto over key decisions.
- The monetary design used **fixed exchange rates:** national currencies (e.g., the **Indian rupee**) were pegged to the **US dollar,** and the dollar was anchored to **gold at $35 per ounce,** providing predictable currency values for trade and investment.

## The Early Post-war Years

- The Bretton Woods system ushered in strong, stable growth for Western industrial nations and **Japan,** with world trade rising at over **8% per year (1950–1970)** and average incomes growing nearly **5% per year,** alongside low volatility.
- Unemployment stayed relatively low, averaging **under 5%** in most industrial economies for much of this period, reflecting broad, steady expansion.
- These decades also featured rapid global diffusion of **technology** and business practices, as innovations spread across borders through trade, investment, and learning-by-doing.
- Many developing countries accelerated efforts to **catch up,** channeling large **capital investments** into importing modern **industrial plants and equipment,** aiming to raise productivity and industrial capacity.

## Decolonisation and Independence

- After the Second World War, most of **Asia and Africa** achieved independence over two decades, but new nations faced deep **poverty,** scarce **resources,** and structural handicaps from prolonged **colonial rule.**
- The **IMF** and **World Bank** were originally built to serve the needs of **industrial countries,** not to tackle poverty and underdevelopment in former colonies.
- As **Europe** and **Japan** rebuilt and needed less support, the Bretton Woods institutions shifted focus toward **developing countries** from the late **1950s.**
- A paradox emerged: newly independent states seeking rapid development now took guidance from **international agencies** dominated by the same former colonial powers.
- Even years after **decolonisation,** former empires retained control over key **resources** (minerals, land) in their old colonies, and powerful foreign corporations (e.g., from the **US**) often secured **cheap extraction rights.**
- Most developing countries did not share in the rapid **1950s–1960s** Western growth, prompting them to form the **Group of 77 (G-77).**
- The G-77 demanded a **New International Economic Order (NIEO)** to secure: real control over **natural resources,** greater **development assistance, fairer prices** for raw materials, and better **market access** for their manufactured goods in rich-country markets.

## End of Bretton Woods and the Beginning of 'Globalisation'

- From the 1960s, the rising costs of overseas commitments weakened **US finances** and competitiveness, eroding global confidence in the **US dollar,** which could no longer hold its **gold parity;** this precipitated the end of **fixed exchange rates** and the move to **floating exchange rates.**
- By the mid-1970s, developing countries increasingly borrowed from **commercial banks and private lenders** rather than international agencies, triggering periodic **debt crises,** falling **incomes,** and rising **poverty,** especially in **Africa** and **Latin America.**
- Industrial countries were also hit: **unemployment** rose from the mid-1970s and stayed high into the early 1990s, reflecting stagflation and structural adjustments.
- From the late 1970s, **MNCs** shifted production to **low-wage Asian** economies; after policy shifts in **China** and the collapse of the **Soviet Union** and Eastern European communism, many countries re-entered global markets.
- Low **wages** in China made it a prime destination for foreign investment, explaining why many **consumer goods** (TVs, phones, toys) were manufactured there as part of a **low-cost** production structure.
- This relocation of industry boosted **world trade** and **capital flows;** over recent decades, the world's economic map has been reshaped by rapid transformation in **India, China, and Brazil.**
