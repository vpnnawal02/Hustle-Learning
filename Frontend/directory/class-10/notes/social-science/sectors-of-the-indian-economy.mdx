Detailed notes on Economics Chapter 2: how the economy is divided into sectors — the three sectors, GDP and GVA, disguised unemployment, job creation, and organised versus unorganised work.

## Introduction

### Primary Sector

- Primary sector definition: Activities that directly exploit natural resources to produce raw materials, such as agriculture (crops, dairy), fishing, forestry, mining, and quarrying. These outputs (cotton, milk, timber, ores) come primarily from nature.
- Why it's called "primary": It forms the base of the economy. Raw materials from this sector are the essential inputs that the secondary sector (manufacturing) transforms and the tertiary sector (services) supports.
- Dependence on natural factors: Production relies heavily on rainfall, climate, sunshine, soil quality, water availability, and biological processes (e.g., animal health and fodder for dairy). Yields and risks are closely tied to environmental variability.
- Examples:
  - Agriculture: Cotton cultivation within a crop season; harvest depends on monsoon and climate.
  - Dairy: Milk output depends on animal biology, feed/fodder, and veterinary care.
  - Fisheries/Forestry: Harvesting fish and timber from natural stocks, requiring sustainable management.
  - Mining/Ores: Extraction of minerals and fuels from the earth.
- Alternative name: Often called "agriculture and allied activities" because a large share of natural products in many economies comes from farming, dairy, fishing, and forestry.
- Role in employment and livelihoods: A major source of jobs, especially in rural areas; supports food security and provides incomes for farmers, fishers, and forest-dependent communities.
- Linkages to other sectors: Supplies raw cotton to textiles, milk to processing, timber to construction, and ores to metals — driving industrial activity and trade.
- Key challenges: Weather shocks, price volatility, resource depletion (overfishing, deforestation, groundwater decline), and the need for sustainable practices to maintain long-term productivity.

### Secondary Sector

- Secondary sector (industrial): Transforms natural products from the primary sector into new goods through manufacturing and processing in factories, workshops, or homes (e.g., cotton → yarn → cloth; sugarcane → sugar/gur; clay → bricks; steel, cement, electronics, automobiles).
- Why it's "secondary": It is the next step after extraction; it adds value to raw materials, creating intermediate and final goods that power industrial growth, jobs, and urbanization.
- Typical activities: Manufacturing, construction, food processing, textiles and garments, chemicals, oil refining, pharmaceuticals, electronics assembly, and metal fabrication.

### Tertiary Sector

- Tertiary sector (services): Provides services that support production and distribution in primary and secondary sectors and serve households directly; it does not produce tangible goods but enables the entire economy to function smoothly.
- Core services: Transport and logistics (trucks, railways, shipping), storage and warehousing (godowns, cold chains), communication (telephony, internet, postal), banking and finance (loans, payments, insurance), trade (wholesale, retail, e-commerce), and business services (IT, design, marketing).
- Why it's called "service sector": Outputs are services — intangible activities that facilitate production, exchange, and consumption rather than physical goods.
- Interdependence of sectors: Primary supplies raw materials; secondary adds value by transforming them; tertiary moves, stores, finances, and sells the outputs and also provides essential coordination and information services.
- Illustrative flow: Cotton grown (primary) → spun and woven into cloth (secondary) → transported, insured, financed, and retailed (tertiary); sugarcane → sugar (secondary) → distributed via wholesalers/retailers (tertiary).
- Economic significance: Balanced growth needs all three — robust primary for resources and food security, dynamic secondary for higher productivity and jobs, and efficient tertiary for connectivity, finance, and market access.
- Home, workshop, and factory scales: Manufacturing can occur from household/cottage industries to MSMEs to large-scale plants; services similarly range from local shops and carriers to national banks and digital platforms.

### Comparing the Three Sectors

- Total output: Count only final goods and services from each sector, add their values to estimate total production; avoid double-counting intermediates used to make final outputs.
- Sector dominance: In many economies, services lead total production, manufacturing is second, and agriculture is smaller but still vital for food and raw materials.
- Employment patterns: Primary often employs many workers with lower productivity; secondary employs fewer with higher productivity; tertiary's job share rises as economies develop.
- Interdependence: Primary supplies raw materials, secondary adds value via manufacturing, tertiary enables transport, storage, finance, communication, and trade.
- Measuring size: Use Gross Value Added (GVA) or sectoral GDP shares to compare production; use workforce shares to see where people are employed.
- Development trend: As incomes grow, demand for services (health, education, logistics, IT, finance, retail) expands, raising the tertiary sector's share.
- Policy focus: Boost productivity in primary (irrigation, seeds, storage), deepen secondary (infrastructure, skills, MSME support), and upgrade tertiary (digital, logistics, finance) for balanced growth.
- Quality of jobs: Track not just job counts but job security, wages, and skills to understand real employment outcomes across sectors.

### How Do We Count the Various Goods and Services and Know the Total Production in Each Sector?

- Economists add up production using monetary value, not physical units, so unlike "cars + computers," values are comparable across all goods and services.
- Calculate sectoral output by summing the market value of goods and services produced in that sector during a period; do this for all three sectors to compare sizes.
- Use prices to convert quantities into values: e.g., 10,000 kg wheat × ₹20/kg = ₹2,00,000; 5,000 coconuts × ₹15 = ₹75,000; then add values across items within a sector.
- Count only final goods and services to avoid double counting; final goods are purchased by end users and already embed the value of intermediate inputs used to make them.
- Intermediate goods (like wheat and flour used to make biscuits) are excluded because their value is included in the final product's price; adding them separately would count the same value multiple times.
- Example chain: Farmer sells wheat to mill; mill sells flour to biscuit maker; biscuits sold to consumers are the final good. Only the biscuits' value is counted in output/GDP, not wheat and flour separately.
- National output measure: Summing the value of all final goods and services produced within the country over a period yields GDP; sectoral totals sum to overall GDP via value-added or final-use approaches.
- Precaution in practice: Ensure services (education, healthcare, logistics) are included as final services when they are the end product, and exclude intermediate business-to-business services that are inputs to other outputs to prevent overstatement.
- The value of **final goods and services** in each sector for a year gives that sector's total production.
- Adding sectoral production values gives **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** of the country.
- **GDP = value of all final goods and services produced within the country in a year.**
- **GDP** indicates the size of the economy (how big it is).
- In India, a central government ministry compiles GDP by collecting data on quantities and prices from all **states and union territories.**
- Only final goods/services are counted to avoid double counting; intermediates are already included in final values.
- Complex term simplified: "final goods" = goods bought by end users; "intermediate goods" = inputs used to make final goods.
- The government now reports sectoral contribution using **Gross Value Added (GVA)** instead of only GDP to align with global practice.
- **GVA = contribution of sectors after adjusting for taxes and subsidies** (simplified: it shows what each sector actually adds in value).
- You will study the detailed **GDP–GVA** difference later; here the focus is how output in **primary, secondary,** and **tertiary** sectors has changed over time.
- Shift pattern: share of **primary** (agriculture & allied) declines, **secondary** (industry) rises with industrialization, and **tertiary** (services) becomes the largest as economies mature.
- Reading change: look at sector-wise GVA to see which sector is **dominant** in production and how its share and growth evolve across years.

## Historical Change in Sectors

- In early development, the **primary sector** (agriculture, related activities) dominates production and employment.
- As farming methods improve, agriculture produces surplus food, freeing people for non-farm work like crafts and trade.
- With surplus, buying–selling expands; new roles emerge: traders, transporters, administrators, and army.
- Over time, **manufacturing** grows with factories and new methods; many workers move from farms to factories.
- The **secondary sector** becomes most important in production and jobs as factory-made goods become cheaper.
- In the last **100 years,** developed countries shifted further to the **tertiary (service) sector,** now largest in total production and employment.
- General pattern: Primary → Secondary → Tertiary dominance as economies mature.
- Question for India: compare total production and employment across the three sectors to see if a similar shift has occurred.

## Rising Importance of the Tertiary Sector in Production

- Between **1977–78** and **2017–18,** output grew in all sectors, but the **tertiary (service) sector** grew the most and became the largest producer by **2017–18.**
- The service sector is crucial because a country needs basic services — **hospitals, schools, post & telecom, police, courts, local administration, defence, transport, banks, insurance** — and the government must provide or enable them in a developing economy.
- Growth in **agriculture** and **industry** raises demand for services like **transport, trade, storage, communication, finance,** which support production and distribution.
- As primary and secondary sectors expand, linked services scale up, making the tertiary sector increasingly important.
- Basic services improve welfare and productivity, while support services reduce costs and connect producers to markets, further boosting the economy.
- Between **1977–78** and **2017–18,** output rose in all sectors, but the **tertiary sector** grew the fastest and became the largest producer.
- Rising incomes increase demand for services like eating out, tourism, shopping, **private hospitals, private schools,** and professional training.
- New ICT-based services (IT, BPO, digital platforms, telecom) expanded rapidly in the last decade, boosting the service sector.
- Basic services — **hospitals, schools,** post & telecom, police, courts, local administration, **defence, transport, banks, insurance** — are essential; the government ensures these in a developing economy.
- Growth of **agriculture** and **industry** raises demand for transport, trade, storage, communication, and finance that support production and distribution.
- The service sector is uneven: a small, high-skill segment (IT, finance, professional services) grows fast, while many low-skill service workers (small shops, repairs, transport) earn little due to limited alternatives.
- Only part of services is driving growth; employment quality varies, with high-wage modern services alongside low-wage informal services.

## Where Are Most of the People Employed?

- Changing **GVA** shares across sectors over forty years; services rise, agriculture falls, industry grows modestly.
- Remarkable point: employment shares did not shift as much as GVA shares; **primary sector** still the largest employer in **2017–18.**
- Reason: insufficient job creation in non-farm sectors; output grew faster than jobs due to higher **productivity** and automation.
- Industry: output rose **9x,** employment only about **3x;** services: output rose **14x,** employment about **5x.**
- Result: structural transformation in production without matching employment shift, leading to persistent dependence on low-productivity farm jobs.
- Implication: need policies that boost **labor-intensive** manufacturing and modern services, and improve skills to absorb workers moving out of agriculture.
- More than half the workers are in the **primary sector** (mainly agriculture) but it produces only about **one-sixth of GVA,** showing a big productivity gap.
- Secondary and tertiary sectors produce the remaining majority of output while employing **less than half** the workforce, indicating higher productivity per worker outside agriculture.
- This does not mean farm workers are lazy; it means there are **more people than necessary** on farms relative to available land, tech, and capital.
- If a few agricultural workers moved to other sectors, farm output would largely stay the same — this situation is called **underemployment** (too many workers sharing too little work).
- The core issue is lack of enough **non-farm jobs** to absorb surplus rural labor; solutions include labor-intensive manufacturing, modern services, rural enterprises, and skilling.
- Simple meaning of underemployment: people are **working but below their potential** (too few hours, low productivity tasks, or many people doing a job that needs fewer).
- Example of **underemployment:** Small farmer **Laxmi** with ~2 hectares, rain-fed, grows jowar/arhar; all 5 family members work year-round because there's no other work, but tasks are too few for everyone to be fully used (working below potential).
- This is **disguised unemployment:** people appear employed, yet removing some workers does not reduce output; the extra labour adds little or no additional production.
- Why it's "hidden": no one is openly jobless, but work is split into very small parts; each person is not **fully employed** (not used to full capacity).
- Proof via movement: If landlord **Sukhram** hires 1–2 family members, farm output on Laxmi's small plot stays the same, but family income rises from wages.
- Alternative shift: If 2 members take factory jobs, total family earnings rise and the farm still produces as before — showing surplus labour on the farm.
- Simple meaning (simplified): **Disguised unemployment** = too many people doing a job that needs fewer; some workers' contribution is near zero, so output doesn't fall if they leave.
- Many small farmers like **Laxmi** face **underemployment:** too many family members work on small plots, so some workers add little to output.
- Moving some workers out of agriculture to other jobs would not reduce farm production but would raise total family income.
- **Disguised unemployment** also appears in urban services: casual workers (painters, plumbers, repairers) don't get work daily and earn irregularly.
- Street vendors and cart-pushers may work all day yet earn very little; they do this due to lack of **better opportunities.**
- Core idea: people are working but **below their potential;** shifting them to productive non-farm jobs can boost overall output and incomes.

## How to Create More Employment?

- Problem: **underemployment** on small, rain-fed farms like Laxmi's two-hectare plot; not enough productive work year-round.
- Solution 1 (farm-level): Public spending or bank loans to build a well for **irrigation** so she can grow a second **rabi** crop (e.g., wheat).
- Employment impact: 1 hectare of wheat can employ 2 people for ~50 days (sowing, watering, fertiliser, harvesting), absorbing surplus family labour.
- Solution 2 (area-level): Build a **dam** and **canals** to irrigate many farms, multiplying cropping and on-farm tasks.
- Outcome: More farm jobs within agriculture, higher family income, and reduced **underemployment** without lowering output.
- Simplified terms: **Rabi** (winter crop season); **underemployment** (working below potential due to too little productive work).
- Higher production creates a need to **sell surplus;** farmers must transport crops to towns and markets.
- Public investment in **rural roads, transport,** and **storage** lets mini-trucks reach villages and reduces crop losses.
- Better infrastructure generates jobs beyond farms in **transport** and **trade,** raising rural incomes.
- Farmers also need **seeds, fertilisers, equipment,** and **pumpsets;** poor farmers cannot afford these upfront.
- Borrowing from moneylenders means **high interest;** this traps farmers in debt and delays inputs.
- Access to **cheap credit** from local banks at reasonable interest enables timely input purchase and better yields.
- Key idea: combine **irrigation** with **transport/storage** and **affordable credit** to boost production, sales, and rural employment.
- Create jobs in **semi-rural areas** by setting up local industries and services near where people live.
- Example: If farmers grow **arhar/chickpea,** a local **dal mill** can buy, process, and sell to cities, adding value and jobs.
- Build **cold storages** so farmers can store **potatoes, onions** and sell later at better prices, reducing distress sales.
- Near forests, open **honey collection centres** so farmers can sell **wild honey,** adding non-farm income.
- Set up **food processing** units for **potato, sweet potato, rice, wheat, tomato, fruits** to supply outside markets.
- Benefit: More **local employment,** higher farmer incomes, less migration to big cities, and stronger rural value chains.
- About **60%** of India's population is aged **5–29 years;** only about **51%** of them attend educational institutions.
- Many under **18 years** are at home or working as **child labourers** due to lack of access/opportunity.
- Getting all children into school needs more **buildings, teachers,** and support **staff.**
- An erstwhile **Planning Commission (NITI Aayog)** study estimates about **20 lakh** jobs can be created in the **education sector.**
- Improving health requires more **doctors, nurses,** and **health workers,** especially in **rural areas.**
- Investing in education and health both **creates jobs** and advances core goals of **human development** (better health, learning, and well-being).
- Every state/region can raise income and jobs via **tourism, regional crafts,** or **IT/services,** with planning and government support.
- A Planning Commission study estimated improving **tourism** could create over **35 lakh** additional jobs each year.
- Long-term ideas need time; short-term relief is also necessary for immediate employment.
- The government enacted the **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 (MGNREGA 2005),** a legal **Right to Work** in about **625 districts.**
- Under **MGNREGA,** rural adults willing to work are guaranteed **100 days** of wage employment per year; if work isn't provided, they get **unemployment allowance.**
- Priority is given to works that raise **land productivity** (e.g., water conservation, irrigation assets), so today's jobs also boost future output.

## Division of Sectors as Organised and Unorganised

- Classification by employment conditions: activities are grouped into **organised** and **unorganised** sectors based on job regularity, legal registration, and compliance with labour laws.
- **Organised sector:** Government-registered enterprises with **regular employment,** written terms, and formal procedures; they must follow laws like **Factories Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Gratuity Act, Shops and Establishments Act.**
- Assured work and protections: Employees typically get **fixed wages,** defined hours, paid leave, **PF** (provident fund), gratuity, and safer working conditions due to enforceable rules.
- Formal compliance: Firms maintain records, pay taxes, and are accountable to inspections; processes are documented, making employment terms **predictable** and enforceable.
- Self-employed within organised: Some work on their own but are still "organised" if they **register** with the government and comply with applicable rules.
- Key idea simplified: **Organised sector** = registered, rule-bound, and rights-based jobs with stability; **unorganised sector** = irregular, often unregistered work with limited security.
- Workers in the **organised sector** have job **security** and **fixed working hours;** extra hours must be paid as **overtime.**
- They receive **paid leave, holiday wages, provident fund (PF),** and **gratuity** as part of statutory benefits.
- They are entitled to **medical benefits;** employers must ensure **drinking water, safety,** and a healthy work environment under labour laws.
- On **retirement,** many organised-sector workers receive **pensions,** providing income security in old age.
- Simple meanings: **overtime** (extra hours paid at a higher rate), **provident fund** (long-term savings with employer contribution), **gratuity** (lump-sum for long service), **pension** (regular post-retirement income).
- **Unorganised sector:** small, scattered units mostly outside effective government control; rules exist but are often not followed.
- Jobs are **low-paid** and **irregular;** no fixed hours, written contracts, or job security.
- No **overtime, paid leave,** holidays, or **sick leave;** workers can be removed anytime, especially in slack seasons.
- Conditions depend on the employer's **whims** (personal decisions), leading to unstable incomes.
- Includes many **self-employed** doing small jobs: street vending, repairs, casual construction; farmers often hire labour only when needed.
- Simple terms: **irregular** (not steady), **self-employed** (work for themselves), **casual work** (short-term, on-call jobs).

## How to Protect Workers in the Unorganised Sector

- Organised jobs are most desired but have grown **slowly,** so they can't absorb all job seekers.
- Some formal firms shift activities into the **unorganised** space to evade **taxes** and bypass labour laws, weakening worker protections.
- Result: many workers end up in unorganised jobs with **low pay, irregular earnings,** no benefits, and **no job security.**
- Since the **1990s,** layoffs and restructuring pushed many from organised to unorganised work, lowering income stability.
- Need two things: create **more decent work** and provide **protection/support** for unorganised workers (fair wages, social security, safer conditions).
- Vulnerable in rural areas: **landless labourers, small and marginal farmers** (nearly **80%** of rural households), **sharecroppers,** and **artisans** (weavers, blacksmiths, carpenters, goldsmiths).
- Support needed for small/marginal farmers: timely **seeds, inputs, credit, storage,** and **marketing** outlets.
- Vulnerable in urban areas: workers in **small-scale industry,** casual workers in **construction, trade, transport,** and **street vendors, head-load workers, garment makers, rag pickers.**
- Small-scale industry needs help with **raw material procurement** and **marketing** of output.
- **Casual workers** in both rural and urban areas require protection due to irregular work and low pay.
- Many from **scheduled castes, scheduled tribes,** and **backward communities** are concentrated in the unorganised sector and face **social discrimination** along with economic insecurity.
- Why protect them: essential for **economic** (stable incomes, productivity) and **social development** (reduce inequality, discrimination).

## Sectors in Terms of Ownership: Public and Private Sectors

- Classification by ownership: **public sector** (government owns assets, delivers services) vs **private sector** (individuals/companies own assets, deliver services).
- **Public sector** examples: **Railways, Post Office;** goal is public welfare, not just profit; funded by **taxes** and other public revenues.
- **Private sector** examples: **TISCO** (Tata Iron and Steel Company Limited), **RIL** (Reliance Industries Limited); guided by **profit motive** and charge users for services.
- Why governments spend: to provide essential services, ensure **equity,** build infrastructure, correct market failures, and support long-term development.
- Simple meanings: **assets** (valuable resources owned), **delivery of services** (providing public utilities like transport, mail), **profit motive** (aim to earn profit), **taxes** (money collected by government to fund services).
- Some needs are best provided by the **public sector** because private providers would charge very high prices or can't feasibly bill every user (collection is hard).
- Examples needing huge upfront spending: **roads, bridges, railways, harbours, power generation, irrigation (dams, canals).**
- Government ensures universal access by funding these through **taxes** and providing them at **reasonable cost.**
- Some activities need **support/subsidy** so production continues; e.g., selling **electricity** only at cost would make many small industries unviable.
- Government may **bear part of the cost** and supply electricity at affordable rates to prevent shutdowns and protect jobs.
- Simple terms: **public goods** (services meant for all, hard to exclude users), **subsidy** (government pays part of the cost so prices stay low), **infrastructure** (basic facilities that help the economy run).
- Government procures **wheat** and **rice** from farmers at a **fair price** and sells to consumers at lower prices via **ration shops;** government bears part of the cost.
- This system supports both **farmers** (assured price) and **consumers** (affordable food).
- Government has primary responsibility to fund essential **public services** like **health** and **education** for all.
- Running proper schools and ensuring quality **elementary education** is a core duty; India still has a large **illiterate** population.
- Nearly half of India's children are **malnourished,** and a quarter are **critically ill;** improving child health is urgent.
- **Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)** is high in states like **Odisha (36)** and **Madhya Pradesh (43),** worse than some of the poorest regions.
- Key human development needs: **safe drinking water, housing for the poor, food and nutrition.**
- Government must increase spending in the **poorest and ignored regions** to reduce inequality and improve well-being.
- Simplified terms: **fair price** (price that protects farmer income), **ration shops** (government outlets selling subsidised food), **illiterate** (cannot read/write), **malnourished** (not getting enough nutrients), **IMR** (babies dying before age one per 1,000 births).
