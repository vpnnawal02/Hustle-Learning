## What is Globalisation

- Over the past few decades, MNCs have increasingly located production in countries where costs are lower, leading to rising foreign investment and rapid growth in cross-border trade.
- MNCs often control a large share of global trade by coordinating multi-country production networks and sourcing components worldwide.
- Example: Ford Motors' India plant produces for the domestic market, exports fully built cars to multiple developing countries, and ships components to Ford factories globally, integrating India into its global supply chain.
- This model spreads design, manufacturing, and assembly across locations to exploit scale, proximity to markets, and cost advantages, while deepening trade in both goods and services.
- Globalisation is the rapid integration or interconnection of countries driven by rising foreign trade, foreign investment, and cross-border movement of technology and services.
- Greater foreign investment and trade have integrated production and markets across countries, linking distant locations into unified supply chains.
- MNCs play a central role by spreading production across nations, investing abroad, and coordinating large shares of international trade in goods and services.
- The world's regions are now in much closer contact than a few decades ago due to these flows and the technologies that enable them.
- Besides trade, investment, services and technology, countries are linked by the movement of people for jobs, income, and education.
- Unlike goods and capital, cross-border labour mobility remains far more restricted by immigration controls, security concerns, and political pressures.
- Migration is a core dimension of globalisation, but its growth has been uneven: destination countries have diversified, while policies have often tightened, limiting large increases in people flows relative to trade and capital.
- Bottom line: people movement connects countries, yet its expansion has lagged compared with other global flows due to persistent restrictions.

## Factors That Have Enabled Globalisation

### Technology

- Technology has accelerated globalisation by slashing the time and cost of moving goods and information across borders.

Transport advances:

- Containerization, larger and fuel-efficient cargo ships, wide-body jets, and optimized logistics networks have made long-distance shipping faster, more reliable, and cheaper per unit, enabling global supply chains.
- Better cold-chain, warehousing, and multimodal links (port–rail–road) cut spoilage and delays, letting firms source and sell worldwide at competitive prices.

Communication and IT:

- The internet, mobile networks, satellites, fiber optics, cloud computing, and real-time platforms allow instant coordination of design, procurement, production, and sales across countries.
- Digital tools reduce transaction costs (search, contracting, monitoring), making it feasible for even small firms to participate in cross-border trade and services.

Production technology:

- Automation, CAD/CAM, robotics, and advanced analytics standardize quality across locations, letting firms split production stages internationally while maintaining consistency.
- As scale economies rise and costs fall, firms can locate stages where factor costs and capabilities best fit, deepening cross-border integration.

Net effect:

- Faster, cheaper transport plus instant communication has made "produce here, assemble there, sell everywhere" routine, driving rapid integration of production and markets across countries.
- Rapid advances in information and communication technology (ICT) have made global connections instant, cheap, and reliable, accelerating globalization across work, commerce, education, and daily life.

Telecommunications:

- Mobile phones, broadband, and undersea fiber plus satellite links enable instant voice, text, and video from virtually anywhere, shrinking coordination costs across borders.
- From telegraph and landlines to smartphones and 5G, each leap reduced delays and expanded access, letting firms manage global teams and supply chains in real time.

Computers and the internet:

- Cheap computing power and cloud services let even small enterprises store, process, and share data globally, collaborate online, and deploy software at scale.
- The web enables instantaneous information search, e-mail, messaging, and voice/video calls at near-zero marginal cost, opening global markets and knowledge networks.

Digital platforms:

- E-commerce, digital payments, remote work suites, and social media match buyers, sellers, talent, and ideas worldwide, lowering market entry barriers.
- APIs and platforms (logistics, fintech, SaaS) standardize cross-border operations, making it easier to sell services and digital goods internationally.

Remote access from anywhere:

- Satellite communication extends connectivity to remote regions for education, telemedicine, disaster response, and e-government, integrating previously isolated areas.
- Low-cost devices plus vernacular apps broaden participation, though digital divide challenges remain.

Bottom line:

- ICT collapses distance for communication and coordination, so information moves instantly and cheaply; this is why computers, the internet, and modern telecom are central to today's tightly interconnected global economy.

## Liberalisation of Foreign Trade and Foreign Investment Policy

- A tax on imported toys is a trade barrier that raises the landed cost of foreign toys; importers pay the tax, and consumers face higher prices, so demand for imported toys falls.
- With imported toys less price-competitive, domestic toy makers gain market share, output, and potentially employment; this is the protective effect of a tariff.
- Governments use trade barriers (tariffs, quotas, quality controls) to regulate what, how much, and at what price foreign goods enter the country, thereby influencing domestic industry outcomes.
- Policy trade-offs: while tariffs can nurture local industry and address safety/quality concerns, they also raise consumer prices and can provoke retaliation; complementary measures (quality standards, cluster support, easier compliance) often determine long-run competitiveness.
- In short, taxing Chinese toy imports would likely reduce those imports, raise their prices in Indian markets, and help Indian toy producers expand, illustrating how trade barriers shape foreign trade volumes and product mix.
- After Independence, India imposed trade and investment barriers to protect nascent domestic industries from foreign competition during their formative decades. Industries in the 1950s–60s were just emerging; unrestricted imports could have stifled their growth, so India restricted most imports and prioritized only essential goods like machinery, fertilizers, and petroleum.
- This approach aligned with import-substitution industrialization used by many countries in early development phases, where protection helps build capacity, conserve scarce foreign exchange, and foster self-reliance until firms can compete internationally.
- Policy instruments included high tariffs, quotas, and import licensing that favored essential capital goods while limiting consumer goods; over time, as capabilities improved, later reforms eased these restrictions to increase competition and efficiency.
- Bottom line: early protection was intended to nurture domestic producers and strategic sectors; liberalization came later when policymakers judged industry more prepared to face global competition.
- Beginning in 1991, India undertook liberalisation: the government reduced barriers on foreign trade and investment, dismantled many industrial licenses, and let businesses decide more freely what to import/export, with far fewer restrictions than before.

Why liberalisation happened:

- Policymakers judged Indian producers ready to face global competition and believed competition would raise quality, efficiency, and consumer choice.
- International institutions supported these reforms, and easing controls aimed to attract capital, technology, and integrate India with global markets.

What changed:

- Trade barriers were lowered, making it easier to import and export goods and services.
- Foreign companies were allowed to set up factories and offices in India, bringing investment and technology.
- Many prior permissions under the "license" system were removed, giving firms freedom to expand, diversify, and respond to market demand.

What "liberalisation" means:

- Removing or relaxing government-imposed restrictions so markets allocate resources more freely.
- In trade, it means businesses can decide what and how much to import/export with minimal prior approvals, reflecting a more "liberal" policy stance.

Expected effects:

- Greater competition pushing domestic firms to improve quality and productivity.
- Increased flows of goods, services, capital, and technology, deepening global integration.
- More choices for consumers and more opportunities for producers to scale and export.
