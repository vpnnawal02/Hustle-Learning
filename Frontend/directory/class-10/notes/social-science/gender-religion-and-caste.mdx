Detailed notes on Civics Chapter 3: how gender, religion and caste enter politics — women's representation, communalism versus secularism, and the two-way street between caste and politics.

## Gender and Politics

- **Gender division** is a form of hierarchical social division.
- It exists everywhere but is rarely studied in politics.
- It is often seen as natural and unchangeable.
- It is not based on **biology,** but on **social expectations** and **stereotypes.**

## Public/Private Division

- From childhood, boys and girls are taught to believe that the **main duty of women** is to manage housework and raise children, creating a mindset that household responsibilities belong only to women.
- This thinking leads to a **sexual division of labour** in most families, where women handle all tasks inside the home like cooking, cleaning, washing clothes, tailoring, and childcare, while men take responsibility for outside work.
- It is not that men are unable to do household work, but they consider it solely a woman's job. However, when these tasks are seen as paid jobs, men are often willing to do them, which is why most **tailors** and **hotel cooks** are men.
- Women are also not confined to home duties; in villages, they fetch water, gather fuel, and work in fields, while in cities, many poor women work as **domestic helpers** in middle-class families, and middle-class women often find employment in **offices.**
- A majority of women are involved in **some kind of paid work** in addition to household labour, but their contribution is undervalued and does not receive proper recognition in society.
- Despite being **half of the world's population,** women continue to have a very limited role in **public life,** particularly in the field of **politics.**
- Earlier, women were denied opportunities to participate in public matters such as **voting,** contesting elections, or holding offices, as these rights were reserved only for men.
- With time, the **gender issue** became part of political debates as women across the world came together, organised movements, and protested to demand **equal rights.**
- Several countries experienced strong **agitations** by women that forced leaders to extend **voting rights** and include them in the political process.
- These **agitations** focused on raising the **political** and **legal status** of women, while also demanding better opportunities in **education** and **careers.**
- More radical women's movements went further and aimed at achieving **equality in personal and family life,** not just in public positions.
- Such movements came to be known as **feminist movements,** pushing for complete social and gender equality.
- The political expression of **gender division** and the mobilisation of women around this issue greatly improved their chance to play a stronger role in **public life.**
- As a result, women are now seen working as **scientists, doctors, engineers, lawyers, managers,** and **teachers in colleges and universities,** fields that once were considered unsuitable for them.
- In some regions of the world, particularly the **Scandinavian countries** like **Sweden, Norway, and Finland,** women's participation in public life is extremely high.
- In contrast, in **India,** even though women's conditions have improved after **Independence,** they still remain far behind men.
- Society in India continues to be **male-dominated** and is largely a **patriarchal** system.
- The **literacy rate** among women is only **54%,** compared to **76%** among men.
- A smaller number of girls go for **higher studies,** even though school results show that girls perform as well as boys, and sometimes even better.
- Many girls drop out because parents choose to spend more on their sons' education instead of investing equally in both sons and daughters.
- As a result, women make up a very small share in **highly paid and valued jobs.**
- On average, an **Indian woman works one hour more** per day than an Indian man, but much of her work is **unpaid** and therefore not valued.
- The **Equal Remuneration Act, 1976** states that men and women should receive **equal wages for equal work,** yet in most fields like **sports, cinema, factories,** and **agriculture,** women are still paid less than men even when doing the same job.
- In many regions of India, parents **prefer sons** and often resort to **sex-selective abortions,** leading to a serious fall in the **child sex ratio** to only **919 girls per 1000 boys.**
- In some **States,** this ratio has dropped below **850** or even **800,** showing extreme gender imbalance.
- Women continue to face **harassment, exploitation, and violence,** with **urban areas** becoming particularly unsafe.
- Women are also not fully safe in their own homes, where they face **domestic violence, beatings, and harassment.**

## Womens Political Representation

- Although women's issues are widely known, they are not given proper **attention** in society and politics.
- **Feminists** and women's movements argue that unless **women hold real power,** their problems will not be addressed seriously.
- A major solution is to have **more women as elected representatives.**
- In India, the presence of women in legislatures has always been very low.
- In **2019,** women's representation in the **Lok Sabha** reached **14.36%** of total seats for the first time.
- In **State Assemblies,** the share of women is less than **5%.**
- With these figures, **India** ranks among the **bottom nations** globally, even behind the averages of several **African** and **Latin American** countries.
- Even in governments, **cabinets remain male-dominated,** and this is true even when a woman becomes the **Chief Minister** or **Prime Minister.**
- A solution is **legal reservation** to ensure fair representation of women in elected bodies.
- The **Panchayati Raj system** in India already reserves **one-third seats** in local governments like **panchayats** and **municipalities** for women.
- Today, more than **10 lakh elected women representatives** serve in rural and urban local bodies.
- Women's movements long demanded the same formula at the level of **Lok Sabha** and **State Assemblies.**
- In **2023,** the **Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women's Reservation Act, 2023)** was passed, ensuring **33% reservation for women** in the **Lok Sabha, State Assemblies,** and **Delhi Assembly.**
- The issue of **gender division** proves that some **social divisions must enter politics** to be resolved.
- It also shows that **disadvantaged groups benefit** when their inequalities are made a political question.

## Religion, Communalism and Politics

- **Religious division** is different from gender division; while not universal, **religious diversity** is widespread across the world, including **India.**
- Even in countries where most people follow the same religion, like **Northern Ireland,** there can be deep differences in how religion is practiced.
- Unlike gender, **religious differences are often seen in politics.**
- **Gandhiji** believed that religion and politics cannot be separated. By religion, he meant **moral values** common to all religions, not any specific faith. He felt that politics should be guided by these **ethics and values.**
- In India, **human rights groups** have demanded government action to prevent **communal riots** and protect **religious minorities.**
- The **women's movement** has argued that **family laws** of all religions are unfair to women and has demanded changes to make them more **equal and just.**
- These examples show that there is a clear connection between **religion and politics,** and such connections are not always harmful.
- **Ideas and values** from religion can play a positive role in politics, and citizens should be free to present their **needs and demands** as members of a religious community.
- It is also acceptable for those in power to regulate religious practices if it helps prevent **discrimination and oppression,** as long as **all religions are treated equally.**

## Communalism

- Problems arise when **religion is made the basis of the nation,** as shown in the example of **Northern Ireland.**
- The danger increases when **politics becomes exclusive and partisan,** where one religion is set against another.
- This occurs when one religion is treated as **superior,** when demands of one religious group are made against another, or when the **state uses power** to impose domination of one religion over the rest.
- This misuse of religion in politics is called **communal politics.**
- **Communalism** is based on the idea that religion is the main basis of social community.
- It assumes that all followers of one religion have the same **fundamental interests** and must belong to one single community, ignoring other differences within them.
- It also sees followers of different religions as belonging to separate communities with conflicting interests, making equality and unity difficult.
- In its extreme form, communalism claims that people of different religions cannot live together as **equal citizens,** and either one must dominate or different nations must be formed.
- This belief is **flawed,** as even people of the same religion have diverse **interests, roles, and identities** and should not be silenced under one label.
- **Every community has many voices,** and they all deserve to be heard; uniting people only by religion suppresses internal diversity.

## Forms of Communalism in Politics

- **Everyday beliefs** of communalism: show up as **prejudices, stereotypes, and belief in superiority** of one's religion over others, often unnoticed.
- **Political dominance:** A communal mindset pushes for control by one's religious group. For the **majority,** this takes the form of **majoritarian dominance.** For **minorities,** it can turn into a demand for a **separate political unit.**
- **Political mobilisation on religious lines:** Uses **sacred symbols, leaders, emotions, and fear** to unite followers of one religion. In elections, this means appealing specially to one religion's voters over others.
- **Communal violence:** The ugliest form, seen in **riots and massacres.** During the **Partition of India and Pakistan,** large-scale violence broke out. Even after Independence, **communal riots** have occurred frequently.

## Secular State

- **Communalism** has been and still is one of the **major challenges to democracy** in India.
- The makers of the **Constitution** recognized this challenge and adopted the model of a **secular state.**
- The secular choice is reflected in several **constitutional provisions:**
  - There is **no official religion** for India, unlike **Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Islam in Pakistan,** or **Christianity in England.**
  - All individuals and communities have the **freedom to profess, practice, and propagate** any religion, or to follow none.
  - The Constitution **prohibits discrimination** on the basis of religion.
  - The state is allowed to **intervene in religious matters** to ensure **equality within communities;** an example is the **ban on untouchability.**
- Secularism in this sense is not limited to ideology of some political groups but forms a **foundation of India.**
- **Communalism** should not be seen as a threat to only one group; it is a threat to the **very idea of India itself.**
- Thus, combating communalism requires more than just a secular Constitution.
- **Communal prejudices and propaganda** must be challenged in daily life, and **religion-based mobilisation** must be opposed in the field of politics.

## Caste and Politics

We have seen two instances of the expression of social divisions in the arena of politics, one largely positive and the other largely negative. Let us turn to our final case, that of caste and politics, that has both positive and the negative aspects.

## Caste Inequalities

- **Caste division** is unique to **India,** unlike gender and religion.
- All societies have inequalities and occupations passed across generations, but the **caste system** is an extreme form as it was **sanctioned by rituals.**
- Members of a caste formed a distinct **community:** following the same occupation, marrying within the group, and avoiding social mixing like eating together with other castes.
- This system was built on **exclusion and discrimination** against the 'outcaste' **groups,** who suffered the inhuman practice of **untouchability.**
- Reformers like **Jotiba Phule, Gandhiji, B.R. Ambedkar,** and **Periyar Ramaswami Naicker** fought for a society free from caste inequalities.
- Due to their work and socio-economic changes, such as **economic development, urbanisation, literacy, education, occupational mobility,** and weakening of **landlords,** the old rigid caste hierarchy is breaking down.
- In today's **urban areas,** caste carries less visible weight; people can freely share public spaces like **streets** and **restaurants.**
- The **Constitution of India** banned caste-based discrimination and laid down measures to undo caste injustice.
- Compared to a century ago, India has transformed, though **caste has not disappeared.**
- Many traditional practices continue — such as people **marrying within their caste/tribe,** and **untouchability** still existing in some places despite being unconstitutional.
- The historic imbalance remains: caste groups with **access to education in the past** continue to excel in modern education, while those denied education lag behind.
- This creates an **overrepresentation of upper castes** in the **urban middle class.**
- **Caste and economic status** remain closely connected in India even today.

## Caste in Politics

- **Casteism,** like communalism, is rooted in the belief that **caste is the sole basis of social community.**
- This way of thinking assumes that people of the same caste share **common interests** which they cannot share with members of other castes.
- In reality, **caste is only one aspect** of people's lives, not the only or most important factor.

## Forms of Caste in Politics

- While selecting **election candidates,** parties consider the **caste composition** of voters and often nominate candidates of different castes to secure broad support.
- In forming governments, parties ensure different **castes and tribes** are represented.
- Many parties and candidates directly appeal to **caste sentiment** to gain votes. Some parties are even seen as **representatives of particular castes.**
- The system of **universal adult franchise** and **one-person-one-vote** led to the mobilisation of previously **marginalised castes,** increasing their political awareness and participation.

## Limits of Caste in Politics

- No parliamentary constituency has a **majority of one caste,** so every party or candidate must attract support from **multiple castes and communities.**
- No caste votes entirely for one party; when a caste is called a "vote bank", it only means a **large proportion** supports that party, not the whole caste.
- Often, **multiple candidates from the same caste** contest in one constituency, while other voters may have **no candidate belonging to their caste.**
- The ruling party or sitting **MP/MLA** often loses elections, which would not happen if political preferences were fixed strictly by caste.

## Other Decisive Factors

- While caste plays a role, **other factors strongly influence elections:**
  - Loyalty and attachment to **political parties** can be stronger than caste identity.
  - Differences in **economic condition** within the same caste also divide voting behaviour (rich and poor, men and women often vote differently).
  - **Performance of the government** and **popularity of leaders** play a crucial role and are often **decisive** in elections.

## Politics in Caste

- The relation between **caste and politics** is **two-way:** politics influences caste just as caste influences politics.
- It is not just politics that becomes caste-based, rather **caste gets politicised** when brought into the political field.

## How Caste Gets Politicised

- Each **caste group** tries to expand by including neighbouring castes or **sub-castes** once excluded from it.
- Different caste groups often form **coalitions** with others, leading to dialogue and negotiations.
- New political categories like **'backward castes'** and **'forward castes'** have emerged, reshaping traditional caste divisions.

## Positive Effects

- Expression of caste identity in politics has allowed **disadvantaged groups,** like **Dalits and OBCs,** to demand **a share of power.**
- This has increased their **access to decision-making,** dignity, land, resources, and opportunities.
- Many political and social organisations continue to fight against **caste discrimination** and for greater equality.

## Negative Effects

- Focusing only on caste identity in politics is **unhealthy for democracy.**
- It can **divert attention** from critical issues such as **poverty, development,** and **corruption.**
- In extreme cases, caste-based politics leads to **tension, conflict, and violence** within society.
