Detailed notes on Geography Chapter 5: what minerals are and where they occur — ferrous, non-ferrous and non-metallic minerals — plus conservation and every conventional and non-conventional energy source.

## Introduction

- You have studied that the earth's crust is made up of different minerals embedded in the rocks. Various metals are extracted from these minerals after proper refinement.
- The railway lines and the tarmac (paving) of the roads, our implements and machinery too are made from minerals. Cars, buses, trains, aeroplanes are manufactured from minerals and run on power resources derived from the earth.
- Life processes cannot occur without minerals. Although our mineral intake represents only about 0.3 per cent of our total intake of nutrients, they are so potent and so important that without them we would not be able to utilise the other 99.7 per cent of foodstuffs.

## What is a Mineral?

- Geologists define mineral as a "homogenous, naturally occurring substance with a definable internal structure."
- Rocks are combinations of homogenous substances called minerals.
- Some rocks, for instance limestone, consist of a single mineral only, but majority of the rock consist of several minerals in varying proportions.
- A particular mineral that will be formed from a certain combination of elements depends upon the physical and chemical conditions under which the material forms.
- This results in a wide range of colours, hardness, crystal forms, lustre and density that a particular mineral possesses. Geologists use these properties to classify the minerals.

## Mode of Occurrence of Minerals

### Where are these minerals found?

- Minerals are usually found in "ores".
- The term ore is used to describe an accumulation of any mineral mixed with other elements.
- The mineral content of the ore must be in sufficient concentration to make its extraction commercially viable.
- **In igneous and metamorphic rocks** minerals may occur in the cracks, crevices, faults or joints.
- The smaller occurrences are called veins and the larger are called lodes.
- They are formed when minerals in liquid/ molten and gaseous forms are forced upward through cavities towards the earth's surface.
- Major metallic minerals like tin, copper, zinc and lead etc. are obtained from veins and lodes.
- In **sedimentary rocks** a number of minerals occur in beds or layers.
- They have been formed as a result of deposition, accumulation and concentration in horizontal strata.
- Coal and some forms of iron ore have been concentrated as a result of long periods under great heat and pressure.
- Another mode of formation involves the decomposition of surface rocks, and the removal of soluble constituents, leaving a residual mass of weathered material containing ores.
- Bauxite is formed this way.
- Certain minerals may occur as **alluvial deposits** in sands of valley floors and the base of hills.
- These deposits are called 'placer deposits'
- Gold, silver, tin and platinum are most important among such minerals.
- **The ocean waters contain vast quantities** of minerals, but most of these are too widely diffused to be of economic significance.
- Common salt, magnesium and bromine are largely derived from ocean waters.

Coal mining in Jowai and Cherapunjee is done by family member in the form of a long narrow tunnel, known as 'Rat hole' mining. The National Green Tribunal has declared such activities illegal and recommended that these should be stopped forthwith.

- Peninsular rocks contain most of the reserves of coal, metallic minerals, mica and many other non-metallic minerals.
- Sedimentary rocks on the western and eastern flanks of the peninsula, in Gujarat and Assam have most of the petroleum deposits.
- Rajasthan with the rock systems of the peninsula, has reserves of many non-ferrous minerals.
- The vast alluvial plains of north India are almost devoid of economic minerals.
- The concentration of mineral in the ore, the ease of extraction and closeness to the market play an important role in affecting the economic viability of a reserve.
- To meet the demand, a choice has to be made between a number of possible options. When this is done a mineral 'deposit' or 'reserve' turns into a mine.

## Ferrous Minerals

- Ferrous minerals are metallic minerals that contain iron as a major component.
- India exports substantial quantities of ferrous minerals after meeting her internal demands.

### Iron Ore

- India is rich in good quality iron ores.
- **Magnetite** is the finest iron ore with a very high content of iron up to 70 per cent. It has excellent magnetic qualities, especially valuable in the electrical industry.
- **Hematite** ore is the most important industrial iron ore in terms of the quantity used, but has a slightly lower iron content than magnetite.

### Manganese

- Manganese is mainly used in the manufacturing of steel and ferro-manganese alloy.
- Nearly 10 kg of manganese is required to manufacture one tonne of steel.
- It is also used in manufacturing bleaching powder, insecticides and paints.

## Non-Ferrous Minerals

- These minerals, which include copper, bauxite, lead, zinc and gold play a vital role in a number of metallurgical, engineering and electrical industries.

### Copper

- **Characteristics:** malleable, ductile, and a good conductor of heat and electricity.
- **Major uses:** electrical cables, electronics, and chemical industries.
- **Important regions:**
  - Madhya Pradesh (Balaghat mines)
  - Rajasthan (Khetri mines)
  - Jharkhand (Singhbhum district)

### Bauxite

- **Characteristics:** ore of aluminium; light, flexible, and resistant to corrosion.
- **Major uses:** source of aluminum metal for manufacturing.
- **Important regions:**
  - Odisha (leading producer, e.g., Koraput, Sambalpur)
  - Chhattisgarh (Amarkantak plateau)

## Non Metallic Minerals

- **Mica** is a mineral made up of a series of plates or leaves. It splits easily into thin sheets.
- Mica can be clear, black, green, red yellow or brown.
- Due to its excellent di-electric strength, low power loss factor, insulating properties and resistance to high voltage, mica is one of the most indispensable minerals used in electric and electronic industries.
- **Limestone** is found in association with rocks composed of calcium carbonates or calcium and magnesium carbonates.
- It is found in sedimentary rocks of most geological formations.
- **Limestone is the basic raw material for the cement industry and essential for smelting iron ore in the blast furnace.**

## Conservation of Minerals

- Industry and agriculture depend strongly on mineral deposits and the substances made from them.
- Workable mineral deposits are a very small part (only one per cent) of the earth's crust.
- We are using up mineral resources very fast, though they took millions of years to form and concentrate.
- Minerals form so slowly that the rate at which they are replaced is extremely small compared to the rate at which we use them.
- So, mineral resources are limited (finite) and cannot be renewed (non-renewable).
- Rich mineral deposits are very valuable but short-lived possessions of our country.
- As ores keep being extracted, costs increase because minerals have to be taken from greater depths and their quality decreases.
- Recycling of metals, using scrap metals and other substitutes are steps in conserving our mineral resources for the future.

## Energy Resources

- Energy is needed for all activities: cooking, light, heat, running vehicles and driving machinery in industries.
- Energy is produced from fuel minerals (coal, petroleum, natural gas, uranium) and electricity.
- Energy resources are of two types: conventional and non-conventional.
- Conventional sources: firewood, cattle dung cake, coal, petroleum, natural gas, electricity (hydel and thermal).
- Non-conventional sources: solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas, atomic energy.
- Firewood and dung cake are most common in rural India. They meet more than 70 per cent of rural household energy needs.
- Their use is becoming difficult because forest area is decreasing.
- Dung cake use is discouraged because it wastes valuable manure that could be used in agriculture.

## Conventional Sources of Energy

### Coal

Coal in India:

- Coal is the most abundant fossil fuel in India. It is used for power generation, industry and domestic needs.
- India depends heavily on coal for its commercial energy needs.
- Coal is formed by the compression of plant material over millions of years. Its type depends on the degree of compression, and the depth and time of burial.

Types of coal:

- Peat: formed from decaying plants in swamps. Low carbon, high moisture, low heating capacity.
- Lignite: low-grade brown coal, soft, high moisture. Main reserves are at Neyveli (Tamil Nadu) and it is used for electricity generation.
- Bituminous coal: buried deep and heated more. It is the most popular coal in commercial use.
- Metallurgical coal: high-grade bituminous coal, used for smelting iron in blast furnaces.
- Anthracite: the highest quality hard coal.

Coal in India (geological ages):

- Gondwana coal: a little over 200 million years old. It is metallurgical coal, found in the Damodar valley (West Bengal and Jharkhand).
- Important coalfields: Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro.
- Other deposits: Godavari, Mahanadi, Son and Wardha valleys.
- Tertiary coal: about 55 million years old. Found in the north-eastern states of Meghalaya, Assam, Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland.

Location of industries:

- Coal is bulky and loses weight on use (it turns to ash). So heavy industries and thermal power stations are located on or near coalfields.

### Petroleum

Petroleum in India:

- Petroleum (mineral oil) is the second major energy source in India after coal.
- Uses: fuel for heat and lighting, lubricants for machinery, and raw material for many industries.
- Petroleum refineries are a "nodal industry" for synthetic textile, fertiliser and many chemical industries.

Occurrence:

- Most of India's petroleum is found in anticlines and fault traps in rocks of the tertiary age.
- In anticlines (domes), oil is trapped in the crest of the upfold.
- The oil-bearing layer is porous limestone or sandstone. Non-porous layers above and below stop the oil from rising or sinking.
- Oil is also found in fault traps between porous and non-porous rocks.
- Gas is lighter, so it usually occurs above the oil.

Major producing areas:

- Mumbai High, Gujarat and Assam.
- Gujarat: Ankleshwar is the most important field.
- Assam: the oldest oil-producing state of India. Important fields are Digboi, Naharkatiya and Moran-Hugrijan.

### Natural Gas

Natural Gas in India:

- Natural gas is found along with petroleum and is released when crude oil is brought to the surface.
- Uses: fuel in the power sector (electricity), heating in industries, raw material in chemical, petrochemical and fertiliser industries, transport fuel and cooking fuel.
- With city gas distribution (CGD) networks, it is emerging as a preferred fuel:
  - CNG (Compressed Natural Gas) for transport
  - PNG (Piped Natural Gas) for cooking at homes

Major reserves:

- West Coast: Mumbai High and allied fields, supported by finds in the Cambay basin.
- East Coast: new reserves in the Krishna-Godavari basin.

Pipelines:

- The first Hazira-Vijaipur-Jagdishpur (HVJ) pipeline is 1,700 km long and was built by GAIL (India).
- It linked Mumbai High and Bassein gas fields with fertiliser, power and industrial complexes in western and northern India.
- It gave a push to the development of India's gas market.
- Pipelines have grown from 1,700 km to 18,500 km and are expected to reach over 34,000 km as a Gas Grid, linking all gas sources and markets, including the North Eastern states.

### Electricity

Electricity:

- Electricity has a wide range of uses, so its per capita consumption is an index (indicator) of development.
- Electricity is generated in two main ways:
  - Hydro electricity: running water drives hydro turbines.
  - Thermal power: burning fuels like coal, petroleum and natural gas drives turbines.
- Once generated, electricity is exactly the same whichever way it is produced.

Hydro electricity:

- Made by fast flowing water, which is a renewable resource.
- Examples of multi-purpose projects: Bhakra Nangal, Damodar Valley Corporation, Kopili Hydel Project.

Thermal electricity:

- Made using coal, petroleum and natural gas.
- Thermal power stations use non-renewable fossil fuels.

## Non-Conventional Sources of Energy

### Nuclear or Atomic Energy

- Nuclear (atomic) energy is obtained by altering the structure of atoms.
- This releases a large amount of energy as heat, which is used to generate electric power.
- Minerals used: Uranium and Thorium.
- Where found:
  - Jharkhand
  - Aravalli ranges of Rajasthan
  - Monazite sands of Kerala (rich in thorium)

### Solar Energy

- India is a tropical country, so it has enormous possibilities of tapping solar energy.
- Photovoltaic technology converts sunlight directly into electricity.
- Solar energy is fast becoming popular in rural and remote areas.
- Big solar power plants are being set up in different parts of India.
- Benefits:
  - Reduces the dependence of rural households on firewood and dung cakes
  - Contributes to environmental conservation
  - Ensures an adequate supply of manure in agriculture

### Wind Power

- India has great potential for wind power.
- The largest wind farm cluster is in Tamil Nadu, from Nagercoil to Madurai.
- Other states with important wind farms: Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Gujarat, Kerala, Maharashtra and Lakshadweep.
- Nagercoil and Jaisalmer are well known for the effective use of wind energy.

### Biogas

- Raw materials: shrubs, farm waste, animal waste and human waste.
- Biogas is produced by the decomposition of organic matter.
- It has higher thermal efficiency than kerosene, dung cake and charcoal.
- Biogas plants are set up at municipal, cooperative and individual levels.
- Plants using cattle dung are called 'Gobar gas plants' in rural India.
- Twin benefits to the farmer: energy and improved quality of manure.
- Biogas is by far the most efficient use of cattle dung.
- It prevents the loss of trees and manure caused by burning fuel wood and dung cakes.

### Tidal Energy

- Oceanic tides can be used to generate electricity.
- Floodgate dams are built across inlets.
- Working:
  - At high tide, water flows into the inlet and is trapped when the gate is closed.
  - After the tide falls outside, the trapped water flows back to the sea through a pipe that passes through a power-generating turbine.
- Ideal places in India:
  - Gulf of Khambhat (Gujarat)
  - Gulf of Kuchchh (Gujarat)
  - Gangetic delta in the Sunderban region (West Bengal)

### Geothermal Energy

- Geothermal energy is heat and electricity produced from the heat in the Earth's interior.
- It exists because the Earth grows progressively hotter with increasing depth.
- Where the geothermal gradient is high, high temperatures are found at shallow depths.
- Working:
  - Groundwater absorbs heat from the hot rocks.
  - When it rises to the surface, it turns into steam.
  - The steam drives turbines to generate electricity.
- India has several hundred hot springs that could be used to generate electricity.
- Two experimental projects in India:
  - Parvati valley, near Manikaran (Himachal Pradesh)
  - Puga Valley (Ladakh)
