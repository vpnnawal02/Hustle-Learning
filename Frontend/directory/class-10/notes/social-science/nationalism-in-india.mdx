Detailed notes on History Chapter 2: how the national movement grew in India — from satyagraha and Non-Cooperation to Civil Disobedience — and how different social groups understood freedom.

## Introduction

- **Modern nationalism in Europe** became closely associated with the **formation of nation-states,** changing people's understanding of identity and belonging.
- **New symbols, icons, songs, and ideas** helped create fresh connections among communities and redefined their boundaries.
- In most countries, the making of national identity was a **long and gradual process.**
- In **India,** as in other colonies, the **growth of modern nationalism was deeply linked with the anti-colonial movement.**
- People began to **discover their unity through the struggle against colonialism,** with the **shared sense of oppression** becoming a bond that tied different groups together.
- Despite this, **each class and group experienced colonialism differently,** and their **ideas of freedom were not always the same.**
- The **Indian National Congress under Mahatma Gandhi** attempted to bring all these groups together within one unified movement.
- However, **unity did not emerge without conflicts,** as various differences persisted.
- Earlier, students have studied the **rise of nationalism in India up to the first decade of the 20th century.**
- From the **1920s,** the story continues with the **Non-Cooperation Movement and the Civil Disobedience Movement,** exploring:
  - How the **Congress developed the national movement**
  - How **different social groups participated**
  - How **nationalism captured the imagination of the masses**

## The First World War, Khilafat and Non-Cooperation Movement

- After **1919,** the national movement in India spread to new areas, included new social groups, and adopted new forms of struggle.
- The **First World War** created a new economic and political situation, leading to increased defence expenditure financed by **war loans** and higher taxes.
- **Customs duties were raised** and **income tax was introduced,** adding to the burden of the people.
- Prices doubled between **1913 and 1918,** causing severe hardship for the common people.
- Villages were forced to supply soldiers, and **forced recruitment in rural areas** created widespread anger.
- Between **1918-1919** and **1920-1921,** crop failures in many parts of India led to acute food shortages.
- An **influenza epidemic** spread during the same period, worsening the suffering.
- According to the **1921 census,** nearly **12–13 million people** died due to famines and the epidemic.
- People expected relief after the war, but their hardships continued.
- At this time, a **new leader** emerged and proposed a **new mode of struggle.**

## The Idea of Satyagraha

- **Mahatma Gandhi** returned to India in **January 1915** after his success in **South Africa,** where he fought the racist regime using his novel method of agitation called **satyagraha.**
- The idea of satyagraha highlighted the **power of truth** and the importance of searching for it.
- Gandhi believed that if the cause was just and the fight was against injustice, **physical force was unnecessary.**
- A **satyagrahi** could win through **non-violence,** appealing to the **conscience of the oppressor** rather than forcing acceptance through violence.
- This method relied on persuasion and moral strength, emphasizing that ultimately **truth would triumph.**
- Gandhi was convinced that the **dharma of non-violence** had the power to unite all Indians.
- After returning to India, Gandhi launched several satyagraha movements:
  - In **1917,** at **Champaran (Bihar),** he led a movement to support peasants against the oppressive plantation system.
  - In **1917,** he also organised a satyagraha in **Kheda (Gujarat),** where peasants suffering from crop failure and plague demanded relaxation of revenue collection.
  - In **1918,** he went to **Ahmedabad** and organised a satyagraha among **cotton mill workers** demanding better conditions.

## The Rowlatt Act

- Encouraged by earlier successes, **Mahatma Gandhi in 1919** launched a nationwide satyagraha against the **Rowlatt Act (1919).**
- The Act was passed despite opposition from Indian members of the Legislative Council and gave the government sweeping powers, including **detention of political prisoners without trial for two years.**
- Gandhi proposed a **non-violent civil disobedience,** beginning with a **hartal on 6 April 1919.** Rallies were organised, railway workers went on strike, and shops were shut down.
- Alarmed by the unrest, the British cracked down; **local leaders were arrested in Amritsar,** and Gandhi was barred from entering **Delhi.**
- On **10 April 1919,** police fired on a peaceful procession in Amritsar, provoking anger and attacks on banks, post offices, and railway stations.
- **Martial law was imposed,** and **General Dyer** took command.
- On **13 April 1919,** the infamous **Jallianwalla Bagh massacre** took place. General Dyer blocked exits and opened fire on a large unarmed crowd, killing hundreds.
- Dyer later declared his aim was to **'produce a moral effect'** and instil terror among the satyagrahis.
- News of the massacre spread quickly, leading to strikes, protests, and police clashes in many **north Indian towns.**
- The government responded brutally: people were flogged, forced to crawl in the streets, rub noses on the ground, salute sahibs, and villages near **Gujranwala (Punjab, now in Pakistan)** were even bombed.
- Disturbed by the growing violence, **Gandhi called off the Rowlatt Satyagraha.** While widespread, the movement was still largely confined to towns and cities.
- Gandhi realised the need for a **broad-based national movement,** which could succeed only by bringing **Hindus and Muslims closer together.**
- He decided to take up the **Khilafat issue,** related to the fate of the **Ottoman emperor (Khalifa)** after **World War I.**
- To defend the Khalifa's powers, a **Khilafat Committee** was formed in **Bombay, March 1919,** led by younger Muslim leaders like **Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali.**
- Gandhi supported the movement and saw it as a chance to unite Muslims with the broader nationalist cause.
- At the **Calcutta session of the Congress in September 1920,** Gandhi convinced leaders to launch the **Non-Cooperation Movement,** supporting both the Khilafat cause and the demand for **swaraj.**

## Why Non-cooperation?

- In his book **Hind Swaraj (1909), Mahatma Gandhi** declared that British rule in India survived only because of the **cooperation of Indians,** and if this cooperation was withdrawn, **swaraj** could be achieved within a year.
- Gandhi proposed that **non-cooperation** should unfold in stages:
  - First, by surrendering government-awarded **titles.**
  - Then, by boycotting the **civil services, army, police, courts, legislative councils, schools, and foreign goods.**
  - If the government responded with repression, the next stage would be a **full civil disobedience campaign.**
- During the summer of **1920, Gandhi and Shaukat Ali** travelled widely across India to mobilise popular support for the movement.
- Some Congress leaders were skeptical and feared that the movement might:
  - Lead to **violence** among common people.
  - Disrupt participation in the upcoming **council elections of November 1920,** which many members were reluctant to boycott.
- Between **September and December 1920,** there was a serious debate within Congress between supporters and opponents of Gandhi's plan.
- Finally, at the **Congress Session in Nagpur (December 1920),** a **compromise** was reached, and the **Non-Cooperation programme** was officially adopted.
- The next question was how the movement would unfold, who would join it, and how different **social groups understood Non-Cooperation.**

## The Movement in the Towns

- The Non-Cooperation Movement began with **middle-class participation in cities.** Thousands of students left **government schools and colleges,** many headmasters and teachers resigned, and lawyers gave up their legal practices.
- The **council elections** were boycotted in most provinces except **Madras,** where the **Justice Party (non-Brahmans)** participated, seeing it as a way to gain political power usually dominated by Brahmans.
- On the **economic front,** the impact was strong:
  - **Foreign goods were boycotted,** liquor shops were picketed, and **foreign cloth was burnt** in large bonfires.
  - The **import of foreign cloth fell between 1921 and 1922,** dropping in value from **Rs 102 crore to Rs 57 crore.**
  - Many merchants and traders stopped selling or financing foreign goods.
  - With people discarding imported clothes and shifting to Indian ones, the **production of Indian textile mills and handlooms increased.**
- However, the movement in cities began to **lose momentum:**
  - **Khadi cloth** was more expensive than machine-made mill cloth, making it difficult for poorer people to boycott imported cloth for long.
  - Boycotting **British institutions** created challenges, as **alternative Indian institutions** were slow to be established.
  - Gradually, students and teachers returned to government schools, and lawyers resumed work in government courts.

## Rebellion in the Countryside

- From the **cities,** the **Non-Cooperation Movement** spread to the **countryside,** incorporating the struggles of peasants and tribals that were already growing after the war.
- In **Awadh,** peasants were led by **Baba Ramchandra,** a sanyasi who had earlier worked in **Fiji as an indentured labourer.**
- The Awadh peasant movement was directed against **talukdars and landlords** who charged high rents, imposed various cesses, and forced peasants into **begar (unpaid labour).** Tenants had no security of tenure and faced regular eviction.
- Peasant demands included **reduction of revenue, abolition of begar, and social boycott of oppressive landlords.**
- **Nai–dhobi bandhs** were organised by village panchayats to socially isolate landlords by denying them the services of barbers and washermen.
- In **June 1920, Jawaharlal Nehru** began touring Awadh villages to understand peasant grievances. By **October 1920,** the **Oudh Kisan Sabha** was formed under Nehru, Baba Ramchandra, and others, and soon over **300 branches** were established.
- When the Non-Cooperation Movement began in **1921,** Congress attempted to integrate the Awadh peasant struggle into the larger national movement.
- However, the **movement in Awadh often turned violent** – peasants attacked houses of talukdars and merchants, looted bazaars, and seized grain hoards. Many local leaders spread messages in Gandhi's name, claiming that **no taxes were to be paid** and **land would be redistributed.**
- Among **tribals,** the struggle took a different turn. In the **Gudem Hills of Andhra Pradesh,** a **militant guerrilla movement** spread in the early **1920s.**
- The colonial government had restricted access to forests, depriving tribals of grazing cattle, collecting firewood, and fruits, while also forcing them to contribute **begar for road building.** This provoked widespread anger.
- The leader of this tribal revolt was **Alluri Sitaram Raju,** who was believed to have spiritual powers such as astrological prediction, healing, and surviving bullet shots. Many tribals saw him as an **incarnation of God.**
- Raju admired **Mahatma Gandhi,** encouraged the use of **khadi and rejection of liquor,** but also believed that **India could be liberated only by force, not non-violence.**
- The **Gudem rebels** attacked police stations, tried to kill British officials, and carried out guerrilla warfare to achieve **swaraj.**
- Alluri Sitaram Raju was **captured and executed in 1924,** but over time he became a **folk hero** among the people.

## Swaraj in the Plantations

- **Workers** too developed their own understanding of **Mahatma Gandhi and swaraj.**
- For **plantation workers in Assam,** freedom meant:
  - The right to **move freely** beyond the confined tea gardens.
  - Maintaining a connection with their **home villages.**
- Under the **Inland Emigration Act of 1859,** plantation workers could not leave without permission, which was rarely granted.
- Inspired by the **Non-Cooperation Movement,** thousands of workers **defied authorities, left plantations, and began journeys back to their villages.**
- Many believed that under **Gandhi Raj,** they would reclaim land in their villages and live freely.
- However, most never reached home, as they were **stranded during railway and steamer strikes,** and later **caught and brutally beaten by the police.**
- The **workers' vision of swaraj** was not aligned exactly with the Congress programme. They imagined it as a time when **all suffering and troubles would end.**
- Still, when **tribals and workers invoked Gandhiji's name** and shouted slogans like **'Swatantra Bharat',** they emotionally connected themselves to the **larger all-India movement.**
- By linking their struggles to **Congress and Mahatma Gandhi,** they were identifying with a movement that went **beyond their local grievances.**

## Towards Civil Disobedience

- In **February 1922, Mahatma Gandhi withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement,** fearing it was turning violent in many places. He believed satyagrahis required more training before mass struggles.
- Within the **Congress,** differences emerged. Some leaders were tired of mass struggles and preferred to enter **provincial councils under the Government of India Act (1919)** to:
  - Oppose British policies from within.
  - Argue for reforms.
  - Expose the councils as undemocratic.
- **C. R. Das** and **Motilal Nehru** formed the **Swaraj Party** within the Congress to return to council politics.
- Younger leaders like **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose,** however, pushed for **radical mass agitation** and **complete independence.**
- Two major factors shaped Indian politics in the **late 1920s:**
  - The **worldwide economic depression:** Agricultural prices fell from **1926** and collapsed after **1930,** making it difficult for peasants to sell crops or pay revenue. By 1930, the **countryside was in turmoil.**
  - The setting up of the **Simon Commission (1928)** by the British Tory government, headed by **Sir John Simon,** to review the constitutional system. It had **no Indian members,** only British, which outraged Indians.
- When the **Simon Commission arrived in 1928,** it was met with **'Go back Simon'** protests, supported by both **Congress** and the **Muslim League.**
- To calm opposition, **Lord Irwin (Viceroy)** announced in **October 1929** a vague promise of **dominion status in the future** and a **Round Table Conference** to frame a constitution. This failed to satisfy Congress leaders.
- The **radicals in Congress,** led by **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose,** demanded **full independence,** while moderates who wanted dominion status **lost influence.**
- At the **Lahore Congress (December 1929),** presided over by **Jawaharlal Nehru,** the demand for **Purna Swaraj (complete independence)** was adopted.
- It was declared that **26 January 1930** would be celebrated as **Independence Day,** with people pledging to fight for complete freedom.
- However, the day was observed only weakly, and Gandhi realised he had to connect the idea of swaraj with **everyday issues of common people** to make it meaningful.

## The Salt March and the Civil Disobedience Movement

- **Mahatma Gandhi** saw **salt** as a powerful symbol to unite Indians, since it was used by both rich and poor.
- On **31 January 1930,** Gandhi sent a letter to **Viceroy Irwin** with **11 demands,** covering general issues and specific concerns of industrialists, peasants, and other classes.
- The most important demand was the **abolition of the salt tax,** as it represented the oppressive face of British rule.
- The letter stated that if the demands were not met by **11 March 1930,** the **Civil Disobedience Movement** would begin.
- Irwin refused to negotiate, and Gandhi launched the **Salt March,** accompanied by **78 volunteers.**
- The march covered **240 miles** from **Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi,** lasting **24 days** (10 miles a day). Along the way, Gandhi addressed large crowds about swaraj and peaceful defiance.
- On **6 April 1930,** Gandhi reached **Dandi** and broke the law by **making salt from seawater,** formally launching the movement.
- The **Civil Disobedience Movement** differed from the **Non-Cooperation Movement:**
  - People were asked not just to refuse cooperation but also to **break colonial laws.**
  - Thousands violated the **salt law,** boycotted foreign cloth, and picketed liquor shops.
  - **Peasants refused to pay revenue and chaukidari taxes,** many **village officials resigned,** and forest communities defied restrictions by entering **Reserved Forests.**
- Alarmed, the government arrested Congress leaders one by one, which sparked violent clashes:
  - After the arrest of **Abdul Ghaffar Khan in April 1930,** crowds in **Peshawar** protested against police and armoured cars, leading to deaths.
  - When **Gandhi was arrested in May 1930,** workers in **Sholapur** attacked police stations, courts, municipal buildings, and railway stations.
- The government responded with **brutal repression:** peaceful satyagrahis and even **women and children** were beaten, and about **100,000 people were arrested.**
- Seeing the violence, Gandhi called off the movement and signed the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 March 1931),** agreeing to attend the **Round Table Conference in London** in exchange for the release of political prisoners.
- Gandhi went to **London in December 1931,** but the negotiations failed, and he returned disappointed.
- By then, a new wave of **repression** had begun: **Ghaffar Khan and Jawaharlal Nehru** were jailed, the **Congress was declared illegal,** and meetings and boycotts were banned.
- Gandhi relaunched the **Civil Disobedience Movement,** but by **1934** it had gradually lost momentum.

## How Participants saw the Movement

- In the **countryside, rich peasants** like the **Patidars of Gujarat** and **Jats of Uttar Pradesh** actively supported the Civil Disobedience Movement.
  - Being commercial crop producers, they suffered heavily during the **trade depression** and falling prices.
  - They could not pay the government's high revenue demands and resented the refusal to reduce these rates.
  - For them, **swaraj meant relief from high revenues.**
  - They organised their communities, sometimes forcing reluctant members to join the boycott.
  - When the movement was suspended in **1931** without tax reductions, they felt betrayed and many did not rejoin in **1932.**
- The **poor peasants** (small tenants) were more concerned with **unpaid rents** owed to landlords.
  - As depression deepened, their cash income shrank and they wanted rent remissions.
  - Many joined **radical movements led by Socialists and Communists.**
  - The Congress, wary of alienating rich peasants and landlords, refused to support **'no rent'** campaigns, leaving relations with poor peasants strained.
- Some young nationalists believed **non-violence could not defeat the British.**
  - In **1928,** the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Army (HSRA)** was founded at Ferozeshah Kotla, Delhi.
  - Leaders included **Bhagat Singh, Jatin Das, and Ajoy Ghosh.**
  - They launched daring attacks: in **April 1929, Bhagat Singh** and **Batukeswar Dutta** threw a bomb in the Legislative Assembly; that same year, there was an attempt on Lord Irwin's train.
  - **Bhagat Singh, executed at age 23,** declared he did not glorify violence but sought a **social revolution,** proclaiming **"Inquilab Zindabad!"**
- The **business classes** also played a role.
  - Profiting during **World War I,** they opposed colonial restrictions and wanted protection against imports.
  - They formed the **Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920)** and **FICCI (1927),** led by **Purshottamdas Thakurdas** and **G. D. Birla.**
  - They supported the Civil Disobedience Movement by funding it and boycotting foreign goods, seeing **swaraj as economic freedom from colonial control.**
  - However, after the **failure of the Round Table Conference,** many business groups pulled back, fearing violence, disruption of trade, and the growing socialist influence within Congress.
- The **industrial working class** only partially participated.
  - Large-scale involvement was limited, except in regions like **Nagpur.**
  - Some joined strikes: **railway workers (1930), dockworkers (1932),** and **Chotanagpur tin miners (1930)** who wore Gandhi caps and joined protests.
  - Workers adopted limited parts of the Gandhian programme (like boycott of foreign goods) but Congress hesitated to include full labour demands, fearful of alienating industrialists.
- **Women's participation** was a significant aspect of the movement.
  - During the **Salt March (1930),** thousands of women joined Gandhi, making salt, picketing shops, and participating in marches.
  - Many women were jailed. In cities, they came mostly from **high-caste families,** while in villages they were from **rich peasant households.**
  - Inspired by Gandhi, they began to see **service to the nation as sacred duty,** yet their public role did not lead to equality.
  - Gandhi still believed in their role as good wives and mothers, and the Congress allowed only **symbolic presence,** avoiding giving women positions of real authority.

## The Limits of Civil Disobedience

- Not all groups were equally attracted to the abstract idea of **swaraj.** One such group were the **'untouchables' (dalits),** who by the **1930s** began calling themselves the **oppressed.**
- For long, the **Congress avoided addressing dalit issues,** fearing backlash from **sanatanis (conservative high-caste Hindus).**
- **Mahatma Gandhi,** however, declared that **swaraj would not come for 100 years if untouchability was not abolished.**
  - He called the untouchables **harijan (children of God).**
  - He organised **satyagrahas** to secure their entry into **temples, wells, tanks, roads, and schools.**
  - He himself **cleaned toilets** to dignify the work of the **bhangi (sweepers)** and urged upper castes to repent for the **sin of untouchability.**
- Many dalit leaders, however, preferred **political empowerment** over social reform, seeking:
  - **Reserved seats** in education.
  - **Separate electorates** for dalit members in legislative councils.
- Dalit participation in the Civil Disobedience Movement was limited, especially in **Maharashtra and Nagpur,** where dalit organisations were strong.
- **Dr. B. R. Ambedkar** founded the **Depressed Classes Association in 1930,** demanding **separate electorates** at the **Second Round Table Conference.**
- When the British accepted his demand, Gandhi opposed it, fearing it would **delay integration into society,** and began a **fast unto death in 1932.**
- This resulted in the **Poona Pact (September 1932):**
  - Dalits (Depressed Classes, later Scheduled Castes) were given **reserved seats** in provincial and central legislatures.
  - But they would be elected by the **general electorate,** not separate electorates.
- Despite this, the **dalit movement remained wary of the Congress-led national struggle.**
- Among **Muslims,** response to the Civil Disobedience Movement was also lukewarm.
- After the collapse of the **Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Movement,** many Muslims felt alienated as, from the **mid-1920s,** Congress appeared increasingly close to **Hindu nationalist groups like the Hindu Mahasabha.**
- With rising **Hindu-Muslim communal riots** triggered by militant religious processions, distrust widened.
- In **1927,** Congress and the **Muslim League** tried to negotiate unity.
  - **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** was willing to drop Muslim demands for **separate electorates** if assured:
    - **Reserved seats in the Central Assembly,** and
    - **Proportional representation** in Muslim-majority provinces like Bengal and Punjab.
- However, hopes collapsed at the **All Parties Conference in 1928,** when **M. R. Jayakar** of the **Hindu Mahasabha** strongly opposed compromise.
- Thus, when the **Civil Disobedience Movement** began, it was marked by **mutual suspicion** between Hindus and Muslims.
- Many Muslims, fearing domination by the **Hindu majority,** stayed away, as leaders voiced concerns over the **future identity and culture of minorities.**

## The Sense of Collective Belonging

- **Nationalism** spreads when people begin to see themselves as part of a **single nation,** united by shared struggles and cultural symbols.
- A sense of **collective belonging** developed not only through **united struggles,** but also via **cultural processes** like **history, fiction, folklore, songs, popular prints, and symbols.**
- The nation was often symbolised in a **figure or image,** helping people identify emotionally with the nation.
- In the **20th century,** India came to be represented through the image of **Bharat Mata:**
  - First created by **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay,** who wrote **'Vande Mataram'** in the **1870s** as a hymn to the motherland, later included in Anandamath.
  - During the **Swadeshi movement in Bengal,** the hymn became popular.
  - Inspired by the movement, **Abanindranath Tagore** painted **Bharat Mata,** portrayed as an **ascetic, calm, divine, and spiritual figure.**
  - Over the years, Bharat Mata was depicted in various forms in **popular prints** and **paintings,** with devotion to her seen as a sign of nationalism.
- Nationalists also tried to **revive Indian folklore** to strengthen cultural pride.
  - They believed **folk literature and songs** revealed the **true culture** that had been corrupted by foreign domination.
  - In **Bengal, Rabindranath Tagore** collected ballads, nursery rhymes, and myths.
  - In **Madras, Natesa Sastri** compiled a **four-volume collection of Tamil folklore** titled The Folklore of Southern India, calling it the **national literature** expressing the real spirit of the people.
- **Nationalist symbols** also played a crucial role in unifying people:
  - During the **Swadeshi movement,** a **tricolour flag (red, green, yellow)** was designed with **eight lotuses** for provinces and a **crescent moon** representing **Hindus and Muslims.**
  - By **1921, Gandhiji designed the Swaraj flag,** a **tricolour (red, green, white)** with a spinning wheel in the centre, symbolising **self-reliance (khadi and charkha).**
  - Holding the flag in marches became a strong **symbol of defiance** against colonial rule.
- **Reinterpretation of history** was another way nationalism spread.
  - By the **late 19th century,** Indians began rewriting history to counter the British portrayal of them as uncivilised.
  - They highlighted India's **glorious ancient achievements in art, science, religion, philosophy, crafts, law, and trade,** followed by a period of decline under colonialism.
  - These **nationalist histories** inspired pride in the past and a determination to end the misery of British rule.
- However, these cultural efforts also had **limitations.**
  - Glorification of **Hindu traditions and symbols** often **alienated other communities,** making them feel excluded from the national identity.

## Conclusion

- In the **first half of the 20th century,** growing **anger against colonial rule** united various groups and classes of Indians in the struggle for freedom.
- Under **Mahatma Gandhi's leadership,** the **Congress** attempted to channel these grievances into **organised movements for independence.**
- Nationalists sought to **forge national unity,** but the movement included **diverse groups with varied aspirations and expectations.**
- Since grievances differed – peasants, workers, merchants, industrialists, women, dalits, and tribals each had unique demands – **freedom meant different things to different people.**
- The **Congress tried to balance demands,** ensuring one group's aspirations did not alienate another, but this often proved difficult.
- As a result, phases of **nationalist unity and collective struggle** were frequently followed by **periods of disunity and internal conflict.**
- Thus, the Indian nation that was emerging was one with **many voices,** all united in seeking **freedom from colonialism,** though their **visions of swaraj differed.**
