## Stanza 1: The Asian Lion

```stanza
If ever you should go by chance
To jungles in the east;
And if there should to you advance
A large and tawny beast,
If he roars at you as you're dyin'
You'll know it is the Asian Lion.
```

**Line 1: "If ever you should go by chance"** The poet begins by imagining a situation in which someone happens to visit a jungle. The expression "by chance" means accidentally or without planning it specifically. The poet addresses the reader directly and presents the possibility of encountering a wild animal during a visit to the forest. The tone is conversational, as though the poet is giving the reader advice about identifying wild animals.

**Line 2: "To jungles in the east;"** The poet refers to the jungles of the eastern parts of the world, particularly Asia. She establishes the setting in which the reader might encounter the animal she is about to describe. The reference to the eastern jungles prepares the reader for the identification of the Asian lion.

**Line 3: "And if there should to you advance"** The word advance means to move forward or approach someone. The poet imagines a large wild animal approaching the reader in the jungle. The sentence continues from the previous lines, creating suspense about the identity of the animal. The situation is presented as though the reader is being given a practical lesson in wildlife identification.

**Line 4: "A large and tawny beast,"** The poet describes the approaching animal as large and tawny. The word tawny refers to a yellowish-brown or golden-brown colour. This description refers to the physical appearance of the Asian lion, whose coat is generally sandy or golden-brown. The poet provides two clues to help identify the animal: its large size and its tawny colour.

**Line 5: "If he roars at you as you're dyin'"** The poet humorously suggests that if the animal roars at you while you are dying, you can identify it as the Asian lion. The word roars refers to the loud, powerful sound made by a lion. The phrase "as you're dyin'" introduces dark humour. Instead of warning the reader to escape from the dangerous animal, the poet suggests that the reader will recognise it while facing death. The humour arises because the identification comes at a moment when it may be too late to do anything about the encounter.

**Line 6: "You'll know it is the Asian Lion."** The poet concludes that the animal must be the Asian lion. The word Asian identifies the geographical association of this lion. The stanza uses the lion's tawny colour, large size and powerful roar as identifying characteristics. The humorous twist is that the reader supposedly learns the animal's identity only when the encounter becomes life-threatening.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 1:** The poet explains how to identify the Asian lion. If someone visits an eastern jungle and encounters a large, tawny animal that roars at them, it is likely to be a lion. The poet humorously suggests that the person will recognise the animal even while dying. This exaggeration creates humour and makes the description memorable.

## Stanza 2: The Bengal Tiger

```stanza
Or if some time when roaming round,
A noble wild beast greets you,
With black stripes on a yellow ground,
This may be the Bengal Tiger.
```

**Line 1: "Or if some time when roaming round,"** The poet introduces another possible encounter with a wild animal. The expression "roaming round" means wandering or moving around a place. The poet imagines the reader walking through a jungle or another natural environment when they encounter a wild animal. The stanza continues the poem's humorous attempt to teach readers how to identify dangerous animals.

**Line 2: "A noble wild beast greets you,"** The phrase "noble wild beast" refers to a majestic and impressive wild animal. The word noble suggests dignity, grandeur and an impressive appearance. The word greets is usually associated with a friendly welcome. However, the poet uses it humorously to describe an encounter with a potentially dangerous animal. The tiger is not literally welcoming the reader as a human might welcome a guest. The word makes the encounter sound polite and pleasant, even though it could be extremely dangerous.

**Line 3: "With black stripes on a yellow ground,"** The poet describes the animal's physical appearance. The phrase "yellow ground" refers to the yellowish or golden background colour of the tiger's coat, on which black stripes are visible. The combination of black stripes and a yellow-orange coat is a distinctive identifying feature of the Bengal tiger. Unlike the lion described in the previous stanza, the tiger is identified primarily through its striped appearance.

**Line 4: "This may be the Bengal Tiger."** The poet concludes that the animal may be a Bengal tiger. The word may introduces a slightly playful tone, as though the identification is a cautious guess. The Bengal tiger is a powerful predator known for its strength, sharp teeth and hunting abilities. The humour comes from the contrast between the animal's dangerous nature and the apparently pleasant way in which the poet describes the encounter.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 2:** The poet describes the Bengal tiger as a majestic wild animal with black stripes on a yellowish coat. If someone encounters such an animal while wandering through the jungle, it may be a Bengal tiger. The poet uses the word "greets" humorously, making a potentially dangerous encounter sound like a friendly meeting.

## Stanza 3: The Leopard

```stanza
If strolling forth, a beast you view,
Whose hide with spots is peppered,
As soon as he has leapt on you,
You'll know it is the Leopard.
```

**Line 1: "If strolling forth, a beast you view,"** The poet imagines the reader walking casually through an area when they notice a wild animal. The expression "strolling forth" means walking or moving forward at a relaxed pace. The word view means to see or observe something. The situation begins innocently, with the reader simply walking and observing the surroundings. However, the encounter is about to become dangerous.

**Line 2: "Whose hide with spots is peppered,"** The poet describes the animal's skin as being covered with numerous spots. The word hide refers to the animal's skin. The word peppered means scattered or covered with many small marks, resembling pepper sprinkled over a surface. Leopards have spotted coats that help them camouflage themselves in their natural habitats. These spots are an important physical characteristic that helps distinguish a leopard from other large cats.

**Line 3: "As soon as he has leapt on you,"** The poet humorously imagines the leopard suddenly jumping on the reader. The word leapt means jumped suddenly or forcefully. Leopards are agile predators capable of moving quickly and attacking their prey with considerable force. The poet exaggerates the situation by suggesting that the reader will identify the animal only after it has attacked them. The humour is based on the absurdity of using a dangerous attack as a method of identifying a wild animal.

**Line 4: "You'll know it is the Leopard."** The poet concludes that the animal is a leopard. The leopard's spotted coat is the main identifying feature, but the poet humorously emphasises the attack as though it were the decisive clue. This creates dark humour because the reader might recognise the animal only after being placed in a dangerous situation.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 3:** The poet describes the leopard as a wild animal with a coat covered in spots. She humorously suggests that if a spotted animal suddenly leaps on someone, that person will recognise it as a leopard. The exaggeration creates humour while highlighting the leopard's agility and predatory nature.

## Stanza 4: The Bear

```stanza
'Twill be difficult to say,
If ever you are cross a bear,
He'll give you just one more caress,
If you have any doubts, I guess.
```

**Important Note About the Text:** The standard NCERT text of this stanza is: If when you're walking round your yard / You meet a creature there, / Who hugs you very, very hard, / Be sure it is a Bear. The explanation below follows the standard NCERT version, which is commonly used in CBSE examinations.

**Line 1: "If when you're walking round your yard"** The poet imagines the reader walking around their yard when they unexpectedly encounter a wild animal. The word yard refers to an outdoor area around a house. The setting is humorous because a bear is a wild animal that one would not ordinarily expect to encounter in a domestic yard. The poet presents the encounter as though it were an ordinary everyday situation.

**Line 2: "You meet a creature there,"** The reader encounters an unidentified animal in the yard. The word creature refers to a living being, particularly an animal in this context. The poet delays revealing the animal's identity, creating suspense and preparing the reader for the humorous conclusion.

**Line 3: "Who hugs you very, very hard,"** The poet describes the animal embracing the reader tightly. The repetition of "very" emphasises the strength of the embrace. The word hugs usually suggests affection and friendliness. However, the poet uses it to describe the bear's powerful physical grip. A bear may attack by grabbing or overpowering another animal. The poet humorously describes this dangerous behaviour as though the bear were giving someone an affectionate hug.

**Line 4: "Be sure it is a Bear."** The poet concludes that the animal giving the reader such a powerful embrace must be a bear. The humour arises from the difference between the friendly meaning of a hug and the dangerous reality of being grabbed by a powerful wild animal. The poet presents the bear's strong grip as a clue for identification.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 4:** The poet humorously describes how to identify a bear. If someone encounters an animal that embraces them with tremendous force, they should recognise it as a bear. The description turns a potentially dangerous encounter into a humorous situation by comparing the bear's attack to a friendly hug.

## Stanza 5: The Hyena and the Crocodile

```stanza
Though to distinguish beasts of prey
A novice might nonplus,
The Crocodile you always may
Tell from the Hyena thus:
```

**Line 1: "Though to distinguish beasts of prey"** The poet acknowledges that identifying different wild animals can be difficult, especially for someone without much knowledge of wildlife. The word distinguish means to recognise the differences between things. The phrase beasts of prey refers to animals that hunt and feed on other animals. The poet introduces the challenge of identifying predators and prepares the reader for a comparison between the crocodile and the hyena.

**Line 2: "A novice might nonplus,"** A novice is a beginner or someone with little experience or knowledge of a subject. The word nonplus means to confuse, puzzle or bewilder someone. The poet suggests that an inexperienced person might find it difficult to identify different predators. The unusual word choice adds a playful and slightly formal tone to the poem.

**Line 3: "The Crocodile you always may"** The poet begins explaining how to distinguish a crocodile from a hyena. The word always suggests that the identification method she is about to provide will be reliable. The line continues into the next one, where the poet explains the supposed difference between the two animals.

**Line 4: "Tell from the Hyena thus:"** The poet introduces a comparison between the crocodile and the hyena. The word thus means in this way. The next stanza provides the humorous clue: the poet associates the crocodile with tears and the hyena with laughter.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 5:** The poet acknowledges that identifying wild animals can be difficult for beginners. She then introduces a humorous comparison between the crocodile and the hyena, preparing the reader for the idea that one can distinguish them by their apparent expressions.

## Stanza 6: The Crocodile and the Hyena

```stanza
The Crocodile weeps as it kills,
The Hyena laughs as it devours;
And if you think this sounds quite strange,
Remember that nature has its ways.
```

**Important Note About the Text:** The standard NCERT text is: The Crocodile you always may / Tell from the Hyena thus: / Hyenas come with merry smiles; / But if they weep they're Crocodiles. The explanation below follows the standard NCERT version.

**Line 1: "Hyenas come with merry smiles;"** The poet describes the hyena as an animal that appears to smile. The word merry means cheerful, happy or full of joy. Hyenas can produce vocalisations that sound like laughter, and their facial expressions may sometimes appear similar to a smile. The poet exaggerates this characteristic by presenting the hyena as though it were genuinely happy while behaving as a predator. The description is humorous because human emotions such as happiness are being attributed to an animal's appearance and sounds.

**Line 2: "But if they weep they're Crocodiles."** The poet contrasts the hyena's apparent smile with the crocodile's association with tears. Crocodiles produce tears as part of normal physiological processes. The expression "crocodile tears" is also commonly used to describe insincere sadness. The poet uses this familiar association to suggest that an animal appearing to weep must be a crocodile. The humour depends on the contrast between the two animals' supposed expressions: the hyena smiles, while the crocodile weeps. The poet is not providing a scientifically reliable method of identifying these animals. Instead, she uses familiar associations and exaggeration to entertain the reader.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 6:** The poet humorously distinguishes between the hyena and the crocodile. She associates the hyena with a cheerful smile and the crocodile with tears. This playful comparison makes the animals easy to remember while using human-like expressions to create humour.

## Stanza 7: The Chameleon

```stanza
The true Chameleon is small,
A lizard sort of thing;
He hasn't any ears at all,
And not a single wing.
```

**Line 1: "The true Chameleon is small,"** The poet introduces the chameleon, a small reptile. The word true emphasises that the poet is now describing the actual animal rather than making a humorous comparison based on its expressions. Chameleons are generally small to medium-sized reptiles, although their size varies by species. The line provides a straightforward physical description of the animal.

**Line 2: "A lizard sort of thing;"** The poet describes the chameleon as resembling a lizard. Chameleons belong to a group of reptiles closely related to lizards and share several characteristics with them. The informal expression "sort of thing" gives the description a conversational and slightly humorous tone. The poet keeps the explanation simple, as though she were giving the reader an easy way to recognise the animal.

**Line 3: "He hasn't any ears at all,"** The poet states that the chameleon has no ears. More precisely, chameleons lack external ear openings and external ear structures like those seen in humans. The line is a simplified description rather than a detailed scientific explanation. The poet includes this feature as another clue for identifying the animal.

**Line 4: "And not a single wing."** The poet explains that the chameleon has no wings. This distinguishes it from birds and flying animals. Chameleons are reptiles that generally move by climbing and walking rather than flying. The line completes the description by emphasising the chameleon's physical characteristics.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 7:** The poet describes the chameleon as a small reptile resembling a lizard. It lacks external ears and has no wings. Unlike the earlier stanzas, which rely heavily on exaggerated and dangerous encounters, this stanza offers a comparatively straightforward description of the animal.
