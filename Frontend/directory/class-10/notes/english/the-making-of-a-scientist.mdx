One-paragraph revision of The Making of a Scientist by Robert W. Peterson: how Richard H. Ebright's curiosity, guidance and perseverance shaped him into a scientist.

## Summary

The chapter describes the life and achievements of Richard H. Ebright, a brilliant scientist whose curiosity and determination helped him become a successful researcher. From childhood, Ebright was fascinated by collecting butterflies, rocks, fossils and coins. His mother played a significant role in his development by encouraging his interests, providing him with scientific equipment and helping him explore new ideas.

A major turning point came when she gave him the book The Travels of Monarch X, which introduced him to the scientific study of monarch butterflies and their migration. Inspired by the book, Ebright began tagging butterflies for research and later started raising them to study their life cycle. However, when he entered a science fair in seventh grade and lost, he realised that merely displaying collected specimens was not enough; genuine scientific work required conducting experiments and investigating meaningful questions.

He subsequently carried out several experiments, including research on a disease affecting monarch caterpillars and an investigation into why viceroy butterflies resemble monarch butterflies. His dedication earned him recognition at various science fairs. During high school, Ebright investigated the purpose of the twelve tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa. Working with another student, he demonstrated that these spots produced a hormone essential for the butterfly's development.

His continued research eventually helped him identify the hormone's chemical structure. Later, while examining X-ray photographs of a hormone, he developed a theory explaining how cells read the genetic instructions contained in DNA. He worked with his college roommate, James R. Wong, to develop models illustrating the theory, and they published their findings in a scientific journal. Ebright graduated from Harvard University with high honours and continued his research.

Besides being an outstanding scientist, he was also a skilled debater, public speaker, photographer and outdoor enthusiast. His social studies teacher, Richard A. Weiherer, admired his dedication and willingness to put in extra effort. The chapter concludes that becoming a scientist requires more than intelligence or a passion for collecting information.

## Frequently asked questions

Q: What turned Ebright from a collector into a researcher?

A: Losing at the seventh-grade science fair taught him that displaying specimens is not science; real science needs experiments that test meaningful questions.

Q: What was Ebright's most important early discovery?

A: That the twelve gold spots on a monarch pupa produce a hormone essential for the butterfly's development, research that later led to his theory on how cells read DNA.
