Current through a conductor produces a magnetic field around it. This chapter covers magnets, field lines, fields from straight wires, loops and solenoids, electromagnets, force on conductors, Fleming rule and domestic wiring with earthing and fuses.

## Magnets and magnetic field

- Copper wire wound around an iron nail connected to battery magnetises the nail so it attracts small nails.
- Magnet attracts iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Bar magnet is long and rectangular with two poles.
- Poles near ends have strongest effect. Freely suspended magnet points north-south. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
- Uses: motors, generators, speakers, refrigerator doors, magnetic storage, MRI machines and toys.
- Magnetic field is space around a magnet where its force is felt. Compass needle deflects and iron filings stick due to this force.
- Field has magnitude and direction. Direction at a point is force on a hypothetical north pole there. Compass north shows field direction.

## Magnetic field lines

- Path traced by a freely moving north pole in a magnetic field, also called lines of force.
- Outside magnet they go from north to south. Inside they go from south to north, forming closed continuous curves.
- Compass aligns along tangent to line. Direction from compass south to north shows field direction.
- Closer lines mean stronger field, strongest at poles. Widely separated lines mean weak field.
- No two lines intersect because field at a point cannot have two directions.
- Lines exist in all planes around magnet, not one plane.
- Earth acts as a giant magnet. Magnetic south is near geographical north and magnetic north near geographical south. Axes are inclined about 11.5 degrees, so suspended magnet does not point exactly north-south. Field is believed to come from molten conducting material in outer core.

## Oersted experiment and straight conductors

- Oersted in 1820 discovered electromagnetism. Wire placed parallel to compass needle deflects it when current flows, returns to north-south when switched off. Current produces magnetic field.
- Used in motors, generators, telephones and radios. Reversing current reverses field. Plotting compass can detect hidden current wire.
- Field around straight wire forms concentric circles centred on wire. Direction found with plotting compass.
- Current upward gives anticlockwise field viewed from above. Reversing current reverses direction.
- Strength is directly proportional to current and inversely proportional to distance from wire.

### Right-hand rules

- Maxwell right-hand thumb rule: hold wire in right hand with thumb along current, curled fingers show field direction.
- Corkscrew rule: screw moving along current rotates in field direction.
- Current upward gives anticlockwise field from above. Current downward gives clockwise field.

## Circular loop, solenoid and electromagnet

- Loop produces circular lines near wire that grow larger away. At centre lines are nearly straight and parallel in same direction. Different parts add up to stronger field.
- Strength at centre is directly proportional to current and inversely proportional to radius. Coil with n turns gives about n times field of single turn.
- Increase by raising current, raising number of turns, or reducing radius.
- Clock face rule: loop face with clockwise current acts as south pole, anticlockwise as north pole. Opposite faces have opposite polarity. Also applies to solenoids and electromagnets.
- Solenoid is a long coil of insulated copper wire. Its field resembles a bar magnet with north and south ends. Inside lines are parallel and straight, showing uniform field with same magnitude and direction everywhere.
- Poles found with bar magnet: end repelling north is north, end attracting is south.
- Increase solenoid field by more turns, more current, or soft iron core inside.
- Electromagnet is temporary magnet of coil around soft iron core, magnetic only when current flows. Soft iron magnetises strongly and loses most magnetism when switched off. Steel retains magnetism so is not used.
- Increase by more turns, more current, or smaller air gap between poles. U-shaped magnet is stronger than straight bar type because poles are closer.
- Permanent magnet needs no current, electromagnet needs current. Permanent strength is fixed, electromagnet strength is controllable. Electromagnet can be very strong. Permanent polarity is fixed, electromagnet polarity reverses with current. Permanent magnets use carbon steel, chromium steel, cobalt steel, tungsten steel and Alnico, an alloy of aluminium, nickel, cobalt and iron. Uses include microphones, loudspeakers, clocks, ammeters, voltmeters and speedometers.

## Force on current-carrying conductor

- Conductor in magnetic field experiences mechanical force and can move. This motor principle was shown by Faraday in 1821 and underlies motors and moving-coil galvanometers.
- Direction depends on current direction and field direction.

### Kicking wire experiment

- Suspend thick copper wire vertically with flexible joint, lower end between U-magnet poles, circuit completed through mercury pool.
- Switch on sends current downward, wire moves forward due to magnetic force. Switching off removes force and wire returns.
- Force is perpendicular to both current and field. Reversing either reverses force. Force is maximum when perpendicular to field and zero when parallel.

### Fleming left-hand rule

- Stretch thumb, forefinger and middle finger mutually perpendicular. Forefinger is field, middle finger is current, thumb is force or motion.
- Example: field west to east with current downward gives force towards south. Used in motors and loudspeakers.
- Conventional current is flow of positive charges, opposite to electron flow.
- Reversing current alone or field alone reverses force. Reversing both together keeps force same.

## Domestic electric circuits

- Supply comes from power stations through overhead or underground lines. Two wires enter home: live at high potential and neutral near zero. Example supply is 220 V between them.
- Textbook colours are live red, neutral black, earth green. Modern standards may differ. Never touch live or neutral; faulty wiring can make neutral dangerous.
- Main fuse in live wire protects from excessive current. Meter records energy in kilowatt-hours or units. Main switch turns whole house supply on or off for repairs. Consumer fuse after main switch protects wiring.
- Lighting circuit uses 5 A fuse for bulbs, tube lights, fans, radios and televisions. Power circuit uses 15 A fuse for irons, heaters, geysers, stoves and refrigerators.
- Each distribution circuit has separate fuse so fault in one does not stop others. Circuits are in parallel so each gets full voltage and works independently.
- Earth wire is safety wire giving low-resistance path for leakage current to ground. It connects appliance metal body to buried earthing system.
- Appliances connect in parallel across live and neutral. Each gets full mains voltage about 220 to 230 V in India, switches independently, and others keep working if one fails. Series would divide voltage and stop all if one fails.
- Bulb has one terminal to live through switch, other to neutral. Three-pin socket has live and neutral on lower terminals, earth on upper terminal. Fan has live through switch and regulator, neutral on other terminal. Each appliance has separate switch.
- Switch must always be in live wire so off position disconnects from live supply. Switch in neutral leaves appliance live even when off and risks shock.

## Earthing and fuse

- Shock occurs when current passes through body from live wire or faulty appliance. Damaged live insulation touching metal body can make body live.
- Earthing connects metal body to ground through low-resistance earth wire. Fault current flows to ground and blows fuse or trips breaker, disconnecting supply. It also protects appliances. Irons, heaters, refrigerators and coolers need earthing. Never rely on dry wood for safety; wet wood conducts.
- Fuse is a safety device with short thin tin-plated copper wire of low melting point. It melts and breaks circuit on excessive current. It works on heating effect.
- Thickness and length suit maximum safe current. Fitted near main switch, connected in series so all current passes through it.
- Porcelain holder with brass terminals and removable grip holds wire. Normal current passes intact. Short circuit or overload heats wire until it melts.
- Fuse wire melts before household copper wires and prevents fires. Thin wire has higher resistance so heats and melts faster. Thick wire is unsuitable. Pure copper is unsuitable due to high melting point.
- Rating shows maximum safe current like 5 A, 10 A or 15 A. 10 A wire is thicker than 5 A wire. Textbook example uses 5 A for lighting and 15 A for power. Replace blown fuse only after correcting cause.
- MCB automatically disconnects on excessive current through electromagnet action and can be reset after correction. Fuse works on heating effect, MCB on magnetic effect.
- Appliances like televisions and refrigerators have internal fuses rated slightly above normal current, e.g. 2 A fuse for TV drawing under 1 A, often as thin wire sealed in glass tube with metal caps.

> [!TIP] Exam tip: fuse always in live wire and in series, voltmeter in parallel, ammeter in series — write connections before diagrams.
