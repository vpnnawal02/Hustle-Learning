Offspring resemble parents but show variations. Heredity is transmission of traits through DNA, while variations and natural selection drive evolution. Mendel's pea experiments explain the basic rules.

## Variations and heredity

- Differences between offspring and parents are variations, e.g. height, hair type, facial features.
- Asexual reproduction gives little variation as in bacterial fission. Sexual reproduction gives more variation by combining two parents. Sugarcane from cuttings shows little variation, humans show much variation.
- Useful variations help survival, e.g. bacteria resistant to antibiotics or heat survive better.
- Inheritance passes basic body design plus variations to offspring. Next generations add new DNA-copying variations.
- Natural selection favours individuals with helpful variations to survive and reproduce, contributing to evolution.

## Inherited versus acquired traits

- Similarities are shared features of a species. Variations are differences, e.g. humans share body plan but differ in height and skin colour.
- Earlobes vary: free earlobes hang freely, attached earlobes join the head directly.
- Inherited traits pass through genes, e.g. eye colour and blood group.
- Acquired traits develop in lifetime, e.g. muscles from exercise or scars, and are not inherited because germ-cell DNA is unchanged. Only germ-cell changes can be inherited.

## Mendel's experiments

- Each child gets about half genetic material from each parent, generally two copies of each gene that may be identical or different.
- Mendel crossed pure tall with pure short pea plants. All F1 were tall. Tall is dominant, short is recessive.
- F1 self-pollination gave F2 with about 75 percent tall and 25 percent short, a 3 to 1 phenotypic ratio. Shortness reappeared, so it was hidden in F1.
- Hereditary factors are now called genes, segments of DNA. Versions of a gene are alleles, e.g. T for tall, t for short.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| ---- | ------- | ------- |
| Dominant | Expressed with at least one dominant allele | TT and Tt are tall |
| Recessive | Expressed only with two recessive alleles | tt is short |
| Genotype | Allele combination | TT, Tt, tt |
| Phenotype | Observable trait | Tall or short |
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles | TT or tt |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles | Tt |

- Monohybrid cross TT with tt gives all Tt tall in F1. F1 selfing gives genotypes TT, Tt, Tt, tt, so genotypic ratio 1 to 2 to 1 and phenotypic ratio 3 tall to 1 short.
- Dihybrid cross pure tall round TTRR with pure short wrinkled ttrr gives all TtRr tall round in F1. F1 selfing gives gametes TR, Tr, tR, tr and four trait combinations including new types tall wrinkled and short round.
- Law of independent assortment: alleles of different genes assort independently when genes are not linked. Typical dihybrid phenotypic ratio is 9 tall round to 3 tall wrinkled to 3 short round to 1 short wrinkled.

> [!TIP] Exam tip: memorise 3:1 for monohybrid and 9:3:3:1 for dihybrid with one example each.

## Why pea plants

- Clear contrasting traits like tall and short, round and wrinkled seeds, violet and white flowers.
- Short generation time, natural self-pollination for pure lines, easy manual cross-pollination, many seeds for ratios.

## How traits are expressed

- DNA carries instructions for proteins that control traits. Plant height depends on growth hormone made with help of enzymes.
- Efficient enzyme gives more hormone and taller plant. Less efficient enzyme gives shorter plant.
- Body cells have two chromosome sets, one from each parent, so two copies of each gene. Germ cells have one set through meiosis. Fertilisation restores two sets.
- During meiosis gene copies separate so each germ cell gets one allele. Independent assortment of chromosomes creates new combinations.
- Chromosomes are DNA plus protein threads carrying genes. Homologous chromosomes carry genes for same traits but alleles may differ.
- Humans have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs in body cells, 23 in sperm and egg each.
- Asexual reproduction copies DNA directly with little variation. Sexual reproduction reshuffles genes, plus recombination by exchange between homologous chromosomes.

## Sex determination

- Some reptiles use temperature-dependent sex determination. Some snails can change sex, so sex is not always purely genetic.
- Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes plus one pair of sex chromosomes. Females are XX, males are XY.
- Female eggs all carry X. Male sperms are half X and half Y.
- X sperm plus X egg gives XX female. Y sperm plus X egg gives XY male. Father's sperm decides sex, with about 50 percent probability each time.
- Biological sex development can show variations beyond typical XX and XY patterns.

## Evolution and evidence

- Evolution is gradual change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations, not change of one individual in its lifetime. Darwin proposed natural selection.
- Closely related organisms share more traits and a common ancestor. Humans across regions share ancestry in anatomy and DNA. Evolution branches differently, not always towards complexity.
- Homologous structures share basic plan and origin but differ in function, e.g. forelimbs of humans, whales and bats, showing divergent evolution.
- Analogous structures share function but differ in plan and origin, e.g. wings of birds and insects, showing convergent evolution.
- Fossils are preserved remains, impressions or traces like bones, shells and leaf impressions. Deeper undisturbed layers are older. They show extinct forms and relationships, but the record is incomplete.
- Bacteria and humans both evolved successfully. Bacteria are simpler but well adapted over billions of years. Success means adaptation, not complexity.
- Genetic variation helps populations survive change. Small populations have less variation and more drift and inbreeding risk. Genetic drift is random allele frequency change, strong in small populations.
- Speciation forms new species. Geographical barriers like mountains and rivers can isolate groups, reduce interbreeding, and let mutations, selection and drift diverge them until they cannot interbreed. Germ-cell DNA changes and chromosome number changes can aid isolation.
