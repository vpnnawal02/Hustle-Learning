Life processes — nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion — maintain life in every organism. Plants and animals carry them out differently, but all need continuous energy from food.

> [!NOTE] Molecular movements inside cells maintain life but cannot be seen with the naked eye. Viruses show such activity only inside a host, so their living status is debated.

## What are life processes

- Life processes are basic functions that maintain life: nutrition, respiration, transportation, excretion, control and coordination, growth, movement and reproduction.
- Nutrition is taking in food and converting it into absorbable substances.
- Respiration releases energy from food. Transportation moves substances within the body. Excretion removes wastes.
- Food acts as fuel. Energy is released from food by respiration, even during sleep for heartbeat and circulation.

## Nutrition and modes of nutrition

- Carbohydrates are the most common energy source. Fats provide energy. Proteins help growth and repair. Vitamins, minerals and water support normal functioning.
- Autotrophs prepare their own food from carbon dioxide and water, e.g. green plants and some bacteria. They contain chlorophyll and perform photosynthesis.
- Heterotrophs depend on others for food, e.g. animals, fungi, most bacteria. Enzymes break complex food into simpler substances.

| Type of heterotrophic nutrition | How food is obtained | Example |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------- | ------- |
| Saprotrophic | From dead and decaying matter | Bread mould, mushroom, yeast |
| Parasitic | From a living host without killing it immediately | Cuscuta, Plasmodium, roundworm |
| Holozoic | Taking in solid or liquid food and digesting inside | Humans and most animals |

- Holozoic nutrition has five steps: ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation and egestion.

## Photosynthesis in plants

- Photosynthesis occurs mainly in green leaves. Chlorophyll absorbs sunlight. Carbon dioxide enters through stomata. Water comes from roots via xylem. Oxygen is released as by-product. Excess glucose is stored as starch.

```formula:Photosynthesis
6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (in presence of sunlight and chlorophyll)
```

- Three steps: chlorophyll absorbs light, light energy splits water into hydrogen and oxygen, carbon dioxide is reduced to glucose.
- Conditions needed: sunlight, chlorophyll, carbon dioxide and water.
- Destarching: keep plant in darkness about three days so stored starch is used up before experiments.

### Stomata and water

- Each stomatal pore is guarded by two guard cells. They swell with water to open, shrink to close.
- Carbon dioxide enters and oxygen leaves through stomata. Water vapour is also lost by transpiration.
- Roots absorb water by osmosis and minerals including nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and magnesium from soil. Nitrogen is needed for proteins.
- Site: chloroplasts in mesophyll cells of palisade and spongy layers. Cuticle reduces water loss.

## Nutrition in Amoeba

- Amoeba shows holozoic nutrition by phagocytosis using pseudopodia.
- Steps: ingestion by engulfing food into a food vacuole, digestion by enzymes, absorption into cytoplasm by diffusion, assimilation for energy and growth, egestion by rupturing the membrane.
- Paramecium sweeps food with cilia and follows the same five steps.

## Human digestive system

- Alimentary canal: mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine. Associated glands: salivary glands, liver, pancreas.
- Mouth: teeth cut and grind. Saliva moistens food. Salivary amylase breaks starch into sugars. Tongue mixes and swallows.
- Oesophagus: peristalsis pushes food to stomach.
- Stomach: churns food. Gastric juice has hydrochloric acid, pepsin and mucus. Acid activates pepsin for proteins and kills bacteria. Mucus protects the wall. Sphincter releases food to small intestine.
- Small intestine, about 6.5 m, is the main site of complete digestion and absorption. Herbivores have longer intestine for cellulose; carnivores shorter for meat.
- Liver secretes bile stored in gall bladder: neutralises acid, emulsifies fats.
- Pancreatic juice: pancreatic amylase for starch, trypsin for proteins, lipase for fats.
- Intestinal juice completes digestion to glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol.
- Villi increase surface area for absorption into blood. Blood carries nutrients to cells. Excess glucose is stored as glycogen in liver.
- Large intestine absorbs water. Rectum stores faeces. Egestion occurs through anus controlled by anal sphincter.

> [!TIP] Exam tip: learn bile as no enzyme but two roles — neutralisation and emulsification.

## Teeth care

- Enamel is the hardest substance in the body. Dentine lies below. Pulp cavity has nerves and vessels.
- Bacteria act on sugars to make acids that dissolve enamel and dentine, forming cavities called dental caries.
- Plaque is sticky food plus bacteria that blocks saliva. Brush regularly after meals.

## Respiration and energy

- Respiration releases energy from food in cells, stored as ATP, the energy currency of the cell.

```formula:ATP cycle
ADP + Phosphate + Energy → ATP
ATP → ADP + Phosphate + Energy (about 30.5 kJ per mol)
```

- Breathing is physical intake of oxygen and release of carbon dioxide. Respiration is biochemical breakdown of glucose in mitochondria.
- Glycolysis in cytoplasm breaks glucose into two pyruvate, without oxygen. Fate of pyruvate depends on oxygen.

| Condition | Location | Products | Energy |
| --------- | -------- | -------- | ------ |
| Aerobic, with oxygen | Mitochondria | Carbon dioxide + water | Large |
| Anaerobic in yeast | Cytoplasm | Ethanol + carbon dioxide | Small, 2 ATP |
| Anaerobic in muscle | Cytoplasm | Lactic acid | Small, 2 ATP |

- Lactic acid accumulation causes cramps. Hot bath or massage improves circulation to remove it.

## Respiration in plants and other animals

- All plant parts respire independently, slowly, over short distances by diffusion.
- Roots take oxygen from soil air through root hairs. Waterlogging fills air spaces, causing anaerobic respiration that can kill the plant.
- Soft stems exchange through stomata. Woody stems exchange through lenticels.
- In leaves, oxygen enters through stomata for respiration, carbon dioxide leaves. By day, photosynthesis uses some respiratory carbon dioxide and releases oxygen. At night only respiration occurs.
- Amoeba exchanges gases through cell membrane by diffusion.
- Earthworm breathes through moist skin. Oxygen dissolves in moisture, diffuses to blood, carbon dioxide diffuses out.
- Fish breathe through gills with rich blood supply. Dissolved oxygen diffuses into blood, carbon dioxide diffuses out.

## Human respiratory system

- Path: nostrils, nasal cavity with hairs and mucus, pharynx, trachea with cartilage rings and larynx, bronchi, bronchioles, lungs, alveoli, diaphragm.
- Alveoli are thin-walled sacs with capillaries. Millions give about 80 sq m surface for exchange.
- Oxygen diffuses from alveoli to blood, binds haemoglobin in red blood cells, reaches cells. Carbon dioxide returns mainly as bicarbonate ions and is exhaled.
- Inhalation: rib muscles contract, ribs move up and out, diaphragm flattens, chest volume rises, air enters.
- Exhalation: muscles relax, diaphragm domes up, volume falls, air is pushed out.
- Residual air always remains to allow continuous exchange.
- Normal rate at rest is 15 to 18 breaths per minute, controlled by respiratory centre in brain. Rate rises in exercise.
- Normal haemoglobin about 12 to 18 g per dL. Deficiency causes tiredness and breathlessness.
- Carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion binds haemoglobin strongly as carboxyhaemoglobin and can cause death. Never burn coal in closed rooms.

## Transportation in plants

- Xylem carries water and minerals from roots to leaves. Phloem carries food from leaves to other parts.
- Root cells absorb ions, water enters by osmosis creating root pressure. Main upward pull is transpiration pull from water loss through stomata.
- Translocation through phloem needs ATP. Sucrose is loaded, water enters by osmosis, pressure drives food to low-pressure tissues. Direction can be upward or downward as needed, e.g. stored food to buds in spring.

## Human circulatory system

- Heart, blood and blood vessels form the circulatory system. Lymphatic system also transports lymph.
- Heart is fist-sized cardiac muscle with four chambers: two atria above, two ventricles below. Valves prevent backflow. Septum separates left and right. Pericardium protects it. Ventricles have thicker walls.

| Vessel | Wall | Carries |
| ------ | ---- | ------- |
| Arteries | Thick, elastic | Away from heart, mostly oxygenated. Aorta to body, pulmonary artery to lungs with deoxygenated blood |
| Veins | Thin, with valves | Towards heart, mostly deoxygenated. Vena cava from body, pulmonary veins from lungs with oxygenated blood |
| Capillaries | One cell thick | Exchange of oxygen, nutrients, carbon dioxide and wastes |

- Flow: pulmonary veins to left atrium to left ventricle to aorta to body capillaries to vena cava to right atrium to right ventricle to pulmonary artery to lungs and back.
- Double circulation: blood passes heart twice per cycle. Pulmonary circulation is heart to lungs and back. Systemic circulation is heart to body and back. It keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate.
- Mammals and birds have four chambers for efficient supply as warm-blooded animals. Amphibians and most reptiles have three chambers with some mixing. Fish have two chambers with single circulation: heart to gills to body to heart.

## Lymphatic system

- Network of lymph capillaries, vessels and nodes carrying light-yellow lymph without red blood cells, containing lymphocytes, proteins, fats, germs and cell fragments.
- Lymph from tissue fluid flows one way to subclavian veins near collarbone. Nodes filter germs. Lymphocytes make antibodies.
- Functions: carry fats and proteins to blood, protect against infection, remove dead cell fragments.

## Excretion

- Plants have no special excretory organs. Gaseous wastes leave through stomata and lenticels. Carbon dioxide is released mainly at night, oxygen by day, water vapour by transpiration. Some wastes stored in falling leaves, bark, fruits, as raphides, gums, resins, or released into soil.
- Humans remove carbon dioxide through lungs and urea through kidneys. Urea is made in liver from excess amino acids.
- Excretory system: two kidneys, two ureters, urinary bladder, urethra. Renal artery brings blood, renal vein takes filtered blood.
- Nephron has Bowman's capsule around glomerulus for filtration, and a long tubule for selective reabsorption. Glucose, amino acids, salts and water are reabsorbed. Urea, excess salts and water form urine.
- Urine contains water, salts, urea and some uric acid.
- Kidney failure from infection, injury, high pressure, high sugar or low blood flow causes waste accumulation. Treatment is transplant or dialysis with an artificial kidney where blood passes through selectively permeable tubes in dialysing fluid and urea diffuses out.
