Practice set of exam-pattern questions from Sectors of the Indian Economy. Read each question, frame your answer, then tap it to compare against the structured answer below.

Q: Arrange the following in the correct sequence:
1. Transporting cloth to the workshops
2. Sale in shops and showrooms
3. Spinning the yarn
4. The weaving of the fabric

Q: Reema works as Head Technician at a textiles company while Shirin works as Sales Executive at a fashion showroom. Identify their sectors and evaluate each sector's role in the Indian economy.

A: Reema works in the secondary or manufacturing sector and Shirin in the tertiary or service sector. **Secondary sector:** it converts natural products into other forms through manufacturing, hence called the industrial sector, and it provides large-scale employment and huge revenue for national development. **Tertiary sector:** these activities support primary and secondary production without producing goods themselves, like transporting goods by truck or train, wholesale and retail sale, and godown storage; the service sector contributes the most to the national economy and keeps growing.

Q: With the example of sugarcane, explain the interdependence of all three sectors of the economy.

A: **Primary:** cultivation of sugarcane. **Secondary:** processing sugarcane into sugar. **Tertiary:** transporting cane to factories, moving sugar packs to markets, and wholesale and retail sale. Cultivated cane is the raw material for sugar mills of the secondary sector, vehicles of the tertiary sector carry cane to mills, and milled sugar reaches markets and retailers through the tertiary sector.

Q: Explain the interdependence of all three sectors giving examples from the transportation system.

A: The tertiary sector produces no goods but helps production in the primary and secondary sectors. In the primary sector, transport supplies agricultural inputs and carries food grains to market or raw materials to agro-based industries. In the secondary sector, transport brings raw materials and takes final products to market. Transport also serves the service sector itself, such as medical facilities and fire services.

Q: Distinguish between final goods and intermediate goods.

A: Final goods are used for final consumption and their value is included in GDP, with examples like TV, bread and bakery products. Intermediate goods are used up in producing final goods and services and their value is excluded from GDP, with examples like flour and cotton.

Q: Define GDP. At present which sector has the maximum contribution to India's GDP?

A: Gross Domestic Product is a broad measure of a nation's overall economic activity — the monetary value of all finished goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific time period. At present the tertiary sector contributes the most to India's GDP.

Q: What does the history of developed countries indicate about shifts between sectors?

A: Initially the primary sector dominated, with most goods being natural products. Later, new manufacturing methods and factories shifted people from agriculture to the secondary sector. Still later, employment shifted from primary and secondary work into the service sector, making it the most dominant sector.

Q: Tertiary sector is playing a significant role in the development of the Indian economy. Justify the statement.

A: **Basic services:** hospitals, educational institutions, post and telegraph, transport, banks and insurance. **Development of primary and secondary sectors:** agriculture and industry demand transport, trade and storage services. **Rise in income levels:** richer sections demand eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals and schools, and professional training. **Rise in information technology:** ICT-based services became important over the past decade. **Globalisation:** awareness of new services and activities raised the tertiary sector's importance.

Q: Why has the entire tertiary sector not grown in importance? Explain.

A: The service sector has two kinds of people: highly educated, skilled high earners like doctors, engineers and software professionals, and uneducated, unskilled workers like street vendors and repair persons. Though the sector grew over decades, growth was unequal — skilled workers rose high while the unskilled barely earn a living, performing these services only because no alternative work exists.

Q: Name the sector that is the largest employer in India. Why does this sector produce only a quarter of the national GDP?

A: The primary sector employs nearly 44 per cent of the population yet produces little GDP because land holdings are tiny with low per-holder productivity, farmers use little modern technology, financing and marketing facilities have been insufficient since independence leaving small and marginal farmers without loans and market access, and the absence of alternate rural income avenues creates disguised unemployment with underused labour.

Q: Define underemployment and disguised unemployment.

A: **Underemployment:** working below full potential or capacity, through part-time work while wanting full-time, jobs not using one's skillset, or seasonal work with limited off-season hours. **Disguised unemployment:** a specific underemployment where surplus labour crowds a sector, often agriculture. In short, underemployment concerns job quality while disguised unemployment concerns job quantity.

A remarkable fact about India is that while there has been a change in the share of the three sectors in GDP, a similar shift has not taken place in employment.

Q: Why didn't a similar shift out of the primary sector happen in the case of employment? Substantiate your answer.

A: Secondary and tertiary sectors failed to create adequate jobs despite large output growth. Agriculture suffers underemployment, with more workers than needed, so moving a few out hardly affects production and productivity stays low. Industrial output rose over nine times but industrial employment only about three times; services output rose 14 times but employment about five times. Hence over half the workers remain in primary activity producing about one-sixth of GDP, while secondary and tertiary sectors produce the rest with less than half the workforce.

Q: How can more job opportunities be created in rural areas?

A: Employ people in village construction of dams, canals and roads; provide irrigation for two or three crops a year employing more people; open training and financial-assistance centres for self-employment; invest in transportation and storage services; set up honey collection and fruit and vegetable processing units; and promote cottage industries and services with soft loans and marketing support.

Q: In what ways can employment be increased in urban areas?

A: Encourage foreign investment across manufacturing, technology and services; develop small and medium enterprises as major urban employers; provide vocational training matching in-demand skills; promote services like tourism, retail and healthcare; develop infrastructure for short-term jobs and long-term business ease; encourage entrepreneurship including the informal sector; and improve credit access for entrepreneurs and small businesses.

Q: Ravi works in a leather bag manufacturing and export company and feels insecure under flexible labour laws. How can the government help him?

A: Flexible labour laws mean short-period hiring, heavy pressure with overtime, and no work without demand. The government can set laws ensuring companies protect worker welfare, check that labour laws are properly implemented, and create alternative employment opportunities.

Q: Explain NREGA 2005.

A: The central government enacted the Right to Work in about 625 districts as the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005. All rural people able to work and needing it are guaranteed 100 days of yearly employment, with unemployment allowances if the government defaults. Works that will raise future land production get preference under the Act.

Q: Rohan works as a bank clerk while Sumit works as a construction labourer. Compare their employment conditions and judge each sector. OR Compare organised and unorganised sector employment. OR Explain working conditions in the unorganised sector.

A: Rohan is in the organised sector and Sumit in the unorganised sector. Organised units are government-registered, big, follow labour laws, use formal processes, offer employment security, fixed hours with overtime pay, and benefits like paid holidays, medical facilities, safe environments and provident fund. Unorganised units are unregistered, small and scattered, ignore labour laws, use no formal processes, offer no security, no fixed hours or overtime pay, and no such benefits.

Q: Agriculture is an unorganised sector in India. Do you agree? Justify with examples.

A: Yes: there are no fixed working hours and no job security, government labour protections go unfollowed, labourers are exploited with unfair wages, and they get nothing beyond daily wages, sometimes doing unpaid overtime.

Q: Distinguish between the public and private sectors. OR Classify the economy by ownership of enterprises.

A: The public sector is government-controlled, owning most assets and services, motivated by public benefit, funded through taxes, with examples like police, army, health and Indian Railways. The private sector is owned by individuals or companies holding assets and services, motivated by profit, paid for directly, with examples like Reliance Company Limited and TISCO.

Q: Though public and private sectors coexist, the public sector plays a leading role. Explain. OR Public sector contributes to India's economic development. Justify.

A: Government-owned, it controls the economy for general benefit, creates employment, generates financial resources for development, and ensures income equality with balanced regional development. Its huge-cost activities still serve at lower rates, its infrastructure investment paves agricultural and industrial development, and it encourages small, medium and cottage industries.
