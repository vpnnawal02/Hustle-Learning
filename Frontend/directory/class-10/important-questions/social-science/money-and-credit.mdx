Practice set of exam-pattern questions from Money and Credit. Read each question, frame your answer, then tap it to compare against the structured answer below.

Q: What do you understand by the double coincidence of wants?

A: When both parties in a barter economy agree to sell and buy each other's commodities, it is known as a double coincidence of wants.

Q: What is the barter system? What are the limitations of the barter system?

A: In the barter system, goods, property and services are exchanged for other goods without money. Its limitations are that both parties must agree to buy and sell each other's commodities, valuation of all goods cannot be done easily, and certain products cannot be divided.

Q: What is money? How does money eliminate the need for a double coincidence of wants? Why is money called a medium of exchange?

A: Money is a medium of exchange widely accepted in transactions for goods and services, taking forms like currency, coins, bank deposits and digital currency. Money acts as an intermediate in the exchange process, eliminating the need for a double coincidence of wants — which is why it is called a medium of exchange.

Q: What are the two forms of modern currency? Why is modern currency accepted as a medium of exchange?

A: Paper notes and coins are the two forms of modern currency. It is accepted because it is authorised by the government, the law legalises rupees in India as a medium of payment that cannot be refused, and the Reserve Bank of India issues currency notes on behalf of the government.

Q: What are demand deposits? How is money safe in banks? Explain.

A: Deposits in bank accounts which can be withdrawn on demand are called demand deposits. Banks accept deposits from many people, lend out part of the money and keep the rest for payments, so the money is safe with banks and depositors can withdraw whenever they want.

Q: How are deposits with banks beneficial for an individual as well as for the nation? Explain with examples.

A: Deposits ensure safety of money and earn interest, can be withdrawn on demand, and allow cheque payments that transfer money directly between banks without cash. Banks extend loans from these deposits, mediating between surplus holders and those needing funds, and since deposits are white money, the nation's economy becomes more transparent.

Q: What is a cheque?

A: A cheque is a paper instructing the bank to pay a specific amount from a person's account to the person in whose name the cheque is issued.

Q: How do the deposits with banks become their source of income? OR Business is all about solving people's problems at a profit. Explain how banks function like a business.

A: People with extra cash open bank accounts and deposit surplus money. Banks keep about 15 per cent as minimum cash balance for daily withdrawals and use a major portion of deposits to extend loans. They charge higher interest on loans than they pay on deposits, and this difference between borrowing and deposit rates is the banks' main source of income.

Q: Why is credit a crucial element in economic development? OR Why is cheap and affordable credit important for a country?

A: Credit meets ongoing production expenses, increases earnings and encourages investment in agriculture, business and small industries, and helps complete production on time. Cheap credit ends the vicious cycle of debt traps, and cheap, easy credit inspires better technology investment and increases competition.

Q: State the potential negative economic implications in an economy without the concept of credit.

A: Limited access to funds for starting businesses or investments, limited capacity of savings, limited ability to finance education and skill development, limited ability to purchase high-worth items like cars and houses, and limited financial flexibility and convenience.

Q: Explain the meaning of debt trap with an example.

A: A debt trap is a cycle of debt a borrower cannot escape: high interest, fees or obligations force fresh borrowing to meet existing dues, spiralling into distress. For example, the small farmer Swapna borrowed for cultivation, faced crop failure, borrowed again for pesticides, yet production could not repay the loans — she was caught in a debt trap.

Q: What do you mean by collateral? Why do banks ask for collateral while giving loans?

A: Collateral is an asset the borrower owns, such as land, building, vehicle, livestock or bank deposits, pledged as guarantee until the loan is repaid. If the borrower defaults, the lender may sell the collateral to recover payment.

Q: What are the main terms of credit?

A: Interest rate, collateral, documentation requirement and mode of repayment are the main terms of credit.

Q: A bank is a place that will lend you money if you can prove that you do not need it. Justify with reference to banks' security requirements.

A: Every loan specifies interest plus principal, and lenders may demand collateral to secure their money — an owned asset pledged as guarantee, saleable on default. Interest rate, collateral, documentation, time period and mode of repayment together form the terms of credit, which vary loan to loan.

Q: Amrita (government employee, rich urban household) and Rani (construction helper, poor rural household) both face a crisis and seek loans. Who gets a formal-sector loan, and why?

A: Amrita gets the formal loan because she can complete the required documentation, fulfil the terms of credit, and assure repayment through salary EMIs.

Q: Explain the significance of the Reserve Bank of India in the Indian economy.

A: The RBI issues currency notes on behalf of the Central Government, supervises formal lending, and requires banks to maintain minimum cash balances, which it monitors. It ensures banks lend not only to profit-making businesses and traders but also to small cultivators, small-scale industries and small borrowers, collecting periodic information on lending volumes, recipients and interest rates.

Q: In what ways does the RBI supervise the functioning of banks?

A: The RBI monitors that banks maintain the required cash balance, ensures they lend to small cultivators, small-scale industries and small borrowers besides profit-making businesses, and collects periodic reports on lending volumes, recipients and interest rates.

Q: Compare the conditions for loans from formal and informal sources. Suggest the best alternative source for the rural poor.

A: **Formal sources** like banks and cooperatives charge low interest, use no unfair recovery means, are RBI-supervised, and follow terms of credit with collateral, interest, payment mode and documents. **Informal sources** like moneylenders, friends and relatives charge high interest, use unfair measures, are unsupervised, and impose conditions like labour at harvest time. Self-help groups are the best alternative source for the rural poor.

Q: The credit activities of the informal sector should be discouraged. Support with arguments.

A: No organisation supervises informal lenders, who charge very high interest and use unfair recovery means. High borrowing costs consume borrowers' earnings leaving little income, and repayment amounts can exceed income, trapping borrowers in debt.

Q: Why do most rural households still remain dependent on informal sources of credit? OR Describe circumstances driving individuals to informal credit.

A: Banks are scarce in rural areas, rural people struggle with documentation, and absence of collateral blocks bank loans for the poor. Richer households give easy informal loans, and moneylenders lend even without clearing earlier dues.

Q: Why do we need to expand formal sources of credit in India? OR Why are formal sources preferred? OR Why must banks and cooperatives increase rural lending?

A: Formal credit saves people from informal exploitation and dependence, charges low interest, raises income through cheap borrowing, and prevents debt traps. It provides cheap, affordable credit crucial for development, RBI-supervised towards small cultivators and borrowers, and must be distributed equally so the poor benefit from cheaper loans.

Q: Imagine you are XYZ, a member of a women's Self-Help Group. Analyse how your group provides loans to members.

A: Self-Help Groups pool savings, typically with 15 to 20 neighbourhood members meeting and saving regularly, from Rs. 25 to Rs. 100 or more per member. Members take small loans from the group at interest below moneylender rates, and after a year or two of regular savings the group becomes eligible for bank loans.

Q: Explain the role of Self-Help Groups (SHGs) in rural society.

A: Members take small group loans at lower interest, groups become bank-eligible after a year or two, and the group owns loan repayment with serious follow-up of defaults. SHGs overcome the collateral problem, give timely reasonable-interest loans for varied purposes, make women financially self-reliant, and their meetings provide a platform for health, nutrition and domestic-violence issues.

Q: How do Self-Help Groups help borrowers overcome the lack of collateral? Explain.

A: People get timely loans for varied purposes at reasonable interest, backed by regular group savings, with small collateral-free loans and serious peer follow-up of defaults. Hence banks lend to SHG-organised poor women despite no collateral, and timely repayment brings further bank loans to SHGs.
