Practice set of exam-pattern questions from Gender, Religion and Caste. Read each question, frame your answer, then tap it to compare against the structured answer below.

Q: What is the state religion of Sri Lanka?

A: Buddhism is the state religion of Sri Lanka.

Q: At which level of government in India are one-third of seats reserved for women?

A: One-third of seats are reserved for women in Local Self Government bodies, that is, Panchayats and Municipalities.

Q: Name three important leaders who worked for the elimination of caste inequalities in India.

A: Jotiba Phule, Mahatma Gandhi and Dr. B. R. Ambedkar worked for the elimination of caste inequalities in India.

Q: Define a feminist.

A: A feminist is a person who believes in equal rights and opportunities for all human beings.

Q: What was the feminist movement? Explain the political demands of the feminist movement in India.

A: The feminist movement was a radical movement that demanded equality for women in personal and family life apart from politics, society and the educational field. Its political demands in India are more women representatives in local government, more political representation for women through elected women representatives, and reserving at least one-third of the seats in the Lok Sabha and Legislative Assemblies for women.

Q: What is the basis of gender division in society?

A: Gender division is based on social expectations and stereotypes.

Q: The blank includes those communities that led a secluded life usually in hills and forests and did not interact much with the rest of society.

A: The Scheduled Tribes includes those communities that led a secluded life usually in hills and forests and did not interact much with the rest of society.

Q: Gandhiji believed that politics must be guided by the ethics drawn from different blank.

A: Gandhiji believed that politics must be guided by the ethics drawn from different religions.

Q: Social divisions based on blank are peculiar to India.

A: Social divisions based on castes are peculiar to India.

Q: Blank division is a form of social division seen everywhere but rarely recognised.

A: Gender division is a form of social division seen everywhere but rarely recognised.

Q: What do you mean by the sexual division of labour?

A: The sexual division of labour is a system in which all work inside the home is either done by the women of the family or organised by them through domestic helpers.

Q: Shruti's web search for teacher showed mostly women while pilot showed mostly men. Discuss how these results reflect societal perceptions and the sexual division of labour, with an example.

A: The over-representation of women as teachers and men as pilots reinforces traditional notions of job roles: teaching is considered a caregiving job suitable for women, while a pilot's role is regarded as a physically and mentally demanding job ideal for men. Similarly, nursing is associated more with women and engineering with men. These trends represent social perceptions and perpetuate the sexual division of labour.

Q: Elaborate on the political representation of women in India. OR What is the status of women's representation in India's legislative bodies?

A: In India, the proportion of women in the legislature has been very low: in the Central Legislature, elected women members in the Lok Sabha touched 14.36 per cent of total strength for the first time in 2019, while in State Legislatures women form less than 5 per cent of total strength. In local government, one-third of seats are reserved for women in Panchayats and Municipalities, and India is among the bottom group of nations in the world in this aspect. Women's organisations and activists have demanded at least one-third reservation in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies, and in 2023 the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women's Reservation Act, 2023) was passed, giving 33 per cent reservation of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly.

Q: How are religious differences expressed in politics?

A: Gandhiji used to say that religion can never be separated from politics, believing politics must be guided by ethics drawn from religion. Human rights groups have argued that most victims of communal riots are from religious minorities, demanding special steps to protect them. The women's movement has argued that family laws of all religions discriminate against women, demanding that the government change these laws to make them more equitable.

Q: What are the various ways in which women in India face disadvantage, discrimination and oppression?

A: The literacy rate among women is only 54 per cent compared to 76 per cent among men, as many parents spend resources only on sons, and the proportion of women in highly paid and valued jobs is very low. Though the Equal Wages Act mandates equal pay for equal work, women are paid less than men in sports, cinema, agriculture and factories. Girl children are aborted before birth as parents prefer sons, and such sex-selective abortion has driven the child sex ratio down to merely 919. Women face harassment, exploitation and violence inside and outside their homes in both rural and urban areas.

Q: Communalism can take various forms in politics. Explain.

A: The most common expression of communalism is in everyday beliefs involving religious prejudices and stereotypes of religious communities. A communal mind often leads to a quest for political dominance of one's own religious community. Political mobilisation on religious lines uses sacred symbols, religious leaders and emotional appeal to bring followers of one religion together in the political arena. Sometimes communalism takes its ugly form of communal violence, riots and massacre.

Q: How can the relationship between politics and religion be beneficial and problematic at the same time? Explain.

A: **Beneficial:** religion can make politics value-based, religious communities can politically express their needs and interests, and political authority can monitor religious discrimination and oppression. **Problematic:** political parties try to make gains by pitting one group against another, state power may establish domination of one religious group over another, communalism leads to violence and riots, it can disintegrate a country, and it distorts the secular values of the country.

Q: Why is communal thinking flawed?

A: People of one religion do not have the same interests and aspirations, and there are many voices inside every community, all with a right to be heard. Therefore, any attempt to bring all followers of one religion together in a context other than religion is bound to suppress many voices within that community.

Q: Mention the constitutional provisions that make India a secular state.

A: There is no official religion of the Indian state, and the Constitution gives no special status to any religion. It provides all individuals and communities the freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion, or not to follow any, and it prohibits discrimination on the grounds of religion. It also allows the state to intervene in matters of religion to ensure equality within religious communities.

Q: What are the various forms that caste takes in politics?

A: When parties choose election candidates, they keep in mind the caste composition of the electorate. Political parties and candidates make appeals to caste sentiments to muster support. Universal adult franchise and the principle of one-person-one-vote compelled political leaders to gear up for mobilising and securing political support.

Q: The focus on caste in politics can sometimes give an impression that elections are all about caste and nothing else. Do you agree? Explain.

A: No, this is not true. No parliamentary constituency has a clear majority of one single caste, so every candidate and party must win the confidence of more than one caste and community. No party wins the votes of all voters of a caste, many parties may field candidates from the same caste, and some voters have several candidates from their caste while many have none. The ruling party and the sitting MP or MLA frequently lose elections, which could not happen if castes were frozen in their political preferences.

Q: Caste has not still disappeared from contemporary India. Write any three examples to justify the statement.

A: Even now most people marry within their own caste or tribe, and untouchability has not ended completely despite constitutional prohibition. The effects of centuries of advantage and disadvantage are still felt: caste groups with access to education under the old system have done well in modern education too, while groups denied education have naturally lagged behind.

Q: It is not politics that gets caste-ridden, it is the caste that gets politicised. Explain.

A: Each caste group tries to become bigger by incorporating neighbouring castes or sub-castes earlier excluded from it. Various caste groups must enter coalitions with other castes or communities. New kinds of caste groups like backward and forward caste groups have come up in the political arena.

Q: How have caste and the caste system undergone changes in modern India? OR Explain the socio-economic changes responsible for breaking down the old notion of caste hierarchy in India.

A: Social reformers like Jotiba Phule, Mahatma Gandhi and Periyar Ramaswamy Naicker carried out movements to end caste inequalities. Economic development and urbanisation have broken caste hierarchies, and growth of literacy, education and occupational mobility have changed mindsets towards caste. The Constitution prohibits caste-based discrimination, and practising untouchability is a punishable offence in India.

Q: What are some negative aspects of caste in politics?

A: Caste politics leads to tension and violence, politics based on caste identity alone is not healthy in a democracy, and it can divert attention from pressing issues like poverty, development and corruption.
