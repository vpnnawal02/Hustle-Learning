Q: Explain how water becomes a renewable resource.

A: Freshwater is mainly obtained from surface runoff and groundwater that is continually being renewed and recharged through the hydrological cycle. All water moves within the hydrological cycle, ensuring that water is a renewable resource.

Q: Why does the availability of water resources vary over space and time?

A: The availability of water resources varies over space and time mainly due to the variations in seasonal and annual precipitation.

Q: Which state has a 200-year-old system of tapping stream and spring water by using bamboo pipes?

A: Meghalaya has a 200-year-old system of tapping stream and spring water by using bamboo pipes.

Q: Describe the Atal Bhujal Yojana (Atal Jal), including the states and regions it covers, its objectives, the percentage of water-stressed blocks it targets, and the key behavioural changes it aims to promote in communities.

A: **Implementation:** the scheme covers 8,220 water-stressed Gram Panchayats and is implemented in seven states — Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh. **Coverage:** the selected states represent 37 per cent of India's total water-stressed blocks, including over-exploited, critical and semi-critical blocks. **Key focus:** it promotes smart water management practices and aims to shift community behaviour from water consumption to conservation.

Q: Multipurpose projects and large dams have come under great scrutiny and opposition. Explain with reasons.

A: Regulating and damming rivers affects their natural flow, causing poor sediment flow and excessive sedimentation at the bottom of the reservoir, resulting in rockier stream beds and poorer habitats for the rivers' aquatic life. Dams also fragment rivers, making it difficult for aquatic fauna to migrate, especially for spawning. Dams have triggered floods due to sedimentation in the reservoir and the release of excess water during heavy rains, and multipurpose projects have induced earthquakes and caused water-borne diseases and pests, besides pollution resulting from excessive use of water.

Q: How is agriculture responsible for creating stress on water resources? Suggest any one solution for it.

A: To facilitate higher food-grain production, water resources are being over-exploited to expand irrigated areas for dry-season agriculture, and irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer of water. Most farmers have their own wells and tube wells for irrigation to increase productivity, which leads to falling groundwater levels, adversely affecting water availability and food security. Adopting efficient methods like drip irrigation is one solution to this stress.

Q: What are the diversion channels of the Western Himalayas called?

A: The diversion channels of the Western Himalayas are called guls or kuls.

Maharashtra is a state located in western India, with a population of over 110 million people. The state is home to several large cities, including Mumbai, and has a significant agricultural sector. However, the state is facing a severe water crisis, with its water resources coming under increasing pressure due to climate change, industrialization, and urbanization. The main challenges faced by water resource management in Maharashtra are: I. Overexploitation of groundwater: Maharashtra is one of the most groundwater-stressed states in India, with the demand for water exceeding the supply. Overexploitation of groundwater for agriculture and urban use has led to a decline in water levels, which has severe implications for the sustainability of water resources. II. Pollution of surface water: Industrialization and urbanization have led to the pollution of surface water bodies such as rivers and lakes. The pollution has led to water quality degradation, which poses risks to human health and the environment. III. Inefficient irrigation practices: The agricultural sector is the largest user of water in Maharashtra, accounting for around 80% of total water use. However, traditional irrigation practices such as flood irrigation are inefficient and lead to the wastage of water.

Q: Case-Based Question: (i) Mention any two reasons for the water crisis faced by the state of Maharashtra. (ii) Despite being the second-highest rainfall-receiving state of the country, Maharashtra still faces a water crisis. Substantiate this statement in 40 words. (iii) Propose any one solution to mitigate the water crisis faced by Maharashtra state.

A: (i) Two reasons for Maharashtra facing a water crisis are over-exploitation of groundwater and pollution of surface water bodies due to industrialization and urbanization. (ii) Despite receiving the second-highest rainfall in the country, traditional irrigation practices like flood irrigation lead to water shortages in Maharashtra. This is because flood irrigation involves excessive water use, and the water gets lost due to runoff, leading to less water available for other uses. (iii) To mitigate the water crisis in Maharashtra, one solution could be to promote the adoption of more efficient irrigation practices, such as drip irrigation and sprinkler systems, that use less water and are more targeted in their delivery. The state can also use a rainwater harvesting system to improve groundwater levels along the western side of the Western Ghats which receive maximum rainfall. This will increase the efficiency of water use in the state of Maharashtra.

Q: Intensive industrialisation and urbanisation exerted pressure on existing freshwater resources. Justify the statement with suitable examples.

A: Post-independent India witnessed intensive industrialisation and urbanisation, and the ever-increasing number of industries has exerted pressure on existing freshwater resources. Industries, apart from being heavy users of water, also require power to run them, much of which comes from hydroelectric power — today hydroelectric power contributes approximately 22 per cent of India's total electricity. Multiplying urban centres with large dense populations and urban lifestyles have added to water and energy requirements, and most housing societies in cities have their own groundwater pumping devices, resulting in over-exploitation of fragile water resources.

Q: The Hirakud project in the blank basin integrated the conservation of water with flood control.

Q: How are multipurpose projects and large dams the cause of social movements?

A: Multipurpose projects and large dams have caused movements like the Narmada Bachao Andolan and the Tehri Dam Andolan, with resistance driven primarily by large-scale displacement of local communities. Local people often had to give up their land, livelihood and their meagre access and control over resources for the greater good of the nation, yet they benefit little from these projects — the beneficiaries are landowners and large farmers, industrialists and a few urban centres.

Q: Who proclaimed dams as the temples of modern India? Why?

A: Jawaharlal Nehru proclaimed dams as the temples of modern India because they integrate the development of agriculture and the village economy with rapid industrialisation and growth of the urban economy. They provide water for irrigation and electricity generation, water supply for domestic and industrial uses, flood control, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding.

Q: What is the need for rainwater harvesting?

A: Rainwater harvesting is needed to provide water for agriculture, collect drinking water, irrigate fields and moisten the soil. It is a viable alternative, both socio-economically and environmentally, to multipurpose projects.

Q: What is a multipurpose river valley project? Mention any four objectives of it.

A: A project where many uses of the impounded water are integrated with one another is known as a multipurpose project. It is built for irrigation, electricity generation, water supply for domestic and industrial uses, flood control, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding.

Q: What is water scarcity and what are its main causes?

A: Water scarcity is the lack of freshwater resources to meet the demands of water usage within a region. Its main causes are over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access to water among different social groups; a large and growing population with greater demands and unequal access; over-exploitation of water to expand irrigated areas for dry-season food-grain production; pressure from intensive industrialisation and urbanisation; and pollution of available water by domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers used in agriculture.

Q: What are the broad objectives of the Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchai Yojana (PMKSY)?

A: The objectives are to increase irrigated area so that every field gets water (Har Khet Ko Pani), to boost efficiency and improve water use to avoid wastage, to promote technologies like drip irrigation (Per Drop More Crop), and to adopt sustainable water-saving conservation practices.

Q: Explain the working of underground tanks as a part of the rooftop rainwater harvesting system practised in Rajasthan. OR What do you understand by Palar Pani? How is Palar Pani collected?

A: Rainwater is commonly referred to as Palar Pani in the arid and semi-arid regions of Rajasthan. In the semi-arid and arid regions, particularly in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, almost all houses traditionally had underground tanks for storing drinking water, some as large as a big room. The tanks were part of a well-developed rooftop rainwater harvesting system, connected to the sloping roofs through a pipe. Rain falling on rooftops travels down the pipe into these underground tanks. The first spell of rain was usually not collected, as it cleaned the roofs and pipes, and the rainwater from subsequent showers was collected.

Q: Analyse the importance of rooftop rainwater harvesting in Rajasthan.

A: It was commonly practised to store drinking water, and rainwater can be stored in tanks till the next rainfall, making it an extremely reliable source of drinking water when all other sources dry up, particularly in summers. Rainwater, or Palar Pani, is considered the purest form of natural water. Many houses construct underground rooms adjoining the tanks to beat the summer heat since it keeps the room cool, and some houses still maintain tanks since they do not like the taste of tap water.

Q: Describe any three traditional methods of rainwater harvesting adopted in different parts of India.

A: In hill and mountainous regions, people built diversion channels like the guls or kuls of the Western Himalayas for agriculture. Rooftop rainwater harvesting was commonly practised to store drinking water, particularly in Rajasthan. In the flood plains of Bengal, people developed inundation channels to irrigate fields, and in semi-arid and arid regions, agricultural fields were converted into rain-fed storage structures that allowed water to stand and moisten the soil, like the khadins in Jaisalmer and johads in other parts of Rajasthan.

Q: Compare the advantages and disadvantages of multipurpose river valley projects.

A: **Advantages:** they provide water for irrigation and electricity generation, water supply for domestic and industrial uses, flood control, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding. **Disadvantages:** regulating and damming rivers affects natural flow with poor sediment flow and excessive reservoir sedimentation, harming aquatic habitats; dams fragment rivers and block fauna migration for spawning; they trigger floods through reservoir sedimentation and excess releases during heavy rains; they induce earthquakes and cause water-borne diseases, pests and pollution from excessive water use; irrigation shifts cropping patterns to water-intensive commercial crops with ecological consequences like soil salinisation; dams widen the social gap between richer landowners and the landless poor; and they create inter-state water disputes over sharing costs and benefits.

Q: What is bamboo drip irrigation? Mention any two features of it.

A: The bamboo drip irrigation system is a 200-year-old system of tapping stream and spring water using bamboo pipes and transporting water from higher to lower regions with the help of gravity. Its features are that 18 to 20 litres of water enter the bamboo pipe system, travel over hundreds of metres and finally reduce to 20 to 80 drops per minute at the plant; the flow is controlled by manipulating pipe positions; and where pipes pass a road, they are taken high above the land.
