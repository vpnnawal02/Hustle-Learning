Q: Which age group of children is included in calculating Net Attendance Ratio?

A: Children in the age group of 14 and 15 years are included in calculating Net Attendance Ratio.

Q: What is development? What are two aspects of development?

A: Development is a situation that can fulfil the aspirations or desires of people; it is the progress or improvement in the lifestyle of the people. Two aspects are that different people can have different development goals, and what may be development for one may not be development for others.

Q: State any two goals of development other than income.

A: Freedom and security are two goals of development other than income.

Q: Why do different people have different notions of development? Explain.

A: Life situations of people are different, so people see the things most important to them that can fulfil their aspirations. For example, the development goal of a boy from a rich urban family would be admission to a reputed college, whereas the goal of a girl from a rich urban family would be as much freedom as her brother.

Q: Conflicting goals can be development goals. Elaborate with examples.

A: At times two people or groups seek conflicting things: a girl expects as much freedom and opportunity as her brother, including his share in household work, which her brother may not like. Similarly, industrialists may want more dams for electricity, but this may submerge land and disturb displaced people such as tribals.

Q: Explain how the development goals and aspirations of landless rural labourers, prosperous farmers and poor rain-dependent farmers are different.

A: **Landless rural labourers** want more days of work and better wages, local schools giving quality education to their children, and no social discrimination so they too can become village leaders. **Prosperous farmers** want assured high family income through higher support prices for crops, hardworking and cheap labour. **Rain-dependent farmers** mainly want irrigation facilities, crop insurance at less premium, and water facilities for themselves and their children.

Q: Give examples to prove that there are other important development goals than income.

A: For rich farmers: high support prices, cheap labour and subsidised inputs. For landless rural labourers: more work days, schools for children and no social discrimination. For Adivasis: no social discrimination, year-round employment, school education and a PDS shop in the village. For urban youth: higher education opportunity, an office job and a self-owned house. For an urban girl from a rich family: freedom to choose her profession and pursue higher studies abroad.

Q: Average income is an important criterion for development. Justify.

A: Income is considered one of the most important attributes for comparing countries' development. Countries with higher incomes are more developed than those with less income, based on the understanding that more income means more of all the things human beings need.

Q: Money in your pocket may not buy all goods and services you need to live well. Is it true? Elucidate.

A: Money cannot buy a pollution-free environment, nor a disease-free life with protection from infectious diseases. Besides money, people want equal treatment in society, freedom, dignity and honour in their lives, which money cannot buy.

Q: National development of a country depends on the availability of public facilities. How?

A: Public facilities are provided by the government instead of individuals or private sources, and may be highly subsidised or free. They matter because many lack income for private facilities, facing inadequate healthcare, poor nutrition and lack of education. The public distribution system (PDS) gives fair-quality food grains and essentials to weaker sections at subsidised prices, and government hospitals and dispensaries provide outpatient and hospitalisation facilities to all at subsidised rates.

Q: In what respects is the criterion used by the UNDP for measuring development different from the one used by the World Bank?

A: The UNDP compares on the basis of literacy rate, gross enrolment ratio and health status, ranks countries by development level, and is a wider concept including things besides income. The World Bank compares on the basis of per capita income, divides countries into rich or high-income, middle-income or developing, and poor or low-income countries, and is a narrow concept.

Q: What is the main norm used by the World Bank in classifying countries as rich and poor? What are the restrictions of such norms?

A: The main norm is per capita income. Its restrictions are that average income hides disparity, does not reveal income distribution among people, and is just a material criterion of comparison.

Q: Mention the formula to calculate BMI (Body Mass Index).

A: Body Mass Index is a person's weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres.

Q: Sunita (25, 1.45 m, 36 kg) and Nisha (same age and height, 48 kg): what can be said about their nutritional condition?

A: Sunita's BMI is 36 divided by 1.45 squared, that is 36 divided by 2.1025, equal to 17.12 kg per sq. m — she is underweight. Nisha's BMI is 48 divided by 2.1025, equal to 22.83 kg per sq. m — she is in the normal weight category.

Q: What are the criteria used by the UNDP for classifying countries? OR Explain HDI and its three components.

A: The UNDP uses the Human Development Index, a composite index of a nation's achievements in longevity, knowledge and standard of living or quality of life. HDI is calculated on per capita income, obtained by dividing total national income by total population; life expectancy, the average expected length of life at birth; and gross enrolment ratio at three levels — primary, secondary and higher education beyond secondary.

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and Per Capita Income: India's GDP was USD 2.7 trillion in 2020, making it the world's sixth-largest economy. However, the per capita income in India is only about USD 1,947 per year, which is much lower than the world average of USD 11,570. This means that the overall economic output in India is high, but the benefits are not evenly distributed among the population.
Human Development Index (HDI): HDI is a composite index that measures the overall development of a country based on indicators such as life expectancy, education, and income. According to the United Nations Development Program (UNDP), India's HDI value in 2020 was 0.645, which is below the world average of 0.737. This indicates that while India has made significant progress in improving human development indicators in recent years, there is still a long way to go.

Q: (A) Why is India's Per capita income low compared to the world average? Suggest any one measure to increase the Per capita income of India. (B) Mention any one measure to increase the Per capita income of India. (C) Propose any two measures to be taken by India in improving its HDI.

A: (A) Rapid population growth directly affects per capita income in an economy. Rapid growth leads to the problem of allocation of scarce resources. The lack of education, healthcare, and employment opportunities lowers the income level of the citizens, which results in the low per capita income of the country. (B) The per capita income of India may increase through the government's investment in infrastructure, Education, and training. Greater education and job skills allow individuals to produce more goods and services, start businesses, and earn higher incomes. (C) Two measures to be taken by India to improve its HDI could be: increase in budgetary allocation on education and healthcare facilities, improving access to healthcare services, especially in rural areas, building more hospitals and clinics, training more healthcare workers, and implementing policies to reduce the cost of healthcare, with subsidies channelized towards the generation of more employment opportunities; and investing in education, increasing the number of schools and colleges, providing better teacher training, implementing policies to ensure that all children have access to education, and improving the quality of education to ensure that students are gaining the skills they need to succeed in the workforce.

Q: The issue of sustainability is important for development. Examine the statement.

A: Sustainable development fulfils today's needs without compromising future generations. Sustainability is the capability to use resources judiciously and maintain ecological balance. It emphasises environmental protection and checks environmental degradation.

Q: How is the issue of sustainability important for development? Explain with three examples. OR We have not inherited the world from our forefathers, we have borrowed it from our children. Explain with an example.

A: Development that meets present needs without harming the environment and keeps it safe for future generations is sustainable development. Groundwater, though renewable, is depleting through overuse, creating water crises. Fast industrialisation cuts forests, leaving a polluted environment for present and future generations. Crude oil, a non-renewable resource with limited stock, is depleting gradually, so we must use it wisely.

Q: Find out the present sources of energy used by the people in India.

A: Electricity, coal, crude oil, cow dung and solar energy are the present sources of energy used by the people of India.

Q: Why does Kerala have a better Human Development Index ranking than Punjab? Explain with three reasons.

A: Kerala has better health facilities, so its Infant Mortality Rate is very low. Kerala has better educational facilities, so literacy is high and the net attendance ratio is high. Kerala has better provisions for the public distribution system.
