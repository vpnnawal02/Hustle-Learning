### Section A: Short Answer Questions (2-3 Marks)

Q: Who was Richard H. Ebright? What made him famous?

A: Richard H. Ebright was a brilliant scientist who became famous for his research on monarch butterflies and his theory explaining how cells read the genetic instructions contained in DNA. At the age of twenty-two, he and his college roommate published their theory in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Science. His achievements resulted from his scientific curiosity, intelligence, determination and hard work.

Q: How did Ebright develop an interest in collecting things?

A: Ebright grew up near Reading, Pennsylvania, where he felt that there were not many activities available to him. Since he could not play team sports alone, he began collecting things. From kindergarten onwards, he collected butterflies, rocks, fossils and coins. He also developed an interest in astronomy and sometimes spent entire nights observing the stars.

Q: How did Ebright's mother contribute to his success?

A: Ebright's mother played a significant role in developing his curiosity and love of learning. She took him on trips, bought him scientific equipment such as telescopes, microscopes and cameras, and encouraged his interests. She also spent time teaching him new things and supported his scientific activities. Her encouragement helped him develop the habits that eventually contributed to his success.

Q: How did the book The Travels of Monarch X become a turning point in Ebright's life?

A: The book The Travels of Monarch X, given to Ebright by his mother, introduced him to the scientific study of monarch butterflies and their migration to Central America. It also invited readers to participate in butterfly research by tagging butterflies. Inspired by the book, Ebright began working with Dr Frederick A. Urquhart on butterfly migration. This experience opened the world of scientific research to him.

Q: What did Ebright learn from tagging monarch butterflies?

A: Ebright learned about butterfly migration by tagging monarch butterflies and releasing them into the wild. However, he eventually became dissatisfied with this activity because it was tedious and produced little feedback. Only two of the butterflies he tagged were recaptured, and both were found within seventy-five miles of his home. This experience encouraged him to pursue more meaningful scientific experiments.

Q: Why did Ebright lose interest in tagging butterflies?

A: Ebright lost interest in tagging butterflies because the activity was tedious and provided very little feedback. Despite tagging many butterflies, only two were recaptured, and both were found relatively close to his home. He realised that he needed to move beyond collecting and tagging butterflies towards conducting experiments that could answer scientific questions.

Q: What lesson did Ebright learn when he lost at the county science fair?

A: Ebright entered a county science fair in seventh grade with slides of frog tissues displayed under a microscope. However, he did not win any prize. He realised that the winners had conducted genuine scientific experiments rather than simply presenting attractive displays. This experience taught him that scientific research requires investigation, experimentation and meaningful conclusions.

Q: What experiment did Ebright conduct in the eighth grade?

A: In the eighth grade, Ebright attempted to discover the cause of a viral disease that killed large numbers of monarch caterpillars every few years. He suspected that the disease might be carried by beetles and raised caterpillars in their presence to test his theory. Although the experiment did not produce definite results, he presented his work at the science fair and won because he had conducted an actual experiment.

Q: What was Ebright's project in the following year?

A: The following year, Ebright investigated the theory that viceroy butterflies imitate monarch butterflies. The theory suggested that monarchs were unpalatable to birds, while viceroys tasted good to them. By resembling monarchs, viceroys could reduce the likelihood of being eaten by birds. Ebright tested whether birds would eat monarch butterflies and found that a starling ate monarchs readily.

Q: What was the significance of Ebright's research on the gold spots of a monarch pupa?

A: Ebright investigated the purpose of the twelve tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa. While many people assumed that the spots were merely decorative, Ebright and another student developed a device to investigate them. They demonstrated that the spots produced a hormone necessary for the butterfly's full development. This research earned Ebright recognition at science fairs and led to further scientific opportunities.

Q: What did Ebright discover about the hormone produced by the gold spots?

A: Ebright discovered that the gold spots on a monarch pupa produced a hormone essential for the butterfly's development. Through further experiments, he investigated the hormone's chemical structure. Later, he showed that cells from a monarch's wing would divide and develop into normal wing scales only when supplied with the hormone from the gold spots.

Q: How did Ebright's research lead to a theory about the life of cells?

A: While examining X-ray photographs of a hormone's chemical structure, Ebright developed an idea about how cells read the genetic instructions contained in DNA. He combined this insight with his earlier research on insect hormones. Working with his college roommate, James R. Wong, he developed models to explain the theory and co-authored a scientific paper presenting their findings.

Q: What is DNA, according to the chapter?

A: According to the chapter, DNA is the substance in the nucleus of a cell that controls heredity. It determines the form and function of a cell and acts as a blueprint for life. Ebright's research contributed to a theory about how cells read the instructions contained in DNA.

Q: What were some of Ebright's achievements besides scientific research?

A: Apart from being a successful scientist, Ebright was a champion debater, a public speaker, a good canoeist and an outdoor enthusiast. He was also an expert photographer, particularly of nature and scientific exhibits. These interests demonstrate that he was a well-rounded individual who pursued activities beyond his scientific work.

Q: Who was Richard A. Weiherer? How did he influence Ebright?

A: Richard A. Weiherer was Ebright's social studies teacher and the adviser to his debating and Model United Nations clubs. Ebright admired him because he introduced him to new ideas and broadened his thinking. Weiherer also appreciated Ebright's willingness to put in extra effort and his desire to perform well for the right reasons.

Q: What qualities helped Richard Ebright become a successful scientist?

A: Ebright's success resulted from his intelligence, curiosity, determination, perseverance and willingness to conduct experiments. He learnt from failure and continuously improved his research methods. His mother encouraged his interests, while his teachers and scientific mentors helped him develop his abilities. He was competitive, but his main goal was to do his best rather than simply win prizes.

### Section B: Long Answer Questions (5-6 Marks)

Q: Write a character sketch of Richard Ebright.

A: Richard H. Ebright was an intelligent, curious and hardworking scientist whose achievements began with his childhood fascination with butterflies. From an early age, he collected butterflies, rocks, fossils and coins. His curiosity encouraged him to investigate the natural world and ask scientific questions. Ebright was determined and persistent. When he lost at a science fair in seventh grade, he understood that simply displaying specimens was insufficient. He began conducting genuine experiments and gradually improved his research methods. His investigations into monarch butterflies, their gold spots and the hormones necessary for their development led to important scientific findings. He was also competitive, but he did not seek prizes merely for personal recognition. He wanted to produce meaningful results and perform to the best of his abilities. Besides science, he excelled in debating, public speaking and photography. Ebright's character demonstrates that intelligence alone does not create a scientist. Curiosity, perseverance, hard work and the desire to learn are equally important.

Q: Describe the role of Ebright's mother in making him a scientist.

A: Ebright's mother played a crucial role in developing his scientific interests. After his father died when Ebright was in third grade, she became his closest companion and devoted considerable time to his education. She encouraged his curiosity by taking him on trips and buying him scientific equipment, including telescopes, microscopes, cameras and materials for mounting specimens. She also spent evenings with him, helping him learn new things and keeping him intellectually engaged. One of her most important contributions was giving him the book The Travels of Monarch X. This book introduced Ebright to scientific research on monarch butterflies and inspired him to participate in butterfly-tagging activities. Her encouragement helped transform his childhood hobby into a serious scientific interest. Therefore, Ebright's achievements were supported not only by his own abilities but also by his mother's guidance, encouragement and commitment to his education.

Q: How did Ebright's interest in butterflies develop into serious scientific research?

A: Ebright's interest in butterflies began during his childhood, when he collected butterflies and other natural objects. By the second grade, he had collected all twenty-five species of butterflies found around his hometown. His interest developed further when his mother gave him The Travels of Monarch X. The book introduced him to monarch butterfly migration and encouraged him to participate in scientific research by tagging butterflies. Initially, Ebright focused on collecting and tagging butterflies. However, he gradually became dissatisfied with this work because it provided limited results. His failure at a science fair in seventh grade taught him that scientific progress required genuine experiments rather than simple displays. He subsequently investigated diseases affecting monarch caterpillars, the relationship between viceroy and monarch butterflies, and the purpose of the gold spots on monarch pupae. His experiments led to the discovery that these spots produced a hormone essential for butterfly development. His research eventually contributed to a theory about how cells read the genetic instructions contained in DNA. Thus, his childhood hobby developed into advanced scientific research through curiosity, experimentation and perseverance.

Q: Explain how Ebright's failure at the science fair helped him become a better scientist.

A: Ebright's failure at the county science fair in seventh grade was an important turning point in his scientific development. He had entered the competition with slides of frog tissues viewed under a microscope, but he did not receive a prize. Initially, he felt disappointed because other participants had won awards. However, he soon understood why his entry had not succeeded. The winning projects involved genuine scientific experiments, whereas his presentation merely displayed specimens. Ebright realised that science required asking questions, testing ideas and reaching conclusions through experimentation. He decided to conduct a real experiment for the following year's fair. He subsequently investigated scientific questions involving monarch butterflies and continued improving his research methods. His experiments earned recognition at county and international science fairs. This experience taught him that failure can provide valuable lessons. Instead of giving up, he analysed his mistakes and improved his approach, demonstrating the importance of perseverance and learning from experience.

Q: Describe Ebright's important scientific experiments and achievements.

A: Richard Ebright conducted several important experiments during his school years. In eighth grade, he investigated whether beetles might carry a viral disease that killed monarch caterpillars. Although he did not obtain conclusive results, the project taught him the importance of conducting experiments. The following year, he investigated the theory that viceroy butterflies resemble monarch butterflies to avoid being eaten by birds. His project won first place in the zoology division and third place overall at the county science fair. His most significant early research concerned the twelve gold spots on a monarch pupa. Working with another student, he demonstrated that these spots produced a hormone necessary for the butterfly's development. He continued investigating the hormone and its role in the development of butterfly wing cells. Later, while examining X-ray photographs of the hormone's chemical structure, he developed a theory about how cells read the genetic instructions contained in DNA. These achievements demonstrate how his curiosity and experimental work gradually led to increasingly advanced scientific discoveries.

Q: What are the essential qualities required to become a scientist, according to the chapter?

A: According to The Making of a Scientist, becoming a scientist requires more than intelligence or extensive reading. A scientist needs a first-rate mind, curiosity and a strong desire to understand the world. Curiosity encourages a person to ask questions and investigate unexplained phenomena. Scientific experimentation is necessary to test ideas and obtain reliable results. Perseverance is equally important because experiments do not always succeed, and failures provide opportunities to learn. Ebright's experience at the science fair demonstrates the importance of analysing mistakes and improving one's methods. His subsequent research shows the value of dedication and sustained effort. The chapter also emphasises the importance of healthy competition. Ebright wanted to win because he wanted to do his best, not merely to obtain prizes. Thus, intelligence, curiosity, experimentation, perseverance and the desire to achieve excellence for the right reasons are the essential qualities of a scientist.

Q: Justify the title The Making of a Scientist.

A: The title The Making of a Scientist is appropriate because the chapter describes how Richard Ebright developed from a curious child into a successful scientist. His journey began with collecting butterflies and other natural objects. His mother's encouragement and the book The Travels of Monarch X introduced him to scientific research. Initially, Ebright concentrated on collecting and tagging butterflies. However, after losing at a science fair, he realised that genuine science required experimentation and investigation. He subsequently conducted experiments on monarch caterpillars, butterfly mimicry and the gold spots on monarch pupae. His research eventually contributed to a theory explaining how cells read the genetic instructions contained in DNA. The chapter highlights the importance of curiosity, intelligence, perseverance, experimentation and guidance in scientific development. Therefore, the title accurately describes the process through which Ebright's interests, abilities and efforts shaped him into a scientist.

Q: How did Ebright's research on monarch butterflies lead to his theory about cell life?

A: Ebright's research on monarch butterflies gradually led him towards understanding the processes of cell development. During high school, he investigated the twelve gold spots on a monarch pupa and discovered that they produced a hormone necessary for the butterfly's development. He continued researching the hormone and demonstrated that cells from a monarch's wing required it to divide and develop into normal wing scales. Further research helped him identify the hormone's chemical structure. Later, while examining X-ray photographs of the hormone, Ebright developed an idea about how cells read the genetic instructions contained in DNA. He discussed the idea with his college roommate, James R. Wong, and together they constructed models to illustrate their theory. They subsequently co-authored a scientific paper explaining their findings. This work represented an important step in Ebright's scientific career and demonstrated how one research question can lead to further discoveries.

### Section C: Higher-Order Thinking and Value-Based Questions

Q: Why is curiosity considered the starting point of scientific discovery?

A: Curiosity encourages people to ask questions about the world and investigate things they do not understand. Richard Ebright's scientific journey began with his fascination with butterflies. His curiosity led him from collecting specimens to studying butterfly migration and conducting experiments. As he developed his interests, he began asking more complex questions, such as why monarch pupae had gold spots and how cells developed. These questions led to scientific investigations and important discoveries. His experience shows that curiosity motivates observation, experimentation and learning. Without curiosity, people may accept existing explanations without investigating whether they are correct. Therefore, curiosity is an essential starting point for scientific discovery because it encourages people to explore, question and seek evidence.

Q: What is the difference between collecting specimens and conducting scientific research? How did Ebright learn this difference?

A: Collecting specimens involves gathering and organising objects for observation or study. Although it can develop knowledge and interest, it does not necessarily explain scientific processes. Scientific research goes further by asking questions, forming hypotheses, conducting experiments, analysing results and drawing conclusions based on evidence. Ebright learnt this difference when he lost at the county science fair in seventh grade. His entry consisted of slides of frog tissues, while the winning participants had conducted genuine experiments. This experience changed his approach. He began investigating specific scientific questions and designing experiments to test his ideas. His subsequent research on monarch butterflies eventually led to significant scientific achievements. His experience demonstrates that scientific progress requires investigation and evidence rather than simply collecting information.

Q: Ebright was competitive, but not in a negative sense. Explain.

A: Ebright possessed a strong competitive spirit, but he did not seek victory merely to defeat others or collect prizes. His teacher, Richard A. Weiherer, explained that Ebright wanted to win because he aimed to do the best possible work. When Ebright lost at a science fair, he did not abandon his interests. Instead, he analysed the reasons for his failure and improved his research methods. His determination helped him produce increasingly meaningful experiments and achieve recognition at scientific competitions. His competitiveness motivated him to improve his performance, work harder and pursue excellence. It was therefore constructive rather than destructive. The chapter suggests that competition is valuable when it encourages personal growth, learning and high standards rather than jealousy or the desire to defeat others at any cost.

Q: How did Ebright demonstrate perseverance throughout his scientific journey?

A: Ebright demonstrated perseverance by continuing his scientific work despite disappointments and unsuccessful experiments. When he failed to win at the seventh-grade science fair, he analysed his mistakes and decided to conduct genuine experiments. His eighth-grade investigation into a disease affecting monarch caterpillars did not produce definite results. Nevertheless, he presented the experiment and learnt from the experience. He continued investigating butterflies, eventually studying the hormone produced by the gold spots on monarch pupae. His research developed progressively and contributed to a theory about how cells read DNA's genetic instructions. His journey demonstrates that scientific achievement often requires sustained effort, learning from failure and the willingness to continue investigating difficult questions.

A: Teachers and mentors helped Ebright develop his scientific abilities by encouraging his curiosity and exposing him to new ideas. His mother supported his early interests and introduced him to scientific research through books and equipment. Dr Frederick A. Urquhart encouraged Ebright's involvement in butterfly research and provided suggestions for scientific experiments. His achievements began with a simple childhood interest in collecting butterflies, which gradually developed into serious scientific research. Q: How can one become a scientist, an economist or a historian? Does it simply involve reading many books?

A: Becoming a scientist, economist or historian requires more than reading books. Reading provides essential knowledge, but a person must also develop curiosity, critical thinking and the ability to investigate questions. A scientist must observe phenomena, formulate hypotheses, conduct experiments and analyse evidence. Similarly, economists examine economic data and patterns, while historians investigate historical sources and interpret evidence. Richard Ebright's experience demonstrates that simply collecting specimens or displaying information is insufficient for scientific achievement. Genuine progress requires asking questions and testing ideas. Therefore, reading is important, but observation, critical thinking, practical investigation and sustained effort are equally necessary.

Richard Ebright's research began with the study of monarch butterflies.
