### Section A: Short Answer Questions (2-3 Marks)

Q: Why did Lomov visit Chubukov's house?

A: Lomov visited Chubukov's house to ask for the hand of his daughter, Natalya Stepanovna, in marriage. He considered Natalya a suitable life partner because she was a good housekeeper, well-educated, and not bad-looking. He also wanted to lead a quiet and regular life, as he was already thirty-five years old and frequently suffered from health problems.

Q: How did Chubukov react when Lomov told him about his intention to marry Natalya?

A: Chubukov was overjoyed when Lomov revealed that he wanted to marry Natalya. He embraced and kissed Lomov, expressing his happiness and claiming that he had always loved him like his own son. He immediately agreed to the proposal and went to call Natalya.

Q: Why was Lomov wearing formal clothes when he visited Chubukov?

A: Lomov was wearing a dress jacket and white gloves because he had come to propose marriage to Natalya. His formal appearance reflected the seriousness of his intention. However, his nervousness and hesitation prevented him from expressing his purpose immediately.

Q: Why did Lomov think that he should get married?

A: Lomov believed that he should get married because he was already thirty-five years old and considered it a critical age for marriage. He wanted to lead a quiet and regular life. He also thought Natalya was a suitable partner because she was an excellent housekeeper, well-educated, and not bad-looking.

Q: What was the first issue over which Lomov and Natalya quarrelled?

A: Lomov and Natalya quarrelled over the ownership of Oxen Meadows, a small piece of land situated between their properties. Lomov claimed that the land belonged to him, while Natalya insisted that it had belonged to her family for generations. Their disagreement quickly developed into a heated argument.

Q: Why did Natalya insist that Oxen Meadows belonged to her family?

A: Natalya believed that Oxen Meadows belonged to her family because her grandfather and great-grandfather had always considered the land part of their property. She claimed that her family had possessed it for nearly three hundred years. She was unwilling to accept Lomov's claim, even though the land was not particularly valuable.

Q: Why did Lomov and Natalya consider Oxen Meadows important despite their low value?

A: Although Oxen Meadows were worth only about 300 roubles, both Lomov and Natalya were unwilling to give up their claims. Their argument was driven more by pride, stubbornness, and a desire to prove themselves right than by the actual monetary value of the land. The dispute exposes their obsession with property and personal prestige.

Q: Why did Lomov leave Chubukov's house without proposing to Natalya?

A: Lomov left Chubukov's house after a heated argument with Natalya over the ownership of Oxen Meadows. Chubukov joined the dispute and exchanged insults with him. The argument became so intense that Lomov's health deteriorated, and he eventually left without making his marriage proposal.

Q: How did Natalya react when she learnt that Lomov had come to propose to her?

A: Natalya was shocked when her father revealed that Lomov had visited to propose marriage. She immediately regretted quarrelling with him and desperately demanded that her father bring him back. Her reaction reveals that she was eager to marry Lomov despite their constant disagreements.

Q: What was the second issue over which Lomov and Natalya quarrelled?

A: Lomov and Natalya's second argument concerned their hunting dogs, Guess and Squeezer. Lomov considered Guess superior, while Natalya insisted that Squeezer was the better dog. They argued about the dogs' qualities, pedigree, age, and hunting abilities, turning another trivial disagreement into a heated quarrel.

Q: Why did Lomov repeatedly complain about his health?

A: Lomov frequently complained about palpitations, trembling, numbness, and other physical discomforts. He was highly nervous and excitable, particularly during arguments. His repeated complaints about his health add to the humour of the play and show how easily he becomes distressed over trivial matters.

Q: How did Chubukov finally get Lomov and Natalya engaged?

A: During their argument over the hunting dogs, Lomov became so distressed that he collapsed, and Natalya believed he was dead. When he regained consciousness, Chubukov hurriedly declared that Natalya was willing to marry him and blessed the couple. Lomov and Natalya kissed, completing the proposal. However, they immediately resumed arguing about their dogs.

Q: What is a farce? Why is The Proposal considered a farce?

A: A farce is a form of comedy that uses exaggerated behaviour, absurd situations, misunderstandings, and ridiculous conflicts to entertain the audience. The Proposal is a farce because the characters repeatedly quarrel over insignificant matters, exaggerate their reactions, and allow trivial disputes to interrupt a marriage proposal. Lomov's dramatic health complaints and the chaotic ending further contribute to the humour.

Q: What is the significance of the title The Proposal?

A: The title refers to Lomov's visit to Chubukov's house to propose marriage to Natalya. However, the proposal repeatedly gets interrupted by arguments over property and hunting dogs. The title is appropriate because the entire play revolves around this marriage proposal and the absurd quarrels that threaten to prevent it.

### Section B: Long Answer Questions (5-6 Marks)

Q: Write a character sketch of Ivan Vassilevitch Lomov.

A: Ivan Vassilevitch Lomov is a wealthy, thirty-five-year-old landowner who visits his neighbour Chubukov to propose marriage to Natalya. He is nervous, excitable, argumentative, and extremely concerned about his health. He frequently complains of palpitations, trembling, and other physical discomforts. Lomov is also practical and materialistic. He considers Natalya a suitable partner because she is a good housekeeper, well-educated, and not bad-looking. He believes that marriage is necessary because he has reached a suitable age and wants a quiet, regular life. However, Lomov is stubborn and quarrelsome. He argues passionately with Natalya over the ownership of Oxen Meadows and later over the superiority of their hunting dogs. Although he claims that the disputes are matters of principle, his behaviour suggests that pride plays a major role in his arguments. Through Lomov, Chekhov satirises wealthy landowners who allow petty disputes and personal pride to dominate their lives.

Q: Write a character sketch of Natalya Stepanovna.

A: Natalya Stepanovna is Chubukov's twenty-five-year-old daughter. She is a capable housekeeper, educated, and interested in managing her family's property. Lomov considers her a suitable marriage partner because of these qualities. However, Natalya is stubborn, argumentative, and possessive. She refuses to accept Lomov's claim over Oxen Meadows and argues fiercely to defend her family's ownership. She is unwilling to compromise, even when the land is relatively insignificant in value. Natalya also displays pride and competitiveness during her argument over the hunting dogs. She insists that her dog, Squeezer, is superior to Lomov's Guess and refuses to accept any criticism. Despite her quarrelsome nature, Natalya is eager to marry Lomov. When she learns that he came to propose to her, she desperately asks her father to bring him back. Her behaviour reveals the contradiction between her desire for marriage and her inability to control her temper. Through Natalya, Chekhov presents a humorous picture of a person whose stubbornness repeatedly overshadows her own interests.

Q: Write a character sketch of Stepan Stepanovitch Chubukov.

A: Stepan Stepanovitch Chubukov is a wealthy landowner and Natalya's father. Initially, he appears warm and welcoming when Lomov visits his house. He is delighted to learn that Lomov wants to marry Natalya and readily supports the proposal. However, Chubukov is also quarrelsome, short-tempered, and possessive about his property. When Lomov claims ownership of Oxen Meadows, Chubukov immediately takes Natalya's side and joins the argument. He exchanges insults with Lomov and makes accusations about his family. Chubukov is also practical and eager to see his daughter married. When he realises that his quarrels have driven Lomov away, he rushes to bring him back. Eventually, he hurriedly arranges their engagement to prevent further complications. His behaviour contributes significantly to the humour of the play. Through Chubukov, Chekhov satirises wealthy landowners who are easily provoked by disputes but remain concerned with property, family interests, and advantageous marriages.

Q: How does Anton Chekhov use humour and satire in The Proposal?

A: Anton Chekhov uses humour and satire to expose the foolishness, pride, and materialistic attitudes of wealthy landowners. The play revolves around Lomov's visit to propose marriage to Natalya, but the proposal is repeatedly interrupted by trivial disputes. The first argument concerns the ownership of Oxen Meadows, a small piece of land. Although the property is worth relatively little, the characters argue passionately and exchange personal insults. Their behaviour reveals how pride and stubbornness can make insignificant matters appear extremely important. The second argument concerns their hunting dogs, Guess and Squeezer. Instead of discussing their marriage peacefully, Lomov and Natalya once again become involved in a heated dispute. Lomov's repeated complaints about his health, Natalya's dramatic reaction when she believes he is dead, and Chubukov's desperate attempts to arrange the marriage add to the absurdity. The play satirises people who prioritise property, status, and winning arguments over understanding and cooperation. Its humour comes from the contrast between the serious purpose of the visit and the ridiculous behaviour of the characters.

Q: Explain how the play shows that pride and stubbornness can destroy relationships.

A: The play demonstrates how pride and stubbornness can damage relationships through the behaviour of Lomov, Natalya, and Chubukov. Lomov visits Chubukov's house with the intention of marrying Natalya, and Chubukov initially welcomes the proposal enthusiastically. However, Lomov and Natalya soon begin arguing over Oxen Meadows. Neither is willing to listen to the other or compromise. Chubukov joins the dispute, and the disagreement escalates into personal insults that force Lomov to leave. When Natalya discovers that Lomov had come to propose, she regrets the argument and insists that he return. Yet, after the first dispute is settled, they begin quarrelling again over their hunting dogs. Their behaviour reveals that they are more interested in defending their opinions than maintaining a peaceful relationship. Even after their engagement, they continue arguing. Chekhov uses this situation to show that uncontrolled pride and stubbornness can turn minor disagreements into major conflicts and undermine the purpose of a relationship.

Q: Why is the play titled The Proposal even though the characters spend most of their time quarrelling?

A: The title The Proposal is appropriate because Lomov's primary purpose in visiting Chubukov's house is to ask for Natalya's hand in marriage. However, the characters' quarrels repeatedly distract them from this purpose. The first disagreement over Oxen Meadows prevents Lomov from making his proposal. After Natalya learns the truth and Lomov returns, they argue over their hunting dogs. Their inability to control their tempers creates obstacles to the marriage. Eventually, Chubukov intervenes and hastily arranges their engagement. The fact that Lomov and Natalya immediately resume arguing makes the ending humorous and ironic. The title highlights the central event of the play while drawing attention to the absurd behaviour that nearly prevents it from happening.

Q: The Proposal is a satire on wealthy landowners and their priorities. Explain.

A: The Proposal satirises wealthy landowners who prioritise property, social position, and material interests over understanding and harmony. Lomov and Chubukov are wealthy neighbours, and Lomov's proposal is welcomed because the marriage would be socially and economically suitable. However, the characters repeatedly become involved in arguments over insignificant matters. Lomov and Natalya quarrel over Oxen Meadows despite its relatively low value. Later, they argue over which hunting dog is superior. Their disputes become so intense that personal insults replace rational discussion. Even the marriage proposal is nearly forgotten because of their obsession with proving themselves right. The play also suggests that marriage among wealthy families may be treated as a practical arrangement rather than a relationship based primarily on love. Through exaggerated quarrels and a comic ending, Chekhov exposes the foolishness of allowing pride, property, and material interests to dominate personal relationships.

### Section C: Competency-Based Questions

Q: Lomov visits Chubukov's house to propose marriage but ends up quarrelling twice. What does this reveal about his personality?

A: This reveals that Lomov is nervous, stubborn, and argumentative. Although he has a clear purpose, he becomes distracted by disputes over property and hunting dogs. His insistence on proving himself right shows that pride and personal interests often overpower his practical intentions. His behaviour also contributes to the play's humour by creating a contrast between his serious purpose and his inability to avoid trivial arguments.

Q: Natalya immediately wants Lomov back after learning about his marriage proposal. What does this reveal about her priorities?

A: Natalya's reaction reveals that she is eager to marry Lomov and considers him a suitable partner. However, her desire for marriage does not prevent her from becoming involved in petty arguments. She is willing to defend her family's property and her dog's reputation aggressively, even when doing so threatens the proposal. Her behaviour highlights the contradiction between her desire for marriage and her inability to control her temper.

Q: Why is it ironic that Lomov and Natalya continue arguing even after their engagement?

A: The irony lies in the fact that their engagement should mark the beginning of a harmonious relationship, yet they immediately resume quarrelling about their dogs. Their behaviour shows that the engagement has not changed their stubborn and argumentative nature. The ending creates humour while suggesting that their future married life may be filled with similar disputes.

Q: What would have happened if Lomov and Natalya had been willing to compromise?

A: If Lomov and Natalya had been willing to compromise, they could have resolved their disagreements calmly and proceeded with the marriage proposal without unnecessary conflict. Their willingness to listen to each other would have prevented the arguments from escalating. The play suggests that patience, mutual respect, and rational discussion are essential for resolving disagreements and maintaining healthy relationships.

Q: How does the play demonstrate the difference between the real importance of an issue and the importance people assign to it?

A: The play demonstrates this difference through the arguments over Oxen Meadows and the hunting dogs. Oxen Meadows are worth relatively little, yet Lomov, Natalya, and Chubukov behave as though the dispute is extremely serious. Similarly, the characters become deeply offended over whose hunting dog is better. Their exaggerated reactions show that personal pride can make insignificant matters appear far more important than they really are.

### Section D: Extract-Based Questions

Q: Why has Lomov visited Chubukov? How does Chubukov initially react to the proposal?

A: Lomov has visited Chubukov to ask for Natalya's hand in marriage. Chubukov is overjoyed and immediately expresses his approval.

Q: Why is Lomov nervous during the proposal conversation? What does the incident reveal about Chubukov?

A: Lomov is naturally excitable and becomes extremely nervous while discussing the marriage proposal. It reveals that Chubukov is pleased by the prospect of the marriage and considers Lomov a suitable match for his daughter.

Q: What are Oxen Meadows? Why do Lomov and Natalya quarrel over the meadows?

A: Oxen Meadows are a small piece of land located between the properties of Lomov and Chubukov. Both believe that the land belongs to their respective families and refuse to accept the other's claim.

Q: What does the Oxen Meadows argument reveal about their personalities? How does the argument affect the marriage proposal?

A: It reveals their stubbornness, pride, possessiveness, and inability to resolve disagreements peacefully. The argument prevents Lomov from making his proposal and eventually drives him out of Chubukov's house.

Q: Which two dogs are being discussed? Why do Lomov and Natalya quarrel over the dogs?

A: The two dogs are Lomov's Guess and Natalya's Squeezer. Each believes that their own dog is superior and refuses to accept the other's opinion.

Q: How does the argument over dogs affect Lomov? What is ironic about the ending?

A: Lomov becomes extremely agitated, complains of severe physical discomfort, and eventually collapses. Although Lomov and Natalya finally become engaged, they immediately resume arguing about their dogs, suggesting that their quarrelsome behaviour is unlikely to change.

### Section E: Important Value-Based Questions

Q: What values can students learn from the play The Proposal?

A: Students can learn the importance of patience, mutual respect, compromise, and effective communication. The characters' repeated arguments demonstrate how pride and stubbornness can turn minor disagreements into major conflicts. Their behaviour also shows the danger of prioritising material possessions over relationships. The play encourages people to listen to others, control their temper, and resolve disagreements rationally rather than allowing personal pride to dominate their decisions.

Q: Do you think Lomov and Natalya are suitable partners for each other? Give reasons for your answer.

A: Lomov and Natalya are similar in several ways. Both are wealthy, concerned about their property, and highly argumentative. Their shared social background makes them a practical match, which is why Chubukov welcomes the proposal. However, their stubbornness and inability to resolve disagreements peacefully may create difficulties in their married life. Although they eventually agree to marry, their immediate return to quarrelling suggests that their relationship may lack harmony. They may be compatible in terms of social position, but they need greater patience and mutual respect to maintain a healthy relationship.

Q: Winning an argument is not always more important than preserving a relationship. Explain with reference to the play.

A: The play illustrates this idea through Lomov and Natalya's repeated disputes. Both are so determined to prove themselves right that they nearly destroy the marriage proposal. Their first argument over Oxen Meadows drives Lomov away, while their second argument over their dogs causes further distress. Although both want the marriage to take place, neither initially makes a serious effort to avoid unnecessary conflict. Their behaviour demonstrates that the desire to win an argument can overshadow more important personal goals. The play encourages people to prioritise understanding and cooperation over pride and the need to be right.
