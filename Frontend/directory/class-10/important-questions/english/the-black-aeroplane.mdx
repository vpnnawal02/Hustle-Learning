### Section B: Extract-Based Questions (1 Mark Each)

Q: What does the word they refer to in the extract: They were huge. They looked like black mountains standing in front of me across the sky?

A: It refers to the huge storm clouds.

Q: Why did the narrator consider the clouds dangerous?

A: The clouds indicated a severe storm that the narrator could neither safely fly over nor easily avoid because of his limited fuel.

Q: What literary device is used in the comparison of the clouds to black mountains?

A: Simile.

Q: What does the comparison of the clouds to black mountains suggest?

A: It emphasises their enormous size, threatening appearance and the danger they posed to the narrator.

Q: Which other means of navigation failed besides the compass?

A: The other instruments and the radio also stopped working.

Q: What does the compass extract reveal about the narrator's situation?

A: It reveals that he had lost his navigational instruments and communication system, leaving him helpless and disoriented inside the storm.

Q: Who lifted a hand and waved in the extract: He lifted one hand and waved. Follow me, he was saying?

A: The pilot of the mysterious black aeroplane.

Q: Why did the narrator decide to follow the pilot who waved?

A: The narrator was lost inside the storm and believed that the unknown pilot was trying to guide him to safety.

Q: How did the narrator feel when he saw the black aeroplane?

A: He felt relieved and hopeful because he believed another pilot had appeared to help him.

Q: Why is the waving moment important to the development of the plot?

A: It introduces the mysterious aircraft that guides the narrator through the storm and ultimately leads to the story's unresolved ending.

Q: Who made this statement: No other aeroplanes were flying tonight. Yours was the only one I could see on the radar?

A: The woman in the control centre.

### Section C: Very Short-Answer Questions (2 Marks Each)

Q: Why was the narrator happy at the beginning of the story?

A: The narrator was enjoying a peaceful night flight over the sleeping countryside. He was looking forward to reaching England, spending his holiday with his family and enjoying a good English breakfast.

Q: Why did the narrator contact Paris Control?

A: He contacted Paris Control to communicate his position and obtain navigational instructions while flying towards England. The controller instructed him to turn twelve degrees west.

Q: Why did the narrator decide against returning to Paris?

A: Although he recognised the danger posed by the storm, he wanted to reach England and join his family. His desire to get home influenced him to take the risky decision to fly through the clouds.

Q: Why could the narrator not fly around the storm clouds?

A: He did not have sufficient fuel to fly around the storm towards the north or south. He also knew that flying over the enormous storm clouds was not a feasible option.

Q: What happened when the Dakota entered the storm?

A: Visibility became extremely poor, and the aeroplane began jumping and twisting in the air. The compass, other instruments and radio stopped functioning, leaving the narrator unable to navigate or communicate.

Q: Why was the narrator relieved to see the black aeroplane?

A: He had become lost inside the storm and could no longer use his instruments or radio. Seeing another aircraft gave him hope that its pilot could guide him to safety.

Q: How did the mysterious pilot help the narrator?

A: The pilot waved to the narrator and signalled him to follow. He led the Dakota through the storm and descended towards an airport, enabling the narrator to reach safety.

Q: Why was the narrator frightened when he checked his remaining fuel?

A: His remaining fuel could sustain the flight for only five or ten minutes. If he failed to reach an airport quickly, his aircraft could run out of fuel while he was still inside the storm.

Q: Why did the narrator turn to look for the black aeroplane after landing?

A: He wanted to thank the unknown pilot for guiding him through the storm and helping him land safely. However, he discovered that the aircraft had disappeared.

Q: What information did the woman in the control centre provide?

A: She told the narrator that no other aeroplanes had been flying that night and that his Dakota was the only aircraft she could see on the radar.

Q: Why is the black aeroplane described as mysterious?

A: The aircraft appears unexpectedly during a dangerous storm, guides the narrator to safety and disappears afterwards. The control-centre employee's statement that no other aircraft was detected makes its existence and identity mysterious.

Q: What mistake did the narrator make before entering the storm?

A: He prioritised his desire to reach home over the risks associated with the storm. Despite recognising the danger and his limited fuel, he chose to fly directly into the clouds.

### Section D: Short-Answer Questions (3 Marks Each)

Q: Describe the events that led the narrator to fly into the storm.

A: The narrator was flying his Dakota from France to England on a clear night. After receiving instructions from Paris Control, he continued towards his destination. However, approximately 150 kilometres beyond Paris, he encountered enormous storm clouds. He knew he could neither fly over them nor safely circle around them because of his limited fuel. Wanting to reach home, he decided to take the risk and entered the storm.

Q: Explain how the storm changed the narrator's situation.

A: The storm transformed a peaceful flight into a dangerous situation. Inside the clouds, the narrator could see nothing, and his aeroplane jumped and twisted in the air. His compass began spinning, the other instruments stopped working and the radio failed. He could no longer determine his position, communicate with Paris Control or identify a safe route out of the storm.

Q: How did the appearance of the black aeroplane change the narrator's state of mind?

A: The narrator was frightened and helpless after losing his instruments and radio. The sudden appearance of another aircraft gave him hope. When its pilot waved and signalled him to follow, he believed that someone had understood his predicament and was willing to help. His fear temporarily gave way to relief, and he obediently followed the mysterious aircraft.

Q: Why did the narrator follow the black aeroplane without questioning the other pilot?

A: The narrator was lost in the storm, unable to navigate and running dangerously low on fuel. Under these circumstances, the appearance of another aircraft seemed like his only chance of survival. He trusted the unknown pilot because the pilot appeared to understand his situation and seemed to be guiding him towards safety.

Q: Describe the narrator's feelings when he saw the runway.

A: The narrator felt immense relief and joy when he emerged from the storm and saw the runway lights. He realised that he had reached an airport and could land safely despite the failure of his instruments and his dangerously low fuel supply. His immediate concern shifted from survival to locating the pilot who had helped him.

Q: Why did the woman in the control centre react strangely to the narrator's question?

A: The woman reacted strangely because the narrator asked about another pilot who had guided him through the storm. According to her, no other aircraft had been flying that night, and the radar showed only the narrator's Dakota. Her response contradicted his experience and made the mystery of the black aeroplane even more puzzling.

Q: How does the story create suspense from beginning to end?

A: The story begins with a peaceful flight that suddenly becomes dangerous when the narrator encounters a storm. The failure of his instruments increases the tension, and the mysterious black aeroplane introduces an unexpected possibility of rescue. The narrator reaches safety, but the disappearance of the aircraft and the control-centre employee's statement leave the central mystery unresolved.

Q: What does the narrator's decision to enter the storm reveal about his judgement?

A: The narrator's decision reveals that his judgement was influenced by impatience and his desire to reach home. Although he recognised the danger of the storm and knew that his fuel was insufficient to fly around it, he chose to take the risk. His decision demonstrates how personal desires can sometimes lead people to underestimate serious dangers.

Q: Why is the final question of the story important?

A: The final question asks who piloted the black aeroplane and helped the narrator reach safety. It is important because the control-centre employee has already stated that no other aircraft was detected. The question leaves the story open to interpretation and encourages readers to consider possible explanations for the mysterious rescue.

### Section E: Long-Answer Questions (6 Marks Each)

Q: Describe the narrator's journey through the storm and explain how he ultimately reached safety.

A: The narrator was flying his old Dakota from France to England on a clear night. He was looking forward to reaching home and having breakfast with his family. However, after travelling approximately 150 kilometres beyond Paris, he encountered enormous storm clouds. Although he recognised the danger, he decided to fly through them because he wanted to reach England. Inside the storm, visibility disappeared, the aircraft became unstable, and the compass, other instruments and radio stopped working. The narrator was completely lost when he suddenly saw a black aeroplane. Its pilot signalled him to follow and guided him through the storm. With only five or ten minutes of fuel remaining, the narrator emerged from the clouds and spotted an airport runway. He landed safely, but the mysterious aircraft had disappeared.

Q: The Black Aeroplane is a story of danger, suspense and mystery. Discuss.

A: The story combines danger, suspense and mystery through the narrator's unexpected experience during a night flight. Initially, he enjoys a peaceful journey towards England. The appearance of enormous storm clouds changes everything, but his desire to reach home makes him enter the dangerous weather. Inside the storm, his instruments and radio fail, leaving him unable to navigate. The sudden appearance of a black aeroplane introduces a mysterious element. Its pilot appears to understand the narrator's predicament and guides him towards an airport. The tension increases as the narrator's fuel approaches exhaustion. Although he lands safely, the black aeroplane disappears. The control-centre employee then reveals that no other aircraft was detected on the radar. This contradiction leaves the identity of the mysterious pilot unresolved, making the ending suspenseful and intriguing.

Q: Explain how the narrator's desire to reach home affects his decisions and nearly places him in danger.

A: The narrator's desire to reach home influences several important decisions during the flight. Initially, he enjoys the journey and imagines having breakfast with his family. When he encounters the storm clouds, he recognises that flying through them is dangerous. However, rather than returning to Paris, he decides to continue towards England. This decision exposes him to severe turbulence and causes him to lose visibility, navigation and communication. With his instruments and radio no longer functioning, he becomes completely lost. His limited fuel further increases the danger, leaving him dependent on the mysterious black aeroplane. Although he eventually reaches safety, the experience demonstrates the consequences of allowing personal desires to override sound judgement. The story suggests that determination must be balanced with caution and responsible decision-making.

Q: What is the significance of the black aeroplane in the story?

A: The black aeroplane is the central mystery and the turning point of the story. It appears when the narrator is lost inside the storm and has no functioning instruments or radio. Its pilot signals him to follow and guides him towards an airport, apparently saving his life. However, the aircraft disappears after the narrator lands. The woman in the control centre then states that no other aircraft was flying that night and that the Dakota was the only plane detected on the radar. The contradiction between the narrator's experience and the woman's statement gives the black aeroplane its significance. It introduces uncertainty about whether the aircraft was real, imagined or otherwise inexplicable. Its unresolved identity encourages readers to interpret the story in different ways.

Q: How does the ending of The Black Aeroplane challenge the reader's understanding of reality?

A: The ending challenges the reader's understanding of reality by presenting two conflicting accounts. The narrator claims that another aircraft appeared during the storm and guided him to safety. He describes seeing its pilot and following its movements until he reached an airport. However, the woman in the control centre states that no other aircraft was flying that night and that only the Dakota appeared on the radar. Her statement contradicts the narrator's experience without providing an alternative explanation. This contradiction leaves readers uncertain about the identity and existence of the black aeroplane. It may encourage interpretations involving an unexplained rescue, an unusual perception during extreme stress or a symbolic intervention. However, the story does not confirm any single explanation. Its unresolved ending is central to its mystery.

Q: Compare the narrator's emotional state at the beginning, during the storm and after landing.

A: At the beginning of the story, the narrator is happy and relaxed. He enjoys flying alone above the sleeping countryside and looks forward to spending time with his family in England. His emotional state changes dramatically when he enters the storm. The darkness, violent movements of the aeroplane and failure of his instruments create fear and helplessness. When the black aeroplane appears, he feels relieved and hopeful, believing that another pilot will guide him to safety. As his fuel approaches exhaustion, his fear returns. After landing, he feels grateful and relieved. However, his relief turns into confusion when the woman in the control centre tells him that no other aircraft was detected. His experience therefore ends with a mixture of safety, gratitude and uncertainty.

### Section F: Competency-Based and Inference Questions (4 Marks Each)

Q: The narrator knew that flying through the storm was dangerous, yet he continued. What does this reveal about the relationship between desire and decision-making?

A: The narrator's decision reveals that strong personal desires can influence judgement and lead people to underestimate risks. Although he recognised the storm's danger and knew that he lacked enough fuel to fly around it, he wanted to reach England and enjoy breakfast with his family. His desire therefore outweighed his caution. The incident illustrates the importance of evaluating consequences objectively rather than allowing impatience or emotional attachment to dominate important decisions.

Q: Suppose the narrator had returned to Paris instead of entering the storm. How might the events have changed?

A: If the narrator had returned to Paris, he would probably have avoided the immediate danger of flying through the storm. His instruments and communication system might have remained usable outside the storm, allowing him to seek assistance and plan another flight. However, he would have reached England later than expected. This alternative highlights the importance of prioritising safety over convenience and shows that a delayed arrival is preferable to taking an unnecessary risk.

Q: The narrator calls the black aeroplane's pilot his friend. What does this suggest about the narrator's psychological state?

A: Calling the unknown pilot his friend reveals the narrator's desperation and relief at finding help during a frightening situation. He is isolated, unable to navigate and uncertain whether he will survive. The appearance of another aircraft gives him hope, so he quickly develops trust in its pilot. His reaction demonstrates how people may form an immediate emotional connection with someone they believe can help them during a crisis.

Q: Why is the failure of the radio as significant as the failure of the compass?

A: The compass provides directional information, while the radio allows the narrator to communicate with Paris Control and seek guidance. When both fail, he loses not only his ability to navigate but also his means of requesting assistance. The simultaneous failure of these systems leaves him isolated in the storm. This intensifies the danger and explains why the appearance of the black aeroplane seems so important to his survival.

Q: The narrator's experience can be interpreted as a psychological response to extreme stress. Support this interpretation without treating it as a confirmed fact.

A: The narrator experiences extreme stress when his instruments and radio fail, visibility disappears and his fuel begins running out. Under such conditions, fear and disorientation may affect perception and memory. One possible interpretation is that his experience of the black aeroplane was influenced by these circumstances. However, the story does not establish this explanation as fact. The aircraft's mysterious appearance and the control-centre employee's statement leave its identity unresolved.

Q: How does the radar information intensify the mystery rather than resolve it?

A: The radar information intensifies the mystery because it contradicts the narrator's detailed account of following another aircraft. The woman states that his Dakota was the only aircraft detected, despite his belief that another pilot guided him through the storm. Rather than explaining who helped him, the information creates a conflict between his experience and the official observation. The reader is left to consider possible explanations without being given a definitive answer.

Q: Reaching a destination is not always worth the risk involved. Explain this statement with reference to the chapter.

A: The narrator's experience demonstrates the dangers of prioritising a destination over personal safety. Although he wants to reach England and enjoy breakfast with his family, he enters a severe storm despite recognising its danger. His instruments and radio fail, and his remaining fuel becomes critically low. He is fortunate to reach an airport safely. The story suggests that goals must be pursued with sound judgement and that changing plans in response to danger is a responsible decision, not a failure.

### Section I: Value-Based and Theme-Based Questions (3-4 Marks Each)

Q: What lesson does the story teach about making decisions under pressure?

A: The story teaches that decisions made under emotional pressure can have serious consequences. The narrator's eagerness to reach home leads him to enter a dangerous storm despite knowing the risks. His subsequent difficulties demonstrate the importance of remaining calm, evaluating alternatives and prioritising safety. Responsible decision-making requires balancing personal goals with an objective assessment of danger.

Q: How does the story demonstrate the importance of hope during a crisis?

A: The narrator loses hope when his instruments and radio fail, leaving him lost inside the storm. The appearance of the black aeroplane gives him a reason to believe that he can still reach safety. By following it, he eventually reaches an airport. His experience demonstrates how hope can help people continue acting during a crisis, although sound judgement remains essential.

Q: Is the narrator responsible for the dangerous situation he faces? Give reasons for your answer.

A: The narrator bears responsibility for entering the storm because he recognises the danger but chooses to proceed in order to reach England sooner. He knows that he lacks enough fuel to fly around the clouds, yet he prioritises his desire to return home over safety. However, the failure of his instruments and radio once inside the storm makes the situation more dangerous than he can manage. His experience highlights the importance of assessing risks carefully and making responsible decisions before a crisis develops.

Q: Why should the mysterious ending be considered a strength of the story?

A: The mysterious ending is a strength because it leaves the reader with a question that has no confirmed answer. The narrator describes being guided by a black aeroplane, but the control-centre employee states that no other aircraft was detected. This contradiction encourages readers to examine the events and consider different interpretations. Rather than resolving every uncertainty, the author allows the mystery to continue beyond the final sentence, making the story memorable and thought-provoking.
