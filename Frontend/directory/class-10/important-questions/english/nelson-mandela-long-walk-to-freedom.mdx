### Section A: 1-Mark Questions

Q: On which date was Nelson Mandela inaugurated as the President of South Africa?

A: Nelson Mandela was inaugurated on 10 May 1994.

Q: Where did the inauguration ceremony take place?

A: The ceremony took place in the sandstone amphitheatre formed by the Union Buildings in Pretoria, South Africa.

Q: What was apartheid?

A: Apartheid was a political system of racial discrimination that separated people according to their race and denied equal rights to non-white people.

Q: Which organisation did Nelson Mandela join to fight against apartheid?

A: Nelson Mandela joined the African National Congress (ANC).

Q: Who accompanied Nelson Mandela on the day of his inauguration?

A: His daughter, Zenani, accompanied him.

Q: Who was sworn in as the first deputy president of South Africa?

A: Thabo Mbeki was sworn in as the first deputy president.

Q: Who was sworn in as the second deputy president?

A: Mr de Klerk was sworn in as the second deputy president.

Q: What does Mandela mean by the term rainbow gathering?

A: It refers to the gathering of people of different races, colours, and nations united on one occasion.

Q: What were the two national anthems sung during the inauguration?

A: The two anthems were Nkosi Sikelel-iAfrika and Die Stem.

Q: According to Mandela, what is the greatest wealth of a country?

A: According to Mandela, the greatest wealth of a country is its people.

Q: What does Mandela say courage is not?

A: Mandela says that courage is not the absence of fear.

Q: According to Mandela, what is true courage?

A: True courage is the triumph over fear.

Q: What does the word emancipation mean?

A: Emancipation means freedom from restrictions, control, or oppression.

Q: What does Mandela mean by a glimmer of humanity?

A: It refers to a small sign of kindness, goodness, or compassion in a person.

Q: What are the twin obligations mentioned by Mandela?

A: They are obligations towards one's family and obligations towards one's people, community, and country.

Q: What did Mandela initially believe about freedom as a child?

A: He believed he was free to enjoy his childhood, play in the fields, swim in streams, and follow his community's customs.

Q: What is the meaning of prejudice?

A: Prejudice is an unfair or unreasonable dislike of someone without sufficient knowledge or justification.

Q: What does Mandela mean by freedom is indivisible?

A: It means that one person's freedom cannot be separated from the freedom of others.

Q: Who were the people Mandela described as African patriots?

A: They were the people who had sacrificed their lives and struggled for South Africa's freedom and equality.

Q: What was the main achievement celebrated during Mandela's inauguration?

A: The main achievement was the establishment of a democratic, non-racial government in South Africa.

### Section B: 2-Mark Questions

Q: Why was 10 May 1994 an important day in South African history?

A: On 10 May 1994, Nelson Mandela was inaugurated as South Africa's first Black President. The occasion marked the end of apartheid and the beginning of a democratic government based on equality and freedom.

Q: Why did so many international leaders attend Mandela's inauguration?

A: International leaders attended to witness and celebrate South Africa's transition to democracy. Their presence symbolised international support for the country's victory over racial discrimination and oppression.

Q: What did Mandela mean by an extraordinary human disaster?

A: Mandela was referring to apartheid, a system that subjected non-white South Africans to racial discrimination, oppression, and the denial of basic human rights for decades.

Q: What was the glorious human achievement mentioned by Mandela?

A: It was the establishment of a democratic, non-racial society in South Africa in which people would enjoy equal rights and freedom regardless of their skin colour.

Q: Why did Mandela thank the international leaders who attended the inauguration?

A: Mandela thanked them for participating in a historic celebration of freedom and democracy. Their presence recognised South Africa's struggle and its victory over racial oppression.

Q: Why were two national anthems sung during the inauguration?

A: The two anthems represented the country's divided past and its new beginning. Their joint performance symbolised reconciliation and the willingness of Black and white South Africans to build a united nation.

Q: How had the attitude of the South African military generals changed?

A: The generals, who might once have arrested Mandela, now saluted him and pledged loyalty to the democratically elected government. This demonstrated the military's acceptance of democracy and the new political order.

Q: What did Mandela learn from the freedom fighters who struggled against apartheid?

A: Mandela learned the meaning of courage, sacrifice, resilience, and generosity. He saw people risk their lives and endure torture for the cause of freedom and equality.

Q: Why did Mandela describe courage as the triumph over fear?

A: Mandela believed that brave people also experience fear. True courage lies in overcoming that fear and continuing to act in pursuit of what is right.

Q: What does Mandela mean when he says that no one is born hating another person?

A: He means that hatred based on race, religion, or background is learned from society rather than being natural. Since people can learn to hate, they can also learn to love and respect others.

Q: Why did Mandela believe that his country's greatest wealth was its people?

A: Mandela believed that the courage, wisdom, generosity, and resilience of South Africans were more valuable than the country's minerals and precious stones.

Q: What was the effect of apartheid on South African society?

A: Apartheid created racial divisions, denied equal rights, and caused widespread suffering. Its effects damaged relationships and left deep emotional and social wounds that would take generations to heal.

Q: Why was Mandela unable to fulfil both his family and national obligations under apartheid?

A: Under apartheid, fighting for the rights of his people could lead to imprisonment, separation from family, and forced isolation. Consequently, serving his country often prevented him from fulfilling his responsibilities as a husband, father, and son.

Q: How did Mandela's understanding of freedom change as he grew older?

A: As a child, Mandela understood freedom as the ability to play and live according to his community's customs. As he grew older, he realised that true freedom also meant equal rights, dignity, and opportunities for all people.

Q: Why did Mandela join the African National Congress?

A: Mandela realised that racial oppression affected not only his own freedom but also that of all Black South Africans. He joined the ANC to fight for the freedom and dignity of his people.

Q: What does Mandela mean by saying that the oppressor is also not free?

A: Mandela believes that a person who oppresses others becomes a prisoner of hatred and prejudice. Such a person loses part of their humanity by denying freedom to others.

Q: What was Mandela's idea of true freedom?

A: True freedom meant living with dignity and self-respect without discrimination or unjust restrictions. It also meant ensuring that other people enjoyed the same freedom.

Q: What does the phrase a twilight existence mean in the chapter?

A: It refers to a secretive and restricted life forced upon people who resisted apartheid. Mandela used it to describe the life of separation and rebellion that resulted from fighting for his people's rights.

Q: How did apartheid affect Mandela's personal life?

A: His struggle against apartheid separated him from his family and prevented him from fulfilling many family responsibilities. His commitment to national freedom required considerable personal sacrifice.

Q: What message does Mandela convey through the statement that freedom is indivisible?

A: Mandela conveys that freedom is universal and interconnected. A person cannot be truly free while others are oppressed, because denying freedom to others also diminishes one's own humanity.

### Section C: 3-Mark Questions

Q: Describe the significance of Nelson Mandela's inauguration ceremony.

A: Mandela's inauguration was a historic event because it marked the end of apartheid and the beginning of democratic rule in South Africa. First, it established Mandela as the country's first Black President. Second, it symbolised the victory of equality, justice, and human dignity over racial discrimination. Third, the presence of international leaders and people of different races demonstrated hope for a united and democratic South Africa.

Q: How did the inauguration ceremony represent a transformation in South African society?

A: The inauguration represented a fundamental change in South Africa's political and social structure. First, the Union Buildings, formerly associated with white supremacy, became the venue for the installation of a non-racial government. Second, military leaders pledged loyalty to the democratically elected government. Third, people of different races participated in the celebration, symbolising reconciliation and national unity.

Q: Explain Mandela's feelings when he thought about the history of South Africa on inauguration day.

A: Mandela was overwhelmed by the historical significance of the occasion. He remembered how racial domination had been established and how generations of people had suffered under apartheid. He also felt grateful to the freedom fighters whose sacrifices had made democracy possible. However, he felt sadness that many of those who had struggled for freedom were not alive to witness the achievement.

Q: How did the struggle against apartheid contribute to the development of extraordinary leaders?

A: Mandela believed that the extreme oppression experienced under apartheid helped produce people of remarkable character. First, freedom fighters developed courage by facing imprisonment, torture, and persecution. Second, they demonstrated resilience by continuing their struggle despite immense suffering. Third, their sacrifices and commitment inspired others to fight for justice, equality, and freedom.

Q: Explain the significance of the military display during Mandela's inauguration.

A: The military display demonstrated the transformation of South Africa's political system. First, jets, helicopters, and troop carriers flew in formation over the Union Buildings. Second, the military's participation represented its loyalty to the democratically elected government. Third, the event showed that the armed forces, once associated with the old system, had accepted the new democratic order.

Q: How did the singing of the two national anthems symbolise reconciliation?

A: The two national anthems represented the different racial groups and political histories of South Africa. First, Black and white South Africans participated in singing the anthems associated with their country's divided past. Second, their participation symbolised a willingness to overcome old prejudices. Third, the event represented the beginning of a united nation in which different communities could coexist peacefully.

Q: Explain Mandela's statement that the greatest wealth of his country was its people.

A: South Africa possessed valuable minerals and precious stones, but Mandela considered its people more valuable. First, the people had demonstrated extraordinary courage and resilience during the struggle against apartheid. Second, their wisdom, generosity, and sacrifices made democracy possible. Third, Mandela believed that human qualities and the ability to create a just society were more important than material wealth.

Q: What does Mandela teach us about the relationship between fear and courage?

A: Mandela explains that courage does not mean being completely free from fear. Even brave people experience fear when facing danger or hardship. True courage lies in overcoming fear and continuing to act according to one's principles. His experiences during the struggle against apartheid taught him that people can achieve extraordinary things despite their fears.

Q: Why did Mandela say that people must learn to hate and can therefore be taught to love?

A: Mandela believed that hatred based on race, religion, or background is acquired through social influences rather than being natural. Since people learn hatred from their surroundings, they can also learn compassion and respect. He argued that love comes more naturally to the human heart than hatred and that societies can overcome prejudice through understanding and education.

Q: Describe the twin obligations mentioned by Mandela.

A: Mandela explains that every person has two major responsibilities. First, responsibility towards family members, including parents, spouses, and children. Second, responsibility towards one's community, people, and country. Third, under apartheid, fulfilling both responsibilities was extremely difficult because those who fought for their people's freedom often faced imprisonment and separation from their families.

Q: How did Mandela's understanding of freedom evolve throughout his life?

A: As a child, Mandela considered himself free because he could play outdoors, swim in streams, and follow his community's customs. As a student, he wanted personal freedoms, such as choosing what to read and where to go. Later, he recognised that racial discrimination denied freedom to all Black South Africans. His personal desire for freedom therefore developed into a commitment to securing freedom for his entire people.

Q: Why did Mandela consider freedom for his people more important than his personal freedom?

A: Mandela gradually realised that his personal freedom was meaningless while his people remained oppressed. He understood that racial discrimination affected the entire Black community, not just him. Consequently, he joined the struggle against apartheid and dedicated his life to securing dignity, equality, and freedom for all South Africans.

Q: How does Mandela explain the relationship between the oppressor and the oppressed?

A: Mandela argues that both the oppressed and the oppressor lose their humanity under an unjust system. First, the oppressed suffer because their freedom and dignity are denied. Second, the oppressor becomes trapped in hatred, prejudice, and narrow-mindedness. Third, Mandela believes that genuine freedom requires ending oppression so that both groups can regain their humanity.

Q: What role did sacrifice play in South Africa's struggle for freedom?

A: Sacrifice was essential to the struggle against apartheid. Thousands of people endured suffering, imprisonment, and persecution to secure equal rights. Mandela recognised that the establishment of democracy had been made possible by the sacrifices of thousands of freedom fighters. He felt that their sacrifices could never be fully repaid and believed that their legacy should guide the new democratic nation.

Q: What does the chapter teach about discrimination and human equality?

A: The chapter teaches that discrimination based on skin colour, religion, or background is unjust. Mandela argues that all people deserve dignity, freedom, and equal opportunities. He rejects the idea that hatred is natural and believes that people can learn to respect one another. The chapter promotes democracy, equality, reconciliation, and respect for human rights.

### Section D: 4-Mark Questions

Q: Mandela's inauguration was a victory for democracy and human dignity. Explain.

A: Mandela's inauguration marked the end of a long period of racial oppression in South Africa. First, it established the country's first Black President, demonstrating that political leadership was no longer restricted by race. Second, the inauguration marked the establishment of a democratic, non-racial government. Third, the participation of international leaders recognised South Africa's achievement and its return to the international community. Finally, the ceremony symbolised hope for a future in which people of all races could enjoy equal rights, freedom, and dignity. Thus, the inauguration represented not merely a change of government but also a historic victory for democracy and human equality.

Q: How did apartheid affect the lives of Black South Africans and the development of South African society?

A: Apartheid imposed racial discrimination and denied Black South Africans equal rights and opportunities. People who challenged the system faced punishment, imprisonment, and separation from their families. The policy created widespread suffering and deep divisions between racial communities. Mandela describes apartheid as a system that inflicted a lasting wound on the country and its people. Its effects would take many years, possibly generations, to overcome. However, the struggle against oppression also produced courageous leaders who fought for justice and equality. The end of apartheid created an opportunity to rebuild South African society on the principles of democracy, reconciliation, and respect for human rights.

Q: Explain how Mandela's personal experiences shaped his understanding of freedom.

A: As a child, Mandela believed that he was free because he could play outdoors, swim in streams, and follow his community's customs. As a student, he began to understand freedom in terms of personal choices, such as staying out at night, reading what he wanted, and going where he pleased. Later, he realised that racial discrimination denied him and other Black South Africans the opportunity to live with dignity and self-respect. This understanding transformed his personal desire for freedom into a commitment to the liberation of his people. He ultimately concluded that freedom could not be complete while others remained oppressed.

Q: The oppressed and the oppressor are both robbed of their humanity. Explain Mandela's viewpoint.

A: Mandela believes that oppression damages both the victim and the person responsible for it. The oppressed suffer because their rights, dignity, and opportunities are denied. They are prevented from living freely and developing their potential. The oppressor, meanwhile, becomes trapped in hatred, prejudice, and the belief that one group is superior to another. According to Mandela, a person who denies freedom to others cannot be genuinely free because that person has surrendered part of their humanity to injustice. Therefore, liberation requires ending oppression and creating a society where all people can live with dignity and equality.

Q: How does Mandela demonstrate that courage is more than the absence of fear?

A: Mandela learned about courage from the men and women who fought against apartheid. He saw people risk their lives, endure torture, and face persecution for their beliefs. These experiences taught him that courageous people are not necessarily free from fear. Instead, they overcome their fear and continue to act for a just cause. Mandela defines courage as the triumph over fear rather than the absence of fear. His understanding shows that courage involves determination, resilience, and a willingness to face difficulties in pursuit of what is right.

Q: Explain the significance of the statement Freedom is indivisible.

A: The statement means that freedom cannot be enjoyed fully by one person while others remain oppressed. Mandela realised that apartheid did not restrict only his personal freedom. It denied basic rights and dignity to everyone who belonged to the oppressed racial group. He therefore joined the African National Congress and committed himself to collective liberation. He also argued that the oppressor was not truly free because oppression imprisoned that person in hatred and prejudice. The statement emphasises that genuine freedom requires equality, justice, and respect for everyone's rights.

Q: How did Mandela's idea of responsibility create a conflict between his family and political life?

A: Mandela believed that every individual had obligations towards both family and society. However, under apartheid, a person who fought for the rights of the oppressed risked imprisonment, persecution, and separation from family. As Mandela became increasingly involved in the struggle for freedom, he found it difficult to fulfil his responsibilities as a son, husband, and father. His commitment to serving his people required significant personal sacrifices. This conflict demonstrates the difficult choices faced by those who struggle against injustice and the high personal cost of political resistance.

Q: How does the chapter present the importance of reconciliation in a divided society?

A: The inauguration symbolised reconciliation because people of different races participated in the creation of a new democratic South Africa. The military's loyalty to the new government demonstrated the acceptance of political change. The singing of two national anthems also represented a willingness to overcome historical divisions. Mandela believed that hatred could be replaced by love and that people could learn to respect one another. His vision was not limited to ending apartheid; it also involved creating a society where all people could live together with dignity. Reconciliation was therefore essential to building a peaceful and democratic nation.

Q: How did Mandela's personal struggle for freedom develop into a struggle for the freedom of all South Africans?

A: Initially, Mandela wanted freedom for himself, including the ability to make personal choices and pursue his ambitions. As he grew older, he realised that apartheid denied freedom to all Black South Africans. He understood that the problem was not limited to his individual circumstances but was part of a wider system of racial oppression. This realisation led him to join the African National Congress and work for collective liberation. His personal desire for freedom became a commitment to securing dignity, equality, and self-respect for his entire people.

Q: Why did Mandela believe that the people who fought against apartheid were South Africa's greatest treasure?

A: Mandela believed that South Africa's most valuable resource was not its minerals or precious stones but its people. The freedom fighters demonstrated courage by resisting oppression despite the risks of imprisonment, torture, and death. They also displayed resilience, wisdom, and generosity throughout their struggle. Their sacrifices made the establishment of democracy possible and inspired future generations. For Mandela, these human qualities were more valuable than material wealth because they helped create a society based on justice and equality.

### Section E: 5-Mark Questions

Q: Describe the events of Nelson Mandela's inauguration and explain their historical significance.

A: Nelson Mandela's inauguration took place on 10 May 1994 at the Union Buildings amphitheatre in Pretoria, South Africa. The occasion marked a turning point in the country's history because Mandela became its first Black President following the end of apartheid. The ceremony was attended by international leaders and dignitaries from many countries, demonstrating worldwide recognition of South Africa's democratic transition. During the ceremony, Mandela took an oath to uphold the Constitution and work for the well-being of the country and its people. Military generals pledged loyalty to the new government, symbolising the transformation of the armed forces. The singing of two national anthems represented reconciliation between the country's divided communities. Mandela viewed the occasion as a victory for justice, peace, human dignity, and political freedom. He also acknowledged the sacrifices of generations of freedom fighters who had made the achievement possible. Thus, the inauguration represented the beginning of a democratic and non-racial South Africa.

Q: Courage is not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it. Explain this statement with reference to Mandela's experiences.

A: Mandela developed his understanding of courage through his experiences in the struggle against apartheid. He witnessed men and women risk their lives, endure torture, and face imprisonment to fight for justice. Their determination showed him that brave people could experience fear but still continue their struggle. Mandela therefore rejected the idea that courageous people never feel afraid. Instead, he believed that courage lies in overcoming fear and acting according to one's principles. His own life reflected this understanding. He accepted personal sacrifices and continued working towards freedom despite the risks and hardships involved. Mandela also admired the resilience of his fellow freedom fighters, whose determination helped sustain the movement against oppression. His statement teaches that courage is demonstrated through action, perseverance, and the willingness to face difficulties for a meaningful cause.

Q: How did Mandela's understanding of freedom change from childhood to adulthood?

A: Mandela's understanding of freedom evolved considerably as he gained experience. As a child, he believed that he was free because he could play in the fields, swim in streams, and follow his community's customs. He did not fully understand the restrictions imposed by the political system. As a student, he wanted personal freedoms, such as choosing what to read, staying out at night, and deciding where to go. Later, as a young man, he realised that apartheid prevented him and other Black South Africans from living with dignity and pursuing their ambitions. He understood that freedom was not merely about individual choices but also about equality and the right of every person to live without racial discrimination. Consequently, he joined the African National Congress and dedicated himself to the freedom of his people. He eventually concluded that no individual could be genuinely free while others remained oppressed.

Q: Explain how apartheid affected Mandela's personal life and political responsibilities.

A: Apartheid created a conflict between Mandela's responsibilities towards his family and his commitment to his people. Mandela believed that every person had obligations towards family members as well as society and the country. However, under apartheid, people who fought for racial equality faced persecution, imprisonment, and separation from their families. As Mandela became increasingly involved in the freedom struggle, he found that he could not fulfil all his family responsibilities. His political activities forced him to live a life of secrecy and separation. He did not initially choose to place his people above his family. Rather, the oppressive political system made it difficult for him to serve both. His experience demonstrates the personal sacrifices required to resist injustice and the devastating impact of racial discrimination on individuals and families.

Q: What message does Mandela convey about love, hatred, and human nature?

A: Mandela argues that hatred based on race, religion, or background is not an inborn human characteristic. People learn to hate through social influences and prejudice. He believes that because hatred is learned, people can also learn to love and respect one another. In his view, love comes more naturally to the human heart than hatred. His experiences in prison reinforced this belief. Even during the harshest periods, he occasionally noticed small acts of kindness from prison guards. These moments restored his hope and reminded him that goodness could survive even in an oppressive environment. Mandela compares human goodness to a flame that may be hidden but cannot be completely extinguished. His message encourages people to reject prejudice, practise compassion, and recognise the humanity of others.

Q: Discuss the role of sacrifice in the struggle against apartheid and the establishment of democracy in South Africa.

A: Sacrifice played a central role in South Africa's struggle against apartheid. For decades, people fought against racial discrimination despite facing imprisonment, persecution, violence, and separation from their families. Mandela recognised that the establishment of democracy had been made possible by the sacrifices of thousands of freedom fighters. He described himself as the product of the efforts of earlier African patriots whose courage and determination had prepared the way for freedom. He also felt sadness that many of those individuals had died before witnessing the achievement for which they had struggled. Their sacrifices helped create a society that recognised the rights and freedoms of people regardless of skin colour. The chapter therefore emphasises that major social and political changes often require sustained effort, courage, and personal sacrifice.

A: Mandela observed that the struggle against apartheid produced individuals of extraordinary courage, wisdom, and generosity. Freedom fighters endured severe suffering, imprisonment, and persecution but continued to resist injustice. Their experiences strengthened their determination and demonstrated remarkable resilience. Mandela believed that extreme oppression sometimes revealed the highest qualities of human character. However, this did not mean that oppression itself was desirable or justified. Rather, he recognised the courage shown by those who resisted it. The chapter celebrates the strength of people who continued fighting for equality despite immense hardships and acknowledges their contribution to the establishment of democracy.

A: The title refers to Mandela's long personal and political struggle to achieve freedom for himself and his people. His journey began with his early understanding of personal freedom but developed into a wider commitment to ending racial discrimination. His belief that people can learn to love as well as hate supported the idea that a peaceful society can be built through understanding and respect.

Q: How does Mandela's life demonstrate the importance of balancing personal ambitions with social responsibility?

A: Mandela's life demonstrates that personal ambitions sometimes need to be considered alongside the needs of society. Initially, he sought personal freedom and opportunities to pursue his ambitions. However, he later realised that racial discrimination denied freedom to his entire community. He therefore committed himself to the struggle against apartheid, despite the personal cost. His experiences show that social responsibility may require difficult decisions and sacrifices. They also demonstrate how individual efforts can contribute to broader social change.

Q: What lessons can modern societies learn from Mandela's struggle against apartheid?

A: Modern societies can learn several lessons from Mandela's struggle. First, discrimination based on race, religion, gender, or background violates human dignity and must be challenged. Second, courage and perseverance are necessary to overcome injustice. Third, freedom must be understood as a universal right rather than a privilege available only to certain groups. Fourth, reconciliation and mutual respect are essential for lasting peace. Finally, Mandela's belief that hatred is learned suggests that education, understanding, and compassion can help people overcome prejudice and build more inclusive societies.

### Section I: Important Exam-Oriented Questions

Q: What was apartheid, and how did its end change South Africa?

A: Apartheid was a system of racial discrimination that denied equal rights to non-white South Africans. Its end allowed the establishment of a democratic, non-racial government under Nelson Mandela. It marked a historic step towards equality, freedom, and human dignity.

Q: Why does Mandela describe his inauguration as a victory for justice and peace?

A: The inauguration marked the end of institutionalised racial oppression and the beginning of democratic rule. It represented the achievement of equal rights and the possibility of peaceful coexistence among South Africans of different races.

Q: Explain the relationship between courage and sacrifice in Mandela's life.

A: Mandela's struggle required him to face persecution, imprisonment, and separation from his family. His courage enabled him to continue fighting for justice despite these hardships. His experiences also taught him to respect the sacrifices made by other freedom fighters.

Q: Why did Mandela believe that hatred could be overcome?

A: Mandela believed that hatred was learned rather than inborn. Since people can learn prejudiced attitudes, they can also learn love, compassion, and respect. This belief gave him hope for reconciliation between previously divided communities.

Q: What is the central message of Nelson Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom?

A: The chapter emphasises the importance of freedom, equality, courage, sacrifice, and human dignity. It teaches that true freedom requires ending oppression for everyone and that building a just society depends on respecting the rights of all people.

Q: Explain the significance of the phrase a rainbow gathering of different colours and nations.

A: The phrase describes the gathering of people from different racial backgrounds and countries at Mandela's inauguration. Like the colours of a rainbow, these diverse groups came together in one place. The expression symbolises unity, diversity, reconciliation, and hope for a democratic South Africa.

Q: Why did Mandela feel that he was the sum of the African patriots who had gone before him?

A: Mandela recognised that his achievement was made possible by generations of freedom fighters who had sacrificed their lives and worked towards equality. He considered himself part of their collective struggle and felt that his success represented their shared efforts.

Q: What does Mandela's reference to the glimmer of humanity reveal about his beliefs?

A: It reveals his belief that kindness and goodness can survive even in cruel and oppressive circumstances. Small acts of compassion by prison guards reassured him and strengthened his hope that human beings could overcome hatred and prejudice.

## Frequently asked questions

Q: What was apartheid in Nelson Mandela Long Walk to Freedom?

A: Apartheid was South Africa's system of racial discrimination that separated people by race and denied equal rights to non-white people until Nelson Mandela's democratic government ended it in 1994.

Q: What is Mandela's view of courage?

A: Mandela teaches that courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. Brave people feel fear too, but they overcome it and keep acting for what is right.

Q: Why is freedom called indivisible by Mandela?

A: Freedom is indivisible because one person's liberty cannot be separated from others. Both the oppressed and the oppressor lose humanity under injustice, so genuine freedom needs equality and dignity for everyone.
