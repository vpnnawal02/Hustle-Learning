## Stanza 1: The Boy Loses His Ball

```stanza
What is the boy now, who has lost his ball
What, what is he to do? I saw it go
Merrily bouncing, down the street, and then
Merrily over — there it is in the water!
```

**Line 1: "What is the boy now, who has lost his ball,"** The poet observes a young boy who has lost his ball. Instead of simply describing the incident, the poet asks what has happened to the boy emotionally. The question suggests that the boy's loss has affected him deeply. The ball might seem insignificant to an adult, but it is valuable to the child because of the memories and happiness associated with it. The word now highlights the change in the boy's emotional state. Before losing the ball, he was happy and carefree. After losing it, he becomes upset and helpless. The poet wants readers to understand that the importance of an object is not always determined by its price. Sometimes, an object becomes precious because of the memories and emotions attached to it.

**Line 2: "What, what is he to do? I saw it go"** The poet repeats the question "What" to emphasise the boy's confusion and helplessness. The repetition also reflects the poet's concern about the boy's emotional condition. The boy does not know what to do after losing something he values. The phrase "I saw it go" suggests that the poet witnessed the ball moving away from the boy. The poet observes the situation but does not immediately intervene. This is significant because the incident provides the boy with an opportunity to experience loss and learn from it.

**Line 3: "Merrily bouncing, down the street, and then"** The ball is described as bouncing happily down the street. The word merrily means cheerfully or happily. It creates a contrast between the ball's playful movement and the sadness that the boy experiences when he loses it. The ball's movement is initially carefree and joyful. However, as it moves farther away, the boy loses control over it. The line reminds readers of the happiness the boy experienced while playing with the ball before the unfortunate incident occurred.

**Line 4: "Merrily over — there it is in the water!"** The ball continues bouncing until it falls into the water. The expression "there it is in the water" suggests that the poet has watched the ball's final movement and now sees where it has ended up. The word merrily is repeated, but the situation is no longer happy for the boy. The ball's playful movement has resulted in its loss. The ball is now beyond the boy's reach, and he cannot easily retrieve it. The moment marks the transition from happiness to sadness. The boy must now confront the reality that something he valued has been lost.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 1:** The poet observes a boy who has lost his ball in the water. The ball had been a source of happiness, but its disappearance leaves the boy confused and upset. Through this ordinary incident, the poet introduces the theme of loss and its emotional impact.

## Stanza 2: The Boy's Grief and Emotional Attachment

```stanza
No use to say 'O there are other balls':
An ultimate shaking grief fixes the boy
As he stands rigid, trembling, staring down
All his young days into the harbour where
His ball went
```

**Line 1: "No use to say 'O there are other balls':"** The poet explains that telling the boy he can buy another ball will not comfort him. An adult might consider the ball replaceable because many similar balls are available. However, the boy's attachment to his particular ball makes it special. The ball may carry memories of his childhood, games and happy experiences. Another ball might look similar, but it cannot replace the memories associated with the lost one. The line highlights the difference between an adult's practical understanding of material possessions and a child's emotional attachment to them. The poet suggests that emotional loss cannot always be resolved by replacing an object with another one.

**Line 2: "An ultimate shaking grief fixes the boy"** The phrase "ultimate shaking grief" describes the boy's intense sadness. The word grief means deep sorrow, particularly following a loss. The word ultimate emphasises the intensity of his emotional experience, while shaking suggests that his sorrow is so powerful that it affects him physically. The word fixes means that the grief holds him in place, leaving him unable to move or react normally. The boy is experiencing a level of sadness that an adult might find disproportionate to the loss of an ordinary ball. However, the poet recognises that the ball has deep personal significance for the child.

**Line 3: "As he stands rigid, trembling, staring down"** The poet describes the boy's physical reaction to his loss. The word rigid means stiff or unable to move. The word trembling means shaking, often because of fear, cold or intense emotion. The boy stands still, shakes slightly and stares down at the water where his ball has disappeared. His physical behaviour reveals the intensity of his grief. He is so upset that he cannot immediately move on from the incident. The description also suggests that the boy is beginning to understand the finality of losing something precious.

**Line 4: "All his young days into the harbour where"** This line suggests that the boy's memories and childhood experiences are connected with the ball. The phrase "all his young days" refers to the happy moments and memories associated with his childhood. As he looks into the water, he is not merely thinking about the physical ball. He is also confronting the loss of the experiences and memories that the ball represents. The harbour becomes a symbolic place where the boy's memories seem to disappear along with his ball. The line highlights the emotional connection between objects and personal experiences.

**Line 5: "His ball went."** The poet completes the thought by reminding the reader that the ball has disappeared into the water. The short line gives emphasis to the finality of the loss. The ball cannot be easily recovered, and the boy must accept that it is gone. The poet uses this incident to introduce the larger idea that people must eventually learn to cope with losses that cannot be reversed.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 2:** The boy experiences deep grief after losing his ball. Telling him that he can buy another ball does not solve the problem because his attachment is emotional rather than merely material. As he stares at the water, he confronts the loss of an object associated with his childhood memories. The poet emphasises that some losses cannot be repaired simply by replacing what has been lost.

## Stanza 3: Learning to Accept Loss

```stanza
I would not intrude on him;
A dime, another ball, is worthless. Now
He senses first responsibility
In a world of possessions. People will take
Balls, balls will be lost always, little boy.
```

**Line 1: "I would not intrude on him;"** The poet decides not to interfere with the boy's emotional experience. The word intrude means to interfere in someone's private space or experience without being invited. The poet understands that the boy needs time to process his sadness independently. Instead of immediately offering comfort or trying to distract him, the poet allows the boy to experience his grief. This suggests that children sometimes need to confront difficult emotions themselves to understand and learn from them. The poet's decision reflects his belief that the boy's experience of loss is an important part of growing up.

**Line 2: "A dime, another ball, is worthless. Now"** A dime is a ten-cent coin used in the United States. The poet suggests that offering the boy money to buy another ball would be useless in this situation. The word worthless does not mean that money or another ball has no value in general. Rather, it means that these things cannot replace the emotional significance of the particular ball the boy has lost. The boy must learn that the value of an object is not always measured by its monetary price. The word Now marks an important moment in the boy's life because he is beginning to understand a difficult truth about loss.

**Line 3: "He senses first responsibility"** The boy is beginning to understand what it means to take responsibility for his possessions and his emotional responses. The expression "first responsibility" suggests an early lesson in maturity. For the first time, the boy is confronting the reality that he cannot always depend on others to restore what he has lost. He must learn to accept the consequences of events and gradually develop the emotional strength to deal with disappointment. This experience is a step towards adulthood because growing up involves learning to handle difficulties independently.

**Line 4: "In a world of possessions. People will take"** The poet explains that the boy is learning about life in a world where people own things but cannot guarantee that they will keep them forever. The word possessions refers to the things people own. Objects can be lost, damaged, stolen or destroyed. Therefore, ownership does not provide complete security. The phrase "People will take" suggests that possessions may be taken away or lost through circumstances beyond one's control. The poet introduces a universal truth: loss is an unavoidable part of human life.

**Line 5: "Balls, balls will be lost always, little boy."** The poet repeats the word "balls" to emphasise that losing things is a recurring part of life. The phrase "will be lost always" suggests that loss is inevitable and cannot be completely avoided. Although the poet directly addresses the boy, the lesson applies to everyone. As people grow older, they experience different kinds of loss, including the loss of possessions, opportunities, relationships and loved ones. The poet encourages the boy to understand that he cannot prevent every loss. Instead, he must learn to accept it and continue living.

**Overall Meaning of Stanza 3:** The poet decides not to interfere with the boy because he believes the experience will teach him an important lesson. Another ball or a small amount of money cannot replace the emotional value of the lost object. The boy is beginning to understand responsibility, the limitations of ownership and the inevitability of loss. This experience marks an important step towards emotional maturity.

## Stanza 4: The Universal Nature of Loss

```stanza
No use to say 'O there are other balls':
An ultimate shaking grief fixes the boy
As he stands rigid, trembling, staring down
All his young days into the harbour where
His ball went.
```

**Note:** These lines were already explained in Stanza 2. The poem's final section continues the lesson about accepting loss and learning to live with it.

The poem's central idea extends beyond the loss of a physical object. The boy's ball represents something valuable that cannot be recovered in its original form. Although another ball could be purchased, the lost ball's personal history and emotional associations cannot be recreated. The poet suggests that people must learn to cope with the emotional consequences of loss rather than assuming that everything can be replaced. The boy's grief is therefore an important stage in his development. It helps him recognise that possessions are temporary and that life involves situations in which recovery is impossible. The poem does not suggest that the boy should stop feeling sad. Instead, it highlights the importance of experiencing grief and gradually learning to accept reality.

**Overall Meaning:** The loss of the ball becomes a lesson about the human experience. The boy must learn that some things cannot be replaced and that accepting loss is an essential part of growing up.
